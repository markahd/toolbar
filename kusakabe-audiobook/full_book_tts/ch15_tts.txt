Chapter Fifteen. Stay Still.

South is downhill.

Meiji-dori runs down towards the station in a long, slow slope, and if you walk it in the middle, on the white lines, you can feel it in the backs of your legs. The road wants you to go faster. He doesn't.

Panda walks ahead. Kusakabe walks behind. The gap between them stays the same, about three metres, as if somebody measured it.

The street is nearly empty now. Shops with their lights still on and nobody in them. A bicycle on its side with the back wheel turning, very slowly, in no wind. A ramen place with the stools all spun round to face the door, the way people leave them when they get up in a hurry. Above the second floor, nothing. The curtain sits on top of everything, and the streetlights shine up into it and don't come back.

The radio on Panda's collar is still turned down. It still talks.

"Say again, the..."

"Gojo-san has been..."

"No, sealed, she said sealed..."

"That's not possible, say again..."

Then a long hiss.

Panda stops walking.

He doesn't turn round. He stands in the middle of the road with his head tilted towards his collar, very still, the way he stood on the stairs.

"Sensei."

"Yeah."

"Did they say sealed?"

Kusakabe thinks about it. He tries to hear it again, the exact shape of it, and finds he can't. It's gone, the way the end of a dream goes when you sit up too fast.

"Don't know," he says.

That one's true. He checks it, the way you'd check a step before you put your weight on it, and it holds. He doesn't know. Nobody on that radio knows. They're all just passing the word round in the dark, and the word gets heavier every time somebody touches it.

Gojo doesn't get sealed, he thinks. Gojo is what you put on top of everything else so it stays shut.

Panda stands there a while longer. Then he starts walking again. The gap goes back to three metres.

Further down the road. There's a pedestrian bridge further down, the kind with blue railings and a sign telling cyclists to dismount. Somebody has hung a costume over the railing, a full-length rubber dragon with the zip open down its back, as if the person inside just stepped out of it and kept going. It moves a little in the air coming up from the street. It looks more alive than anything else on the road.

They walk under it.

"You'd fit in that," Panda says.

"No."

"You would. You'd have to bend at the knees."

"I'm not getting in the dragon."

"I'm just saying. If you needed to blend in."

He's talking, Kusakabe thinks. He's talking because the radio said sealed and he doesn't want to hear it say it again.

The coin is in his pocket. He doesn't check it. He knows if he checks it, it'll weigh something, and he doesn't want to find out how much.

Past the bridge. It starts a little after ten.

He's watching the white lines, and then the white lines are wrong. Not moving. Nothing's moving. But the air on the back of his hands goes thick, all at once, the way air goes thick in a room when someone opens an oven door. Except it isn't hot. Not yet. It's just heavy. The weight of it settles on his shoulders and the top of his head and inside his ears, and his ears pop, like a train going into a tunnel.

And the circle is up.

He didn't put it there. He didn't think the word, or move his feet, or touch the hilt. It's just there, all round him, the reach of one arm and one sword, already drawn and humming. It came up the way your hand comes off a hot pan. Nobody decided anything. The body heard something he didn't, and answered.

Three metres ahead, Panda has stopped.

Kusakabe sees the fur go up along the back of his neck. All of it, at once, from the collar to the round of his head, like a field when the wind goes over it.

"Sensei," Panda says. Very quietly.

"Yeah."

"What is that?"

He doesn't answer. He's trying to find the edge of it. With a curse there's always an edge. You feel it come in at the line, small or large, and you know where it is and roughly what it weighs, and your hands get ready for that weight.

This hasn't got an edge.

It isn't coming in at the line. It's everywhere past the line, all of it, the whole street, the way weather is everywhere. He can't find the outside of it to hold. He reaches for it the way he'd reach for a wall in the dark and his hand just goes on reaching.

Oh, he thinks. Very calmly, from somewhere a long way back in his head. Oh, no.

Still in the road, under the weight. Then it splits.

Not the pressure. His sense of it. The weight separates into two weights, the way a sound you thought was one note turns out to be two when you listen properly.

One of them is hot. He feels that one clearly now. It's off to the south-west, beyond the station, somewhere in the streets that go up the hill. It burns. Not like a fire burns. Like the inside of the ground. It's angry and it's very old and very proud of itself, and it's so much stronger than anything he's ever stood near that his teeth hurt.

The other one he can't feel at all.

That's the strange part. That's the part that's going to stay. The other one has no heat and no shape and no edge. He only knows it's there because of what it does to everything around it. The way you'd know there was something very heavy on a bed by the way the whole mattress bends towards it. The street bends towards it. His circle, his small humming circle, leans.

And under the leaning, very faint, there's something he recognises.

He's spent five months knowing where that thing is in the building. Five months of corridors and timings and long ways round. He knows it the way you know your own front door in the dark. He's never once been this close to it with the lid off.

"Sensei." Panda has turned round. His face can't show it. But his voice has gone up at the end, a little, which it never does. "Sensei, that's Yuji."

Kusakabe looks at him.

"That's Yuji's," Panda says. "I know his. I've stood right next to him. That's..." He stops. He listens, with his whole head, the round ears turned. "No. That's not. It's coming from where he'd be. But it isn't him."

"No."

"It's the other one."

"Yeah."

Panda doesn't say the name. Neither does Kusakabe. It feels like the kind of name you don't say in a street with no sky, in case something hears it said and looks round.

Then the fire. Away to the south-west, the curtain lights up.

He sees it before he hears anything. The black overhead goes orange in one place, low down, beyond the buildings, the way the underside of a cloud goes orange over a city. Then it goes white. Then the sound gets to them, and it isn't one sound. It's a whole street of glass going at once, a long falling ringing that goes on much too long, like someone dropping every plate in a restaurant down a staircase.

The air comes after the sound.

It comes up Meiji-dori from the south in a wall. Hot. Dry. It smells of stone and burnt plastic and something under that, a smell like a struck match held too close to his face. It pushes his coat flat against his legs. The dragon on the bridge behind them fills up all at once and stands out straight from the railing, empty and fat and furious. Then it tears loose and goes tumbling away up the road.

The circle holds. Of course it holds. It isn't made for this. Nothing's crossing the line. Nothing's in his reach. There's nothing for his hands to answer. The circle just sits there round him, perfectly drawn, guarding a space about the size of a bus shelter against something the size of a district.

Look at it, he thinks. Look how neat it is.

Another flare. Closer, or bigger. Hard to tell. The orange goes up the underside of the curtain in a long sheet and for a second he can see the whole bowl of it overhead, lit from below, the inside of a lamp.

Then a sound he doesn't have anything to put next to. Not glass. Not a bang. A sort of tearing, very long and very fine, as if the air itself has been cut down the middle with a blade and the two halves are still deciding whether to fall apart.

He knows what a cut sounds like. He's made a lot of them.

He has never made one that sounded like that.

In the hot wind. "We're going back," he says.

Panda looks at him. "The order was south."

"I know."

"The crossing's that way."

"I know where the crossing is." He hears his own voice. It's level, and it's tired. It's the easy one, the same one that told Panda there were people down there, and he hates it. So he stops using it. He makes himself say the next part in a different one. Flatter. Worse. True.

"I'm scared," he says. "That's the first reason. The second reason is there's nothing down there we can do. Not you. Not me. We go south, we're just two more things in the road. So we go back. We find whoever's still on this street and we take them north with us. That's what we're for."

It's the longest thing he's said all day. Maybe all week. It comes out in pieces, not smooth. It doesn't sound like an order. It sounds like a man reading out his own bill.

Panda looks at him for a long moment.

Then the ground shakes. Not much. A shiver up through the soles of his shoes, as if the whole road had been tapped once from underneath.

"Okay," Panda says. "Yeah. Okay."

He turns round. So does Kusakabe. They go back up the hill.

That's the first honest order I've given tonight, he thinks. And it's a retreat. Well. Start with what you're good at.

Back up the hill. They find people.

Not many. A side street off the main road, narrow, bars and a hair salon and a shop that sells only hats. In the doorway of the hat shop, behind a half-pulled shutter, there are six of them. A man in a security jacket with a torch he isn't using. Two girls in matching sailor costumes, holding hands so hard their knuckles have gone yellow. A woman in a white bed sheet with eyeholes cut in it, a ghost, holding the sheet up off her face with one hand so she can see. An old man in ordinary clothes. And a young auxiliary in a black suit, a boy really, with a radio exactly like Panda's that he keeps turning off and on again.

"They told us to wait," the auxiliary says, when he sees them. "Someone said wait here. I don't know who."

"Well, now someone's saying come with us," Kusakabe says. "North. Now."

"Okay," the auxiliary says. "Okay. Yes."

They come out from behind the shutter. The ghost has to bend down to get under it.

There's another flare, behind them. No glass this time, there's no glass left. Just the light, and the air, and a sound like a long breath being let out by something with lungs the size of a station.

The sailor girls start crying. Not loudly. Just at the same time.

"This way," Panda says, in his low, even voice. "Stay near me. I'm very easy to see."

They go.

Moments later. They get as far as the end of the side street.

It stops.

The fire, the light, the tearing sound. One second it's all there, the whole south-west of the curtain alive with it. The next second it isn't. The orange on the underside of the black goes out, all at once, like a hand closing over a candle. The hot weight he's been feeling, the angry, ancient, proud one, simply isn't there any more. He reaches for where it was and there's only a hole the shape of it, cooling.

The other weight is still there.

It hasn't changed at all. That's the worst of it. It's not tired. It's not hurt. It isn't even pleased. Whatever just happened over there, it happened to the other one. This one just carries on being exactly as heavy as it was before, bending the street towards it, the way it's probably been bending streets towards it for a very long time.

And then it moves.

He feels it start. Not fast. Not slow. Just coming.

It's coming this way.

"Sensei," Panda says.

"I know."

The auxiliary has stopped. The civilians have stopped behind him, bunched up, because he stopped. They're at the mouth of the side street, where it meets Meiji-dori.

There's nowhere to put them. The street behind is a dead end of bars and hats. The main road is wide open both ways. It's too late to run and it was always too late to run. He did the sum and the sum came out to here.

His hand is on the hilt.

He doesn't remember it going there.

At the mouth of the side street. It comes down out of the dark.

Not along the road. Down. Off a roof, or off the air. One second there's nothing in the middle of Meiji-dori but the white lines and a burning bin. The next there's a figure standing on the lines, about thirty metres off, as if it's been there the whole time and he just hadn't thought to look.

A boy.

That's what it is, first. A boy in a school uniform, the dark high-collared kind, the one the first-years wear, mostly torn off him now and black at the edges where it's burnt. Bare arms. Pink hair, pushed back off the forehead, as if someone ran a hand through it hard.

Kusakabe has spent five months not looking at this boy's face.

He looks now. He can't not. And it isn't the face.

There are marks on it. Black lines, clean and dark as if they've been painted on with a brush, across the cheeks and up over the brow. And under the eyes, under the ordinary eyes, a second pair. They're open. They're looking. Four eyes in a boy's face, red, all of them, and every one of them bored.

Behind him, one of the sailor girls makes a small sound.

The four eyes come round.

They don't hurry. They take the street in, the whole of it, the way you'd look round a room you'd stopped renting a long time ago. The bins. The bridge. The burning thing in the road. The crowd at the end of the side street.

"Don't move," it says.

The voice is the boy's voice. That's what's wrong with it. It's a fifteen-year-old's voice, a bit hoarse, the voice that laughs in the courtyard. Somebody else is using it. Somebody who's said this sentence before, to a great many people, and is tired of it.

"I'm not done," it says. "I don't want to be bothered. Stay where you are."

And that's all. It doesn't explain. It doesn't threaten. It doesn't need to. It says it the way you'd tell a cat to get off a table.

Under those eyes. Kusakabe stays where he is.

He stays where he is the way a wall stays where it is. He doesn't decide to. There isn't anything left in him to decide with. Every part of him that usually has a vote has gone very quiet, and the part that's left is very simple, and only knows one thing.

Don't breathe too loud, he thinks.

He breathes through his nose. Slow. Small. Not to the bottom. Just enough.

Don't be interesting.

His face does nothing. It's good at that. Thirty-something years of practice. He lets it go slack and plain and tired, the face of a man waiting for a train that's late. He doesn't look at the four eyes. He doesn't look away from them, either. Looking away is a movement. He looks at a point just below the boy's chin, at the burnt edge of the collar, and keeps his eyes there.

Be furniture.

The circle is still up. He can feel it all round him, drawn and ready and humming, the way it's been since the air went thick. It's the only thing about him that's moving. And it suddenly seems very loud. Like someone in a library whistling.

He thinks about letting it go.

He doesn't dare. Letting it go is a movement too. So it stays, and he stands in the middle of it, and hopes it's too small to notice. Hopes it's like a candle in a window across a valley. There, and lit, and nothing to do with anyone.

Next to him, Panda is still.

Really still. Not the way Panda's still on the grass, loose and heavy and half asleep. This is something else. Every bit of him set, from the round ears down. Kusakabe can feel him in the circle, the edge of one shoulder just inside the line. Panda isn't breathing at all. Kusakabe doesn't know if Panda needs to. It's one more thing on the list of things he's never asked.

Panda's paw, at his side, is closed. Tight. The way a hand closes when it's stopping itself from reaching for something.

He wants to say the name, Kusakabe thinks. He wants to say Yuji. And see if anyone's in there.

Don't. Please. Don't be the one who's interesting.

Panda doesn't.

Still standing there. The four eyes go along the crowd.

The auxiliary. The man with the torch. The ghost, still holding up her sheet with one hand, frozen like that, her elbow in the air. The old man. The sailor girls, who have stopped crying, because crying is a movement.

Panda. The eyes stay on Panda for a moment. Not long. A flick of something, the way a cat's ear moves towards a sound and then decides against it. Then they move on.

Then they come to him.

He feels it arrive. It doesn't come in at the line. It doesn't come in anywhere. It just lands on him, the whole weight of being looked at by that, and his circle doesn't so much as twitch, because there's nothing in it to answer. Nothing has crossed. Nothing needs to. The circle is built to catch things that come in. It has nothing at all to say about something that simply looks.

There have been men like him for a thousand years.

He knows that, suddenly, standing there. Men with a sword and a bit of training and good feet. Men who practised a small trick until it was very good. Men with a sister somewhere, and a debt, and a sofa. There have been thousands of them. This thing has met them all. It has killed some of them, probably, on days when it had nothing better to do. It doesn't remember a single face.

It isn't going to remember his.

The eyes go through him. Not past him. Not over him. Through, as if he were a pane of glass, and on the other side of the glass there was something faintly more worth looking at. A wall. A drainpipe. The hat shop.

It doesn't see a Grade one. It doesn't see a sword, or a circle, or a man. It sees a street with some things in it, and he's one of the things.

Good, he thinks. Good. That's right. That's all I am.

Keep thinking that. Mean it.

The four eyes move on.

They go up, to the black where the sky should be. To the south-west, where the fire was. They narrow. Not at anything here. At something further off, something it's thinking about, and Kusakabe, very far back inside himself, understands that he has already been forgotten.

The boy's mouth does something. Not quite a smile. The shape a mouth makes when it's been told a joke it already knew.

Then it's gone.

He doesn't see it go. Nobody does. There's no jump, no step, no flare. The space in the middle of the road where it was standing is just a space. The white lines run through it, and a scrap of burnt cloth turns over once on the tarmac and lies still.

The weight goes with it. Not all at once. It drains off south and west, bending other streets now, someone else's air, someone else's problem. He feels it go the way you feel a lorry go past on a bridge you're standing on. The whole thing shaking. Then less. Then a hum. Then just the bridge.

Afterwards. Nobody moves.

That's the thing he'll remember. Long after it's gone, nobody moves. Not the auxiliary. Not the ghost with her elbow up. Not the old man. They all stand exactly where they were, in the shapes they were in, as if they've been told not to and nobody's come back to say they can stop.

Nobody's going to come back and say they can stop.

The ghost is the first. Her arm comes down. Slowly, as if it's very heavy. The sheet falls over her face again and she doesn't lift it. She just stands there, a white shape, breathing under it. Kusakabe can see the sheet go in and out where her mouth is.

Then one of the sailor girls sits down on the kerb. Not falls. Sits. Very carefully, as if the kerb might not be there. Her friend sits down next to her. They're still holding hands.

Panda breathes out. Or does something that sounds like breathing out. His paw opens, at his side. The fur on his neck is still up.

"Sensei."

Kusakabe doesn't answer.

"That was Yuji's face," Panda says.

"No," Kusakabe says. "It wasn't."

It's the first thing he's said since it spoke, and his voice comes out wrong, too dry, like paper. Panda looks at him. Then Panda looks back at the empty road.

"He's not in there right now," Panda says. "Is he."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything to that. There isn't anything. Five months of corridors. Five months of the long way round so he'd never have to look at the boy's face. And tonight he finally looked. And the boy wasn't home.

So you still don't know what he looks like, he thinks. Well done. You managed it after all.

Some time later. They move, eventually.

The auxiliary's radio decides to work. A voice he doesn't know says there's a van at the top of Meiji-dori, north, for civilians. Panda takes them. The ghost, the sailor girls, the man with the torch, the old man. Panda walks in front, slowly, his round back very easy to see.

"Coming?" Panda says, over his shoulder.

"In a minute."

Panda looks at him. Then he nods, and goes on up the hill with his small crowd behind him.

Kusakabe stands at the mouth of the side street.

The circle is still up. He finds that out the way you find out you've been holding your breath. It's still drawn round him, perfectly round, perfectly neat, humming. Guarding nothing. He lets it go. It goes slowly, like something that isn't sure it's allowed.

The road is very quiet. The bin has burnt down to a red glow. The white lines run off south into the dark, towards the station, towards the crossing where they were supposed to regroup, where he'd have been standing if he'd followed the order.

He isn't ashamed of standing still. He checks, the way he checked the other things tonight, and it holds. That one's just true. There was nothing to do but stand still, so he stood still. A man who moved would be lying in the road now. He stood there like a coat rack and he's alive and so are six other people and a panda. He'll take that. He'd take it again.

It's only that his hand won't come off the sword.

He notices it the way you notice a sound that's been going on for a long time. His right hand is on the hilt. It's been there since the side street, since before the thing came down out of the dark. The fingers are wrapped round the grip so hard the knuckles have gone white, four small white hills in a row. His left hand, the bandaged one, is holding the scabbard up by the mouth, and the black cloth Panda tied round it is dark again in one place where the cut's opened.

He never drew. Not an inch. The blade is still in the scabbard. All night, the one thing that's been asked of him, and he did it perfectly, and it was to not do anything at all.

He tells the hand to let go.

It doesn't.

He tells it again. It stays. It isn't frightened, exactly. It's just not finished. It heard the order, don't move, and it believed it more than the rest of him did, and nobody's come back to tell it it can stop.

Up the road, a long way off now, Panda's back goes round the corner under a streetlight, broad and slow, and the little crowd goes after it, and the street is empty.

Kusakabe stands in it.

Hand on the sword. Knuckles white. Long after there's nothing left in the road to be still for.
