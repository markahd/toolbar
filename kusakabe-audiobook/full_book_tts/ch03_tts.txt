Chapter three. The Hurricane with Sunglasses.

The corridor outside the old archive room is the quietest place in the school after four o'clock.

Kusakabe has tested it. Nobody comes down here except to look for old files, and nobody looks for old files. There's a bench under the window, and a radiator that works on one side. If you sit at the warm end with your back to the wall and a folder open on your knees, you look like a man reviewing something important.

He has been reviewing the same page for twenty minutes. It's a list of approved suppliers of paper towels.

Critical work, he thinks. The country depends on it.

He feels it before he hears it. Not through the circle. The way you feel the air change before a storm. A sort of brightness at the far end of the corridor, coming closer. Cheerful. Enormous.

Then footsteps. Long ones, unhurried, with a little bounce in them.

He doesn't look up.

If I don't look up, he thinks, it might be someone else.

"Ku. Sa. Ka. Be."

It isn't someone else.

Satoru Gojo stops in front of the bench. Kusakabe would have to tip his head right back to see his face, so he looks at the knees instead. A paper bag from the bakery by the station. The edge of the blindfold at the top of his vision. Gojo wears it at school the way other people wear a lanyard, and it has never once stopped him seeing exactly what you'd rather he didn't.

There's powdered sugar on the bag. There's powdered sugar on Gojo.

"Found you," Gojo says.

"I wasn't lost."

"No, you were hiding. Different thing." He sits down on the bench, which is not long enough for two grown men when one of them is Gojo, and reads the folder upside down, blindfold and all. "Paper towels. Wow. Gripping stuff."

"It's for a report."

"It's from three years ago. The supplier went bust." He holds out the bag. "Want one?"

"No."

"They're really good."

"No, thanks."

"Your loss." He eats half of one in a single bite, and talks through it. "So. I need a favour."

Here it comes, he thinks.

Kusakabe has seen this happen to other people. They walk into a conversation with Gojo holding a plan for their evening, and they walk out holding Gojo's.

"There's a hospital in the east of the city," Gojo says. "Children's ward. Something's been growing in it. Started with bad dreams, kids waking up crying at the same time every night. Now it's fevers nobody can explain, and a night nurse who says the corridor lights keep going out." He licks sugar off his thumb. "The Window called it Grade two last week. Grade one this week. It's eating well."

"Eating what."

"Fear. Obviously. Kids in hospital at night? They're terrified. It's a buffet." He says it lightly. He says everything lightly. "Anyway, it was mine. And I can't. I'm in Kyoto tonight. Meeting. Old men. You know." He pulls a face. "So I thought of you."

"Why."

"Because you're good. And because you're here."

Kusakabe looks at the paper towels.

There are three ways out of this. He has been getting out of things for most of his adult life.

"I'm busy," he says.

"You're not. You're reading about paper towels in a corridor nobody uses. That's the least busy a person can be without lying down."

Fair. He tries the second way.

"There are other Grade ones on the board. Send one of them."

"There are two this week. One's in Hokkaido with a broken collarbone. The other one I don't like."

"That's not a reason."

"It's a great reason. Also, he'd go in loud. You need somebody quiet on a children's ward. Somebody boring." Gojo points at him with the other half of the pastry. "You are the most boring man I know. That's a compliment. I don't give many."

The third way is the honest one, which is why he keeps it for last.

"I don't want to."

Gojo tilts his head. For a moment he's genuinely still, which with Gojo is like a fountain stopping.

"Oh, I know," he says. "Nobody wants to. Ijichi's picking you up at ten, by the way. I already told him it was you. The file's on your desk."

"You went to my desk?"

"I went to everybody's desk. Yours was the tidiest. Very suspicious." He stands, brushing sugar onto the floor, and onto Kusakabe. "The night nurse is called Mori. She's apparently a nightmare. You'll love her."

And somewhere in there, after the third way and before any fourth, Kusakabe hears his own voice say:

"What time does it start?"

He doesn't know where it came from. He was there the whole time.

"Around three, the nurse says." Gojo is already walking away, the bag swinging. Without turning round, he lifts a hand. "You're the best, Kusakabe!"

Then he's round the corner, and the brightness goes with him, and the corridor is the quietest place in the school again.

Kusakabe sits.

There's powdered sugar on the bench where Gojo was. A little on his own sleeve. He doesn't brush it off. It feels like evidence.

Three ways to say no, he thinks. Used all three. Nobody told me you needed a fourth.

Some of the staff call Gojo a hurricane, when he isn't in earshot. Not because of the damage. Because there's no point arguing with one. You go indoors, and you wait, and afterwards you go outside and find out where your things ended up.

His things, it turns out, have ended up in a children's ward, at three in the morning.

That night.

Ijichi is in the car yard at ten exactly, with the engine running and the heater on.

"Kusakabe-san. Thank you for doing this." He hesitates, his hand on the gearstick. "Gojo-san said you volunteered."

"Did he."

Ijichi looks at him in the mirror. Then at the road, and keeps looking at it.

"I'm sorry," he says.

"Not your fault."

"No. It's quite often not my fault." Ijichi pulls out of the gate. "I find I'm still quite often sorry."

Kusakabe reads the file under the little light in the back. Eleven children, ages two to nine. Four with fevers of no known cause, all in the rooms at the far end, near the playroom. Corridor bulbs replaced twice.

At the bottom, in someone's careful handwriting, copied from the nurse's own notes: They wake up and say something was under the bed. All of them say the same thing. Same time. They don't know each other.

He reads that line twice and closes the file.

Kids, he thinks. Of course it's kids.

He'd rather it were an empty warehouse. Not because children are worse to save. Because if something goes wrong with an empty warehouse, it's just a warehouse.

At the hospital.

The hospital is big and grey and mostly asleep. A man from the night office meets them there and holds out a lanyard at arm's length, as if it might bite. It says Contractor on it. Kusakabe puts it on.

The lift smells of floor polish. The fifth floor smells of everything else.

Disinfectant first, sharp, the kind that gets into the back of your throat. Then warm plastic. Then something sweet: fruit juice from the little cartons with the bent straws. And underneath all of it, the smell hospitals have at night, which isn't really a smell. It's the sound of people trying not to make any noise.

The lights are dimmed to half. The walls have animals painted on them, a little faded. A row of ducks. A giraffe whose neck goes round the corner, so you can only see its body from the lift and you have to walk to the end to find its face.

The doors to the rooms are open a hand's width. Through each gap, a bed.

The beds are small.

He knew they would be. Knowing doesn't help. The beds are small and the blankets are small and in one of them, a hand is lying outside the blanket, and it's about the size of the bottom of a teacup.

He keeps walking.

The nurses' station is a curved desk under the only full-strength light on the ward. Behind it sits a woman in her forties in blue scrubs with a grey cardigan over them, her hair in a clip that's slowly losing the argument. A mug of tea at her elbow with a skin on it. A chart in front of her. The name badge says Mori.

She doesn't look up.

"You're the one admin called."

"Yes."

"I don't know what you are. I don't want to know. I've got eleven children, me, and one agency nurse who's on her break." She turns a page. "Don't wake them. Don't go in the rooms unless you have to. If you have to, knock first. Quietly. They're not animals in a zoo."

"Fine."

"Don't touch the drip stands. Don't touch the trolleys."

"Okay."

Then she does look up. At his face, briefly, and then at the long bag on his shoulder, a second longer. Something goes across her face and she decides not to have it.

She looks back at the chart.

"Room four's night light is broken," she says. "If she wakes up and cries, that's why. It's not you. Facilities said Monday." She writes something. "It's been Monday for two weeks."

He waits to see if there's more. There isn't.

He doesn't take it personally. Eleven children, a cold cup of tea, and now a man with a long bag, the latest in a long line of things that turn up on her shift without asking. He'd be curt too. He usually is.

"The lights," he says. "Around three?"

The pen stops.

"Who told you that?"

"It's in the file."

"Nobody reads what I write."

"Someone did."

She looks at him again. Not warmer. Just a little more carefully, the way you'd look at a step you'd been told was loose.

"Around three," she says. "Ten to, sometimes. Never here. Only down there."

She points with the pen. Down the corridor, past the giraffe's body, to where the light is thinnest.

"Thanks."

She's already writing again.

Later, on the ward.

He walks the ward once with the circle open.

Not fully. Just let out a little, the way you'd crack a window. The reach of an arm and a sword, close and quiet.

What he feels is residue. A faint chill at the threshold of each door, like the draught that comes off a fridge. It's thickest at the far end. Room four. Room five. The playroom, with its door shut and a paper sign on it with a smiling sun.

Room four's door has a name card in a little plastic sleeve. Yui. There's a strawberry sticker on it, slightly peeling.

He doesn't go in. Through the gap he can see the end of a bed, a small hump under a blanket, and a plug-in night light in the wall socket by the floor. A plastic rabbit, sitting up. It isn't lit. Room four is darker than every other room on the ward.

The hump under the blanket breathes fast and shallow. Not awake. Somewhere near it.

There's a plastic chair by the drinking fountain, the kind made for a parent to sleep badly in. He sits on it with the bag across his knees.

And he waits.

The ward at night has its own noises. A pump beeping until somebody's shoes squeak over and stop it. A child talking in their sleep, one clear word, no, and then nothing. The agency nurse back from her break, who sees him and stops dead, and then very carefully doesn't look at him for the rest of the night.

One forty. Two. Two thirty.

He thinks about nothing. He's good at it. It's the main thing the job has taught him. Not the sword. The waiting.

At two fifty-two the hair on his arms stands up.

Then it starts.

The light at the far end of the corridor goes out.

Not a flicker. Just gone, like a mouth closing. Then the next panel. Then the next. The dark comes up the corridor towards him one ceiling panel at a time, over the giraffe, over the ducks.

Behind him, at the nurses' station, the light stays on.

Kusakabe stands. He doesn't remember deciding to. The bag is open. He doesn't remember that either.

The circle draws itself closed around him, tight and certain. Everything inside it goes very clear and very slow.

Under the playroom door, something pours out.

It's ugly. It's made of what children see at three in the morning. The shape of a coat on a hook. The gap under a bed. A face in the pattern of a curtain. All of it stitched together badly, too many elbows, legs that bend the wrong way at the wrong time. Where its middle should be there's a mouth, and in the mouth, very faintly, a monitor beeping out of time.

It doesn't come for him.

It goes for room four. The dark one. Where it's easiest.

It's fast. Faster than anything that shape should be. It moves along the floor like spilled ink finding the low side of a table.

It crosses the line of the circle.

His body is already there. That's all he knows about it. One moment the corridor, the next his shoulder against the doorframe of room four and the blade coming round, low, and the thing meeting it.

It makes a sound only he can hear, thin and high, like a kettle left on in another flat.

The second cut is the one that counts. He feels it go through. Something in the middle of it gives, the way a knot gives when you find the right loop.

Then it comes apart. Not dramatically. It just stops having a shape. A grey mist with a smell like rubber gloves, and then not even that.

Behind him, the corridor lights come back on, one panel at a time, from the near end.

He stands in the doorway of room four with a drawn sword, under a painted giraffe, at two fifty-four in the morning.

His heart takes longer to finish than the curse did.

If anyone comes round that corner right now, he thinks, there's no story in the world.

Nobody comes round the corner. He wipes the blade, sheathes it, zips the bag, and stands there with his hand on the zip until it stops shaking. It never takes long. It's still the longest part.

At the far end of the corridor, Mori has stood up behind the station. She's looking at the lights. Then at him. A man in a doorway with a bag.

She looks at him for a while. Then she sits back down.

Afterwards.

In room four the breathing has changed.

Slower. Deeper. Like someone putting down something heavy.

He should go. That's the job. In, done, out. Nobody wakes. Nobody knows.

He looks at the rabbit.

He knocks on the door frame with one knuckle, very quietly, because she told him to. Then he goes in.

The night light is cheap. The plastic body is cracked down one side, and someone's strip of sticking plaster has come away. He eases it out of the socket. One of the metal prongs is loose in its housing and bent a little sideways, so it never quite meets the contact.

Facilities, he thinks. Monday.

There's a roll of white surgical tape on the trolley outside. Mori told him not to touch the trolleys. He touches the trolley.

He bends the prong straight with the tip of his door key. He presses the cracked body together and winds the tape round it, twice, tight. He tears the tape with his teeth, which he's fairly sure is against some rule. He pushes the rabbit back into the wall.

It lights.

Not much. A soft yellow, low down, the colour of the inside of a lemon. Enough to see the leg of the bed. A pair of small slippers with faces on them, side by side. The edge of the blanket.

The hump under the blanket turns over and sighs and doesn't wake.

He stands there for a moment, which he shouldn't.

Then he goes out, and puts the tape back on the trolley exactly where it was, label facing the same way.

At the desk, on his way out.

"Done?" Mori says, when he passes the desk.

"Done."

"Will it come back?"

"Someone'll check. In a few days."

She nods. She doesn't say thank you. He doesn't wait for it. She has a chart to finish and three hours of shift left, and none of it is about him.

"Right," she says.

He's at the lift when he hears her shoes. He turns. She's walked halfway down the corridor, to check on room four the way she must check on it every hour. She stops at the door.

She stands there a second. Looking in.

Then she goes on to room five, and he gets in the lift.

In the car.

He writes the report in the car, on his phone, before they've left the car park.

Grade one curse, fifth floor, paediatric ward. Exorcised at two fifty-four. No civilian injuries. Residual check recommended within one week.

That's it. He reads it once. He sends it.

Ijichi glances at the mirror. "That's very fast, Kusakabe-san."

"Yeah."

"It's usually." Ijichi stops himself, kindly.

"Late," Kusakabe says. "I know."

He doesn't say why. He isn't sure why. Something about the hand outside the blanket, the size of the bottom of a teacup.

He puts his head back against the seat and closes his eyes and doesn't sleep.

Back at school.

Gojo comes back from Kyoto the next afternoon.

Kusakabe can hear him from the other side of the building.

He's in the staff room at his desk, the one with the dying plant. Ijichi is at the next one, eating rice crackers over mileage forms. Two auxiliaries are at the kettle. The dispatch coordinator is at the big table with her laptop, a woman in her thirties with a headset round her neck that nobody has ever seen her take off. By the photocopier, Okabe is watching each page come out as if it might try something.

The door opens and the weather comes in.

Gojo isn't wearing the blindfold. He's off the clock, or wants everyone to think so. Little round dark sunglasses, perched on his nose. A big box under one arm, wrapped in green paper with gold writing. Kyoto sweets.

"Souvenirs!" he announces, to the room. "Don't fight."

He puts the box down in front of the dispatch coordinator, who moves her laptop without looking up. Then he turns, and the sunglasses find Kusakabe, and Kusakabe knows, with complete calm, that something bad is about to happen to him.

"There he is!"

"Here I am," Kusakabe says. To his screen.

"Everybody. Everybody, listen." Gojo comes across the room and puts a hand on Kusakabe's shoulder. It's like having a bird land on you, a very large and very pleased bird. "Last night. Children's ward. Grade one, getting bigger every night, sitting in a room full of sleeping kids. This man walks in. In and out. Not one kid woke up." He leans down to the room in general. "Do you know how hard that is? I wake children up just by walking past their house."

One of the auxiliaries laughs. Ijichi has stopped eating his rice cracker.

"It was one curse," Kusakabe says.

"See? Modest. I love it." Gojo straightens up and lifts his voice, so it carries all the way to the photocopier. "Best Grade one without a technique in the country. I'm serious. Somebody write that down."

By the photocopier, Okabe takes out a pen.

Kusakabe isn't going to look to see if he uses it.

There's a word in that sentence, he thinks, doing a lot of work. And the word isn't best.

That isn't the part that sits on him. He's heard the other part his whole working life, from people who meant it a lot less kindly.

The part that sits on him is the rest of it.

Because the room is looking at him now. Not long. Nobody looks at Kusakabe for long. But the dispatch coordinator lifts her eyes from her laptop, with the face of somebody moving a name a little further up a list.

And it's out now. Said out loud, by the strongest sorcerer alive, in a room with a dispatch coordinator in it. It'll be in people's heads the next time something ugly turns up in a building and they need a name.

Until today nobody at this school expected anything from him except that his reports would be late.

That was a manageable expectation. He was meeting it.

Praise from Gojo, he thinks, is a loan. Nobody tells you the interest rate.

Gojo has already moved on. He's tearing the gold paper off the box himself, offering the first sweet to Ijichi, who takes it with both hands like a certificate.

On his way out, he comes past Kusakabe's desk again and puts a wrapped sweet down next to the dying plant.

"You were going to say yes anyway," he says. Not loudly, this time.

"I wasn't."

"Sure." The sunglasses slide down his nose. Above them, for half a second, the eyes, very blue and very amused, looking all the way through him and out the other side. He pushes the glasses back up with one finger. "Eat that. It's the good kind."

Then he's gone, and the room goes back to its normal size.

Kusakabe looks at the sweet. Pale green, soft, folded into a triangle, in a little cellophane wrapper.

He doesn't eat it. He puts it in his drawer.

The dispatch board.

On Monday, the dispatch sheet has his name on it four times.

Usually it's once. Sometimes twice, if somebody's off sick.

He stands in front of the board and reads it. A Grade one in a closed-down school in Chiba, Wednesday. A multistorey car park near the docks that has already sent one Grade two home with a broken wrist, Thursday. A shrine in the hills outside the city, Saturday, with a note next to it in the coordinator's small square handwriting: Requested. Per G.

And the fourth one, which was already his. Residual check, paediatric ward. Thursday night.

Okabe comes past with his clipboard, looks at the board, then at Kusakabe.

"Reports by Friday," Okabe says. "All of them."

"Mm."

Okabe goes. Kusakabe stays a little longer, looking at the line that says Per G.

Best Grade one without a technique, he thinks. Also, it turns out, the most available.

He takes a photo of the board so he won't have to come back and look at it. Then he goes to find somewhere quiet. The bench outside the archive room has a box of old files on it now, and somebody's coat.

The hospital, again.

Thursday night, he goes back.

It's late. The car park by the docks took longer than it should have. He has a graze on the back of one hand from a concrete pillar that came closer than it looked, and a plaster on it from Ijichi's glove box, which came with an apology.

The fifth floor smells exactly the same. Disinfectant. Plastic. Juice cartons. The giraffe still hides its face round the corner.

Mori is at the station. Same cardigan. Different mug, same skin on the tea.

"You again," she says.

"Me again."

"Lights have been fine."

"Good."

She goes back to her chart. Whatever she saw the other night, a man standing in a doorway with a bag, she's decided not to carry it around with her. He respects that. It's what he'd do.

He walks the ward with the circle open.

Nothing. No chill at the doors. Just a ward at night, ordinary and tired. The playroom door is propped open now, and inside, in the half-light, there are small chairs round a table and a drawing taped to the wall of a very large green dog.

It's clean. It's done.

He comes back past room four.

The name card has gone from the door. Just the empty plastic sleeve, and a little tacky square where the strawberry sticker used to be.

He looks in.

The bed is stripped. The mattress is bare in its blue plastic cover. The slippers with faces on them are gone. The room is waiting for the next person, clean and patient and not anyone's.

Went home, he thinks. He doesn't ask. He doesn't need to.

Down by the floor, in the socket by the bed, the rabbit is still glowing.

Nobody has thought to unplug it. White tape round its middle, sitting up, giving off its small yellow light to a room with no one in it.

He doesn't unplug it either.

At the station, Mori doesn't look up when he says he's finished. She lifts a hand off the desk an inch, which might be goodbye, or might mean go away. Either way, he goes.

He walks back towards the lift. The lights are at half. Every door is open a hand's width, and behind each one somebody small is sleeping through the night.

At the lift he presses the button, and waits, and looks back.

The corridor is empty. Just the long pale floor, and the painted ducks, and halfway down, from the open door of room four, a little patch of yellow light lying on the tiles, the size of a folded handkerchief.

It doesn't need anybody to see it. It's just on.

The lift doors open behind him. He stays where he is a moment longer, looking at it.

Then he gets in, and the doors close, and the rabbit goes on glowing in the empty corridor without him.
