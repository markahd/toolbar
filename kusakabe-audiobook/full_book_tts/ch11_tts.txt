Chapter Eleven. The Offer.

The September meeting takes eleven minutes.

It's in a room on the second floor at headquarters that has a number on the door and nothing else. A table. Four chairs. A window onto the back of another building, where someone has left a single work glove on a ledge. Okabe sits on one side with his clipboard. Next to him is a woman from Personnel with a laptop and reading glasses on a chain. Kusakabe sits on the other side. Nobody has put his chair in the middle of the room this time. He notices that, and is annoyed at himself for being grateful.

"Formal reprimand," the woman reads. "Twelve months from June. Status: active."

"Yes."

"Complaint from the Office of Field Conduct. May. Status: on file."

"Yes."

"Grade allowance review, originally scheduled for August." She scrolls. "Deferred to August next year. Subject to conduct."

"Yes."

She looks up over the glasses. She seems to have expected him to say something else. People often do.

"Is there anything you'd like to add to the record?"

"No."

She types something. It's short. He wonders what it is. Something like: subject declined to add anything. Subject is very good at it.

Okabe turns a page on his clipboard and turns it back, which is what Okabe does instead of talking. When the woman closes the laptop, Okabe says, "Thank you, Kusakabe-san," and it comes out almost kind, and then he seems to hear it and straightens his cardigan, which is green today.

That's all. Eleven minutes, and nothing in them he didn't already know.

He knew it in June. He did the arithmetic in Yaga's office with the Zen'in letter on the desk. But a thing said in June is a thing that might still change. A thing typed into a laptop in September has stopped moving. It's set, like concrete.

A year, he thinks. Fine. It's only money.

He walks down the stairs. He tells himself that again on every landing. It doesn't get truer, but it gets quicker to say.

Outside.

There's a man waiting on the front steps.

Kusakabe sees him from inside the glass doors. Fifties, silver hair cut very short. A navy suit that fits him the way suits fit people who have them made. A furled umbrella with a wooden handle hooked over one arm, though the sky is clear and has been for a week. He isn't looking at his phone. He isn't looking at anything. He's just standing, the way people who used to do this job stand. Weight even. Hands free.

He's waiting for someone. Kusakabe has a small cold feeling that it's him.

"Kusakabe-san," the man says, as he comes out.

San. Not kun. Not just Kusakabe. It's a small thing. He hates that he notices it.

"Yes."

"Kurosawa." A card, held out with both hands. Thick paper, cream, a name in plain black type and under it Hoshino Security. No logo. No crest. Money that doesn't need to tell you it's money. "I wonder if I could buy you a coffee. Ten minutes. I'll keep to it."

"Who told you I'd be here."

Kurosawa smiles, a little ruefully. "Someone who talks more than they should. It's a small world, ours." He corrects himself. "Yours. I'm out of it."

Kusakabe looks at the card. Then at the street. There's no reason to go. There's no reason not to. He has nothing until four.

Ten minutes, he thinks. I'll keep to it. People only say that when they know they won't need to.

A few minutes later.

The coffee shop is round the corner. Chalkboard menu, wooden counter, a young man with a beard who weighs the beans. Kurosawa orders without looking at the board. Kusakabe says, "Whatever's quick."

It comes in a white cup with a saucer. He drinks some. It tastes of something. Not of the can, not of the jar. Of whatever coffee is supposed to taste of, presumably, when nobody is cutting corners. He doesn't know what to do with it. He puts it down.

"I was a Grade two," Kurosawa says. "Fourteen years. Kyoto, then Tokyo. Never very good. Good enough." He stirs his cup, though he hasn't put anything in it. "Then one day I stopped. Nobody minded. That's the thing they don't tell you. Nobody minds."

Kusakabe says nothing. Kurosawa doesn't seem to need him to.

"I run security for the Takamine family now. You may know the name."

He does, vaguely. Shipping, a long time ago. Property now. A name on the side of buildings near the bay.

"Old money brings old grudges," Kurosawa says. "And old grudges bring what you'd expect. The family has a house in Hayama, by the sea. Another in the city. The grandmother is sensitive. Not a sorcerer. Just sensitive. She sees things at the edges, and she doesn't like it, and she can afford not to like it." He sets the spoon down. "We keep a resident sorcerer. The current one is retiring. His knees."

"Mm."

"We'd like it to be you."

There it is. Laid on the table between the cups, quite gently.

Kusakabe looks at it.

Why me, he wants to ask, and doesn't, because he can guess. A Grade one with no clan to object. A Grade one with a record getting crowded. A Grade one whose allowance has just been put in a drawer for a year. Somebody at headquarters has been talking, and Kurosawa has been listening, and here they are.

"Someone pencilled a question on your file last year," Kurosawa says, as if he'd heard. "About a tunnel in Gunma. I've read the incident. I don't think you froze."

It lands somewhere Kusakabe didn't know was open. He keeps his face where it is.

Kurosawa takes a folded sheet from his inside pocket and puts it beside Kusakabe's saucer. He doesn't push it across. He just leaves it there, within reach.

Kusakabe unfolds it.

A salary. It's a little under twice what he makes now. A flat in Hayama, staff accommodation, rent covered. A car. Medical cover, comprehensive, for the employee and any dependants. Four days on, three off, on a rota.

"Incidents," Kusakabe says.

"Four in six years. Nothing above Grade three. You'd be bored."

"I'm good at bored."

"I know," Kurosawa says. "That's partly why I'm here."

He lists the rest the way a doctor gives good news. No students. No higher-ups. No headquarters. No panels. "You'd answer to me. I answer to Mrs. Takamine. Mrs. Takamine answers to nobody, which is restful, once you get used to it."

Kusakabe reads the sheet again. The number doesn't change. He half expected it to.

He thinks of Ono's invoice, which comes on the fifth. He thinks of the thickening powder, which costs more than it has any right to. He thinks of the reed diffuser by his sister's shoe cabinet, which he replaces every two months, and which suddenly seems like a very small thing to have been worried about.

Any dependants.

"We understand you may have family commitments," Kurosawa says. Lightly. Not prying. Just leaving a door open.

"No," Kusakabe says.

It comes out flat and immediate, the way it's supposed to. Some things you don't put on a table in a coffee shop. Not even a good one.

Kurosawa nods, as if that were a perfectly ordinary answer, which perhaps it is.

And then, for a moment, it's very easy.

He can see it. A flat by the sea. A car he wouldn't drive. A grandmother who sees things at the edges, and a man with a sword who stands near her so she doesn't have to. Nobody waiting for his attendance. Nobody's file to sign. Ono paid. The powder paid. Everything paid, and something left over, and no meeting next September.

His mouth opens.

"When would I," he begins.

He stops.

He doesn't know why. Nothing happens. Nobody comes in. The young man with the beard is still weighing beans. His hand has gone to his coat pocket on its own and is resting there on nothing, the way it does.

"End of the month," Kurosawa says, quite as if the sentence had been finished. "There's no hurry. Take a few weeks. Think about it properly."

"I'll think about it."

"That's all I ask." Kurosawa stands, and leaves money on the counter that more than covers it, and picks up his umbrella. "I'll send you my number. My own, not the office."

At the door he turns back, as if he's just remembered. "One thing. The family entertains at weekends. Sundays can't always be guaranteed."

Kusakabe hears it. He puts it somewhere, and doesn't look at it.

Kurosawa keeps to the ten minutes. Kusakabe checks. Nine and a half.

His phone buzzes before he's reached the corner. A message. Kurosawa. Hoshino Security. Whenever you're ready.

He saves it. He tells himself that's just manners.

That night he makes a list.

He doesn't make lists. He's not a list man. But he finds the Midori Home Care envelope from last month on the kitchen counter, empty, and turns it over, and there's a pen in the drawer that works on the third try.

He draws a line down the middle.

On the left he writes the number. Then he crosses it out and writes it again, more carefully. The first time he wrote it the way he wanted it to be and the second time he wrote it the way it was. It's the same number. He just wanted to be sure.

Under it: Flat. Rent free. Under that: Car. Under that: Medical. Dependants.

Under that, after a while: No Okabe.

He looks at that one for some time. It seems petty. He leaves it.

No meetings. No panels. No Froze.

On the right he writes nothing at all for a long time.

The fridge hums. Somebody in the next building is watching a quiz show with the window open. He can hear the buzzer, and a studio audience making the sound audiences make when someone gets something wrong.

On the left, at the bottom, he writes: No students.

He looks at it. It's true. It goes in the left column. That's where it obviously goes.

He crosses it out. He doesn't put it on the right, either. It doesn't seem to belong in either.

At one in the morning the envelope has six things on the left and none on the right, and one crossed out in the middle. He goes to bed with the feeling of a man who's worked very hard on something and got it wrong in a way he can't find.

Ono rings on Tuesday at half past twelve.

He answers on the first ring. He has done that since December. Nobody has mentioned it, including him.

"Ono."

"Yes."

"Doctor came this morning. About the legs." Ono never starts with hello. She starts with the thing. "He says physio would help. Keep the joints moving. Stop it getting worse."

"Okay."

"Twice a week, he says. The agency has a woman. She's good. I've seen her work." A pause, which for Ono is a long speech. "It's not on the plan. It'd be extra."

"How much."

She tells him. Per session. He multiplies it by two, and then by four, and then by twelve, standing in the corridor outside the staff room with one hand on the wall. Somewhere behind him, a printer starts up and then thinks better of it.

"Can I let you know."

"Yes." Then, after a moment: "She'd like it, I think. She liked the last one. In hospital. Before me."

He didn't know there'd been a last one. He didn't know she'd liked anything, in hospital.

"Okay," he says. "Thanks."

Ono hangs up. She always hangs up first. It saves time.

That night he takes the envelope out again and writes on the left: Physio. Twice.

Then he sits with the pen and the number and does it all over again, properly this time. Tax. Insurance. The rent he wouldn't be paying here. The rent he'd still be paying at his sister's. Ono's hours. The night carer. The physio woman, twice a week, every week, for as long as there are weeks.

It comes out good. It comes out good with tax, and good without the car, and good even when he pretends the flat isn't free. He tries it three ways. It's good every time.

That's the problem with a good number, he thinks. You can't argue with it. You just have to look at it.

Maki hits him on Wednesday.

That isn't news. She hits him most days. But on Wednesday she hits him on the second exchange, high on the shoulder, before he's even properly set. He feels it go all the way down his arm and out of his fingertips.

He steps back. Resets.

She doesn't come in again. She stands there with the practice sword low, looking at him.

"You're somewhere else," she says.

"I'm here."

"You're not." She tilts her head. Her glasses catch the light. "Where are you?"

By the sea, apparently, he thinks. With a grandmother.

"Thinking," he says.

"About what."

He considers several answers and gives her none of them. He lifts his sword.

She looks at him a moment longer, the way she looks at a fault in a weapon she can't find yet. Then she comes in, and this time he's there for it, and the next ten minutes go the way they're supposed to. But she was right, and they both know she was right, and when he goes back to the third step his shoulder has already started to stiffen.

On the grass, Panda rolls his head towards him. "Sensei's got a face on."

"I've always got a face on."

"A different one."

Inumaki, on the bottom step, looks up at Kusakabe and then away. "Tuna mayo," he says, and tilts his hand. It could go either way.

Even the boy who doesn't talk can tell, he thinks.

He doesn't tell them. It's not theirs. It isn't even, yet, a thing that has happened. It's a sheet of paper in his inside pocket that he keeps meaning to take out and leave at home and doesn't.

That week, at night.

The nights go like this.

Thursday: the envelope, the pen. He adds the cost of moving. He adds a deposit he wouldn't need. He takes it off again. Two in the morning.

Friday: he doesn't take the envelope out. He lies in bed and does it in his head instead, which is worse, because in his head there's no line down the middle. The numbers just go round.

Saturday: he looks up Hayama on his phone. Photographs of a small harbour. White boats. A long beach with the sea coming in very flat and very blue. A mountain in the background that might be Fuji on a clear day, and is, in the photographs, always a clear day. He looks for a long time. He doesn't know what he's looking for. Then he looks up the route from Hayama to his sister's flat.

Bus to Zushi. Train. Change. Train. Change. An hour and fifty-two minutes, if everything runs.

From here it's twenty. Six stops.

He puts that on the right side of the envelope. Twenty minutes. It's the first thing on the right.

He looks at the two columns. Six things, seven now, on the left. One on the right. It ought to be easy. The left is heavier. Anyone could see that.

He sits there and finds that it isn't easy at all.

On Friday afternoon Yaga stops him in the corridor by the stairs.

"Kusakabe."

"Mm."

"Second-years' eligibility forms."

"Friday."

"It is Friday."

"Tonight, then."

Yaga nods once. That's the whole conversation. He goes on down the corridor, big and slow, in his dark glasses, with a cardboard tube of something under his arm. He doesn't look back. He never does.

Kusakabe stands at the top of the stairs and watches him go.

His hand goes to his pocket. The thing that isn't there is there.

If I go, Kusakabe thinks, Yaga won't say a word.

He knows that the way he knows the weight of his own sword. Yaga will sign whatever needs signing. He'll find someone else to turn up for the second-years. He won't mention his sister, or Takeru, or a kitchen, years ago, or anything else. He's never mentioned it. He's not going to start now.

That's worse, Kusakabe thinks. If he'd ask for it, I could say no.

The debt doesn't come with terms. There's nothing written down. There's no number. He could put it on the envelope, and it wouldn't fit in either column, and he wouldn't know what to write. He's tried. On Thursday night he wrote a Y on the right side, small, and then scribbled it out until the pen went through the paper. Some things don't go on paper. Not even on the back of an envelope that's going in the bin.

But he knows this much. You don't owe a man that much and then go and live by the sea.

Not because Yaga would mind. Because he'd be somewhere else. And the debt would be here. And that's not how it works. You stay where the thing is owed. You stay where it can be called in, even if nobody ever calls it.

He doesn't think it in those words. He doesn't think it in words at all. He just stands at the top of the stairs with his hand in his pocket. Then a first-year comes up past him and says excuse me, and then he goes and does the eligibility forms.

Sunday. Ono hands over at the door.

"Physio," she says. "You decided?"

"Not yet."

She nods and writes something in the rabbit notebook. He'd like to know what. He doesn't ask.

His sister is in the window chair. The lavender is where it always is. The car park is full of Sunday cars. He makes the tea, thickened, too weak, and holds the cup while she drinks. She makes a face at the powder as she does every week, and he agrees with her as he does every week.

It's a good day. She knows him from the door.

He sits with her through the afternoon. She talks about the dog upstairs, which has got worse. He listens. He tells her the dog is probably lonely. She says, "Everyone's lonely. They don't all bark about it."

Then she looks at him properly. Her eyes are very clear today.

"You're doing sums," she says.

He blinks. "How can you tell."

"Your mouth goes small." She purses hers, to show him. It's not flattering. "You've done it all afternoon."

He doesn't say anything. He has nothing ready for this.

She turns back to the window. Outside, a man is trying to get a pushchair into the boot of a car and losing.

"We should go to the sea," she says, out of nowhere.

Something in his chest goes very still.

"Too much sand," he says.

"There's always too much sand. That's how you know it's the sea." She watches the man and the pushchair. "Not now. When it's warm. You could push me along the front."

"Maybe."

She nods, satisfied with maybe, and in ten minutes she's forgotten it. He can see it go. One moment it's there in her face, the sea, the front, the warm. The next her face is just looking at the car park.

He hasn't.

He sits there with the cup going cold in his hands. An hour and fifty-two minutes, if everything runs. Four days on, three off, on a rota. And Kurosawa at the door of the coffee shop, umbrella on his arm, almost as an afterthought: Sundays can't always be guaranteed.

He looks at it now.

Some Sundays, she'd be in this chair and he wouldn't be in that one. Ono would tell her work. She'd forget it. Next week she'd forget it again. And somebody would pay for all of it, lavishly, very well, and the chair would be empty anyway.

He could bring her to the sea. In theory. With the money. With a car he doesn't drive and a carer who'd come, and a flat with a ramp, and a window chair moved to another window.

Ono's rule. Don't move her from the window. Ono said it like a statement about herself. I don't do that.

He doesn't either.

On the train home he takes out the envelope and writes on the right, under Twenty minutes: Sundays.

That's two.

He calls Kurosawa on the last Thursday of the month, at lunchtime, from the bench outside the old archive room, where nobody comes.

He's been ready to call for a week and a half. Every day he's been ready. He's not sure what took so long. Probably nothing. Probably just him.

"Kusakabe-san." Kurosawa sounds pleased to hear from him. He sounds like a man who is pleased to hear from most people, and means it, and is good at his job.

"It's no," Kusakabe says.

A pause. Not a long one.

"I'm sorry to hear that. May I ask why?"

"No."

Kurosawa laughs, a small real laugh. "Fair enough. That was always going to be the answer to that one." Then, more quietly: "The position's open until the end of the month. After that, I'll have to look elsewhere. If anything changes."

"It won't."

"Then I wish you well. Truly." A beat. "Look after your knees."

He hangs up. Kusakabe sits there with the phone in his hand.

He waits to feel something. Relief, maybe. Or the other thing. He gets neither. He gets a bench, and a radiator that's warm at one end, and a window with a crack in the corner he's never noticed.

After a while he makes the other call.

Mr. Hasegawa at Midori answers on the fourth ring, sounding like a man with a mask under his chin. Kusakabe asks for the physio. Once a week. Thursdays. Starting next week.

"The doctor's note says twice," Hasegawa says. Not unkindly. Just reading.

"Once," Kusakabe says. "For now."

"For now," Hasegawa agrees, and writes it down. Kusakabe can hear the pen.

That's what the left column was. Not a flat by the sea. Not a car. One afternoon a week of a woman moving his sister's legs, so that they don't get worse. He could have bought the second one. He could have bought both and a good deal more. He's just decided not to.

He goes through his own outgoings that evening looking for something to cut, so it could be twice. There isn't much of him to cut. A phone plan. Lunch. He cuts the phone plan to the cheapest one. It isn't enough. It's a start.

Once a week, he thinks. She'll have to make do with half as much not getting worse.

At eleven o'clock that night he's lying in bed in the dark, and it opens again.

He doesn't open it. It just opens, the way a cupboard door does in an old flat, on its own, with a small click.

The position's open until the end of the month.

It's the twenty-seventh.

He lies there and goes through it all again. The number. The flat. The physio twice. No Okabe. No September. The coffee in the white cup that tasted of something.

Then the other side. Twenty minutes. Sundays. A Y, scribbled out until the pen went through.

Two things, he thinks. Against six. Seven.

But they're heavier.

He isn't sure that's true. He's sure, and then he isn't. He can feel himself starting to do the sum again, his mouth going small in the dark, and it occurs to him that he could do this every night for the rest of his life. He could lie here and add it up and take it away and add it again, until the number was a ghost he kept in the flat, like the stain. Until it had a corner of its own.

He reaches for his phone on the floor by the bed. The screen lights his hand, white.

Contacts. K.

Kurosawa. Hoshino Security.

His thumb hangs over it. Call. Message. Edit. It would be so easy. A message. Sorry for the late hour. I've reconsidered. Kurosawa would be gracious. Kurosawa would be gracious at midnight. That's what you'd be paying for.

He holds his thumb there for a long time.

Decided, he thinks. Done. No more.

He doesn't feel decided. He does it anyway.

Edit. Delete contact. Are you sure?

He isn't. He presses it.

The name goes. The list closes up over the gap as if nothing had ever been there. Kawamura, Kimura, the dispatch line. Nobody misses anyone, in a contacts list.

He puts the phone face up on the floor. After a few seconds the screen goes dark on its own.

It's still a lot of money. It's still physio once a week instead of twice. It'll still be a lot of money tomorrow, and in August, and next September in a room with a number on the door. Nothing about that has changed. He's just stopped adding it up.

Through the gap in the curtain, the streetlight lays a thin pale line across the ceiling. He follows it into the corner, above the wardrobe.

The stain is there. The same shape. Exactly where he left it.

He lies on his back and looks at it for a long time.
