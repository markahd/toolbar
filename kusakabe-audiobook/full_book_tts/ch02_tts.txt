Chapter two. Sunday.

On Sundays, he visits his sister.

The train is almost empty at nine. A grandmother with a shopping trolley. Two boys in baseball uniforms, asleep against each other. A man in a suit who has clearly not been home since Friday. Kusakabe sits by the door with a carrier bag between his feet and watches the stations go past.

Twenty minutes. Six stops. He doesn't need to count them anymore. His body gets up at the right one the way it gets up in the mornings.

In the bag: rice crackers, the soft kind. A packet of the tea she likes. Hand cream, unscented, because the scented one made her sneeze for a week. Two persimmons from a stall near the school that he walked past twice before going back for.

Grade One sorcerer, he thinks. Fruit shopping.

He doesn't mind. That's the strange part. Six days a week he minds almost everything. Sunday morning, on this train, with this bag, he doesn't mind anything at all.

It's the one appointment he never misses. Never complains about. Never mentions at school. Ijichi doesn't know. Okabe doesn't know. Gojo doesn't know, which is a small miracle, because Gojo knows everything that's none of his business.

It isn't a secret, exactly. It's just his.

His sister's building.

Her building is four storeys of tired cream tile on a street with a laundrette and a dentist. Ground floor, because of the chair. He chose it for the ramp and the bus stop. He didn't choose it for the view. The view is a car park and, past the car park, a strip of other people's balconies with futons hung out to air.

She likes it anyway. She likes watching the futons.

He rings the bell, then lets himself in with his own key, which is the arrangement. Ring so she's not startled. Key so she doesn't have to wait.

The smell reaches him before anything else. Lavender. The cheap kind, from a little bottle with a wooden stick in it that stands on the shoe cabinet by the door. He buys a new one every two months. It's the first thing in the flat and the last thing when he leaves. Some weeks it's the only thing he remembers clearly about the visit.

"Kusakabe-san." Mrs. Fujita comes out of the kitchen drying her hands on a towel tucked into her apron. "You're early."

"Train."

She doesn't ask what that means. She's in her fifties, short, square, with reading glasses pushed up into grey hair and the forearms of someone who lifts people for a living. She has been coming four mornings a week for almost three years, plus Sunday mornings until he arrives. She talks fast, works faster, and has never once asked him what he does for a job. He's grateful for that in a way he would never be able to say out loud.

"She's in the window," Mrs. Fujita says. "She's had her bath. She ate half. Better than Thursday."

He nods, takes his shoes off, and goes through.

In the front room.

His sister sits by the window in her chair.

Not the wheelchair. The other one, the high-backed armchair with the wooden arms. He carried it up from a recycling shop and had it re-covered in a blue she picked out three years ago and has since forgotten picking. The wheelchair waits folded by the wall. Mrs. Fujita moves her between the two with a sort of practised hug that Kusakabe has learned to do too, badly.

The light from the window is pale and flat. October getting tired. It falls across the blanket on her knees and her hands on top of it, and the side of her face.

She's thinner. She's always a little thinner. He tells himself he imagines it.

"Hey," he says.

She turns her head. It takes a moment. Her eyes go over him the way they go over the car park, the futons, the sky, taking it in without hurry, deciding where it goes.

He waits. He's good at waiting. He's had practice.

"Oh," she says. "You're back."

Then she says their father's name.

Not a question. A greeting. Warm, a little reproachful, the way you'd speak to someone who has been away too long on business and brought nothing home.

He answers to it.

"Yeah," he says. "I'm back."

He sits on the stool beside the chair. She looks at him for a while longer, satisfied, and then back out of the window.

Some days she knows me straight away, he thinks. Some days it takes a minute. Some days I'm a dead man.

He doesn't correct her. He tried that, once, early on. She got frightened, and then upset, and then she cried for an hour because she couldn't work out why she was upset. He decided afterwards that it costs him nothing to be someone else for an afternoon. Lots of people would like that deal.

"The futons are out," she says.

"Good drying weather."

"Mm. That one's crooked."

He looks. One of the futons on the third balcony across is hanging at a slant, half a metre lower on one side.

"Yeah," he says. "It is."

She seems pleased to have been agreed with. He would agree with her about anything. That's another part of the arrangement.

The kitchen.

In the kitchen, Mrs. Fujita runs through it.

She does this every Sunday. A notebook on the counter, the kind with a cartoon cat on the cover that she bought at a hundred-yen shop. She writes everything in it, in neat pencil, in a hand that slopes uphill. Meals. Medicine. Bowels. Mood. Anything odd. He reads it every week, standing at the counter, and she watches him read it to make sure he does.

"Tuesday she didn't want the lunch. Wednesday fine. Thursday she was confused in the evening, she thought it was morning, she wanted to go to the station. I talked her out of it." Mrs. Fujita taps the page. "Friday the bruise."

"Bruise."

"Her left wrist. From the transfer. It's nothing, it's yellow already, but I wrote it down." She looks at him over the glasses. "I write everything down."

"I know."

"In case."

"I know."

She nods, as if that settles something between them. It probably does. He's never asked in case of what. He suspects the answer is in case you ever decide I'm the one who did it, and he suspects some family, somewhere, once did, and that's why the notebook exists.

The rest is routine. The pill organiser for the week, seven little plastic doors with the days printed on them, filled and checked. The physio exercises she did on Wednesday, the ones she refused on Friday. A note that the toilet handle sticks. A note that the lady upstairs has a new dog and it barks at four, and his sister doesn't like it. Mrs. Fujita says all of it in the same quick flat voice, like reading out a train timetable, and he listens to all of it.

This is the part he's good at. Tasks. Lists. Small things that can be fixed by a man with a screwdriver and a bank account.

There's a pile of envelopes by the kettle. He takes them without being told. Electricity. Gas. Water. The building's management fee. The pharmacy. The agency's invoice, which comes from the agency, not from Mrs. Fujita, because she doesn't like handling money and neither does he.

He folds them all in half and puts them in his inside pocket.

He pays them on the Monday. He always pays them on the Monday. He does it at the convenience store near the school, at seven in the morning, before anyone he knows is awake, and then he throws the receipts away. Not hiding it. There's nobody to hide it from. He just doesn't see the point of anyone else knowing a number that isn't theirs.

Grade One pay, he thinks. Grade One bills.

It works out. More or less. Most months.

In the hallway, near noon.

Mrs. Fujita is putting her coat on when she says it.

She does it the way people do it when they've practised. Coat first. Bag on the shoulder. Hand on the door. Then, as if she's only just remembered:

"Kusakabe-san. One thing."

He waits.

"The agency's changing the rates. From next month." She isn't looking at him. She's looking at the lavender bottle on the shoe cabinet. "It's not me. Well. It's partly me. I asked. They wouldn't do it unless they did it for everyone, so they're doing it for everyone. It's another fifteen percent. For the weekday hours and the Sunday. I'm sorry. I know it's a lot. I wanted to tell you myself before the letter."

There's more coming. He can see it lined up behind her teeth. The cost of the train. Her mother. The other client in Nerima who cancelled. Her knees. All the reasons, in order, ready to be read out like the notebook.

He doesn't want to hear any of it. Not because he doesn't care. Because if she explains, he'll have to respond to the explanation, and then they'll both be standing in a narrow hallway explaining things to each other, and he'd rather be under a curse.

"Fine," he says.

She stops.

"Fine?"

"Yeah."

"You don't want to... the agency can send you the breakdown, if you want to look at..."

"It's fine."

She looks at him properly then. Her face does something complicated. Relief, mostly. Something under the relief that might be suspicion, or might be pity. He can't tell. He doesn't try.

"All right," she says. "Thank you. Really. Thank you."

"Mm."

She opens the door. Then she leans back in, and calls past him into the flat, much louder and slower, in the voice she uses for his sister. "I'm off now! See you Tuesday!"

From the window, no answer. Then, after a while, a small hand lifted off the blanket.

The door closes.

He stands in the hallway for a moment, in the lavender, doing the sum he should have done before he said the word.

He gets as far as fifteen percent of and stops.

Next month, he thinks. Next month's problem.

He goes to make the tea.

The kitchen again.

The tea she likes is cheap green tea in a bag, and she likes it weak. Not a little weak. Badly weak. The bag goes in, the water goes in, and the bag comes out again almost immediately, so the water is the colour of a pale green wall in a hospital.

He learned this the hard way. The first time he made it properly, she took one sip and put the cup down and looked at him with an expression of deep, patient disappointment, like he'd done it on purpose.

Now he dips the bag. Counts to three. Takes it out.

He makes his own in the same pot, afterwards, with the same bag. It tastes of almost nothing. He drinks it anyway.

Bad coffee six days a week, he thinks. Bad tea on the seventh. Very balanced life.

He carries both cups through and puts hers on the little table by the chair, turned so the handle faces her right hand. She can lift with the right. Not far. Not always. She has a lid she can use, a plastic one with a spout, like a child's cup. Mrs. Fujita uses it. He doesn't, on Sundays, unless she asks. He holds the cup for her instead, and she drinks, and some of it goes down her chin, and he wipes her chin with the cloth, and neither of them says anything about it.

"Too strong," she says.

It isn't. He's made it exactly the same way for two years.

"Sorry."

"It's all right. You never could make tea."

He doesn't know if she means him, or their father. He doesn't ask. Both are probably true.

Afternoon.

The afternoon goes the way Sunday afternoons go.

He does the blinds in the bedroom, which have a slat that keeps slipping. He fixes the toilet handle, which takes two minutes and a coin. He trims her fingernails, carefully, one hand on the blanket, her fingers held still in his, the little clipper going tick, tick in the quiet. Her nails are soft. They tear if you're not careful. He's careful.

She talks while he does it. Not to him exactly. To the room.

She talks about a summer at the sea. A guest house with a red roof and a woman who gave them watermelon. A beach where the sand burned your feet so you had to run. She tells it the way she always tells it, the same three pieces in the same order. When she reaches the end she starts again from the beginning, and he listens to it again from the beginning.

He doesn't go with her into it. He keeps himself here. On the stool. In the flat. With the clippers in his hand and her fingers in his palm. He can listen to the sea without going to the sea.

Some parts she can still reach, he thinks. I'm not taking any of them away from her by being bored.

At two he does the medicine. Seven little doors. Sunday's door. Three white, one yellow, one that's half a pill because the doctor cut the dose in the summer. He gives them to her one at a time with water, and she takes them one at a time, and frowns at the yellow one the way she always frowns at the yellow one.

"That one tastes," she says.

"I know."

"Of metal."

"I know. Last one."

She swallows it. She looks pleased with herself. He tells her she did well, which feels ridiculous to say to a grown woman, and is also the right thing to say.

By three she's tired. Her head goes down a little at a time, like a flower closing, and then she's asleep in the chair with her mouth slightly open and her hand still on the blanket. He tucks the blanket higher. He takes her cup into the kitchen. He sits back down on the stool.

And because she's asleep, and the flat is quiet, and there's nothing left on the list, the other thing comes in.

On the stool, while she sleeps.

It always comes on Sundays. He's stopped being surprised by it.

Years ago, her son died.

Takeru. Small, sudden. An accident. The kind of thing you can turn over a thousand times and it never makes any more sense than it did the first time. One afternoon there was a boy and then there wasn't. That's all it was. That's all it ever is, for the people it happens to.

She broke.

Not loudly. People think breaking is loud. Hers was quiet and permanent, like a building settling on a bad foundation, one crack and then another and then a door that won't shut anymore. Memory gone in patches. Then a body that stopped doing what she asked. Doctors said things with long words in them. Kusakabe nodded at the long words and went home and looked them up and understood them perfectly and it made no difference at all.

Somebody had to take it on. There wasn't anybody else. So he did.

Nobody asked him. Nobody would have blamed him if he'd found her a place somewhere with a long corridor and visited at New Year. People did that. Reasonable people. He'd have understood it if someone else had done it.

He just didn't.

He found the flat. He signed the lease. He arranged the carers, interviewed four of them in a café with his hands around a coffee he didn't drink. He picked the one who asked the fewest questions and wrote the most things down. He came on Sundays. He sat with her. He made tea wrong, and then right.

It's not kindness, he tells himself, now, on the stool, while she sleeps. It's just what you do. She's my sister.

He doesn't push it further than that. Pushing it further would mean feeling it, all of it, at once, and he isn't built to hold that much. He knows his own limits the way he knows the reach of his own arm. So he does tasks instead. Blinds. Nails. Medicine. Toilet handle. The bills folded in his pocket. The tasks get the same thing done as the feelings would. They just don't wreck him on the way.

That's the first thing.

The second thing nobody knows.

Still on the stool.

At the worst of it, she stopped eating.

She stopped talking, except sometimes to say her son's name, and then she'd look at Kusakabe like he'd know where the boy had gone, like he was hiding it from her. He sat with her through that. He doesn't let himself think about how long. He went to work during the week and came back and sat with her and went to work, and somewhere in there he started to understand that one day she would just stop. Not die, exactly. Stop.

Then Masamichi Yaga came to see him.

The principal. The big man with the sunglasses and the beard and the little felt dolls. The man who made Panda. A craftsman of something the higher-ups would very much like to pretend doesn't exist, the kind of knowledge that gets you executed if the wrong person decides it should.

Yaga sat on a chair too small for him in Kusakabe's kitchen and listened. He didn't say much. He looked at the photographs. He asked to see her. He came here, to this flat, and stood in the doorway for a long time looking at a woman in a chair by a window.

Then he offered.

Takeru's soul, given form again. An independent cursed corpse. Not the boy back. Yaga was very clear about that. Kusakabe remembers him being clear about it, almost stern. Not the boy back, exactly. But the boy, in a way. Something that would know her. Something she could hold.

Kusakabe said yes.

He didn't think about it. He didn't weigh it. There wasn't anything to weigh. Of course he said yes.

And there was a day, after, when a small boy climbed up into his mother's lap in this chair, by this window. He settled there with his head under her chin, as if he'd only been out. And she put her arms around him. The arm that worked and the arm that didn't. She held him the way she couldn't hold a cup.

Kusakabe stood in the kitchen doorway. He had to go and stand in the bathroom for a while afterwards. He ran the tap so nobody would hear anything.

That's all he lets himself remember of it. That much, and no more. It's enough.

The coin.

It's a debt.

He knows that. Yaga has never once mentioned it, never asked for anything, never so much as looked at him in a corridor in a way that said you owe me. That almost makes it worse. A debt somebody calls in, you can pay. A debt nobody mentions just sits there.

It's too big to pay back. He worked that out a long time ago. There isn't a currency for it. So he doesn't try. He just carries it.

He thinks of it as a coin.

Small. Heavy for its size. Sitting in his pocket, always, and every so often his hand goes to it without being told, just to check it's there. In a meeting. On a train. Standing in a corridor while Yaga walks past with a box of dolls under one arm, nodding to him the way he nods to everyone.

Kusakabe nods back. Checks the coin.

Still there.

It's also a secret. Nobody at school knows. Nobody can. What Yaga did for her is exactly the kind of thing that could get them both killed. It's exactly the kind of thing that the people who'd do the killing are always looking for. So Kusakabe keeps it inside the circle with everything else. The flat. The tea. The bills. Her.

His circle isn't large. Arm and sword. That's how he likes it. Anything inside it, he can feel. Anything inside it, his hands answer for before his head has to decide.

She stirs in the chair. Not awake. Just shifting, her fingers moving on the blanket like she's looking for something.

He puts his hand where she can find it. She finds it.

He sits like that until the light goes orange.

Half past four.

She wakes at half past four, all at once, the way she does.

She looks at him. Her eyes are clear. The difference is so sharp it's like a window being cleaned.

"Atsuya," she says.

Just his name. His actual name.

"Hey."

"You came."

"It's Sunday."

"Is it." She thinks about this. "You look tired."

"I'm fine."

"You always say that." She looks at him a moment longer, the way she used to look at him across a table when they were both adults with jobs and he'd said something stupid. Then she looks back out of the window. "Someone should take that futon in. It'll get damp."

He looks. The crooked futon is still out. The sun is off the balconies now, and the air coming in round the window frame has a cold edge to it.

"Yeah," he says. "Someone should."

They sit a little while longer. He doesn't try to keep her. He's learned that too. If you try to hold onto the clear part, it goes faster. You just let it be there, and then you let it go.

By five she's somewhere else again. Not unhappy. Just elsewhere. She tells him the woman with the watermelon had a dog. That's new. He files it.

He makes her dinner, the soft rice and the fish Mrs. Fujita left in the fridge with a note on the lid in uphill pencil. He sits with her while she eats half. He writes in the cat notebook, in his own handwriting, which is worse than Mrs. Fujita's: Sunday. Ate half. Medicine at two, fine. Toilet handle fixed. Knew me at half past four.

He looks at the last line for a while. Then he leaves it.

At seven the night carer from the agency comes, a young man who says almost nothing and that suits them both. Kusakabe hands over. He puts his coat on. He says goodbye to his sister, who says goodbye to him without looking away from the dark window. There is nothing in it now to see but the two of them reflected and the lamp.

"Bye," she says. Then, after a moment, their father's name again, softly, like an afterthought.

"Bye," he says.

The lavender, on the way out. First thing in, last thing out.

The train home.

The train back is fuller. Sunday evening. People coming home from things. A family with a sleeping toddler and too many bags. Students with their earphones in. A couple not talking to each other in the specific way of a couple who've just had an argument and are now going to be polite about it for the rest of the evening.

Nobody looks at him. That's the nice thing about trains.

He gets a seat. He sits with his hands in his coat pockets. The bills are in the inside one, folded in half, waiting for Monday. In the other pocket, nothing. In the other pocket, the coin.

He checks it.

Still there.

Fifteen percent, he thinks, from nowhere. Fifteen percent of what, though.

He could work it out. He could take his phone out right now and open the calculator and do it. He knows roughly what the agency costs, and roughly what he earns, and roughly what's left at the end of most months, which is not much. He could know, in about thirty seconds, exactly how bad it is.

He doesn't take his phone out.

He'll find out next month. When the letter comes. When the money goes out and the number at the bottom is smaller than it should be. He'll stand at the convenience store at seven in the morning and look at it and think ah. That's when he'll know. Not now. Now is Sunday.

I owe him everything, he thinks, watching the stations go past in the other direction. And I don't even like him that much.

It's true. Yaga is stubborn and proud and has never once in his life made a joke Kusakabe found funny. He runs the school like a man holding a door shut in a high wind. He makes stuffed animals in meetings. He looks at Kusakabe sometimes like he's waiting for him to be more than he is.

None of that matters. That's how debts work. You don't have to like the person. You just have to remember.

The train goes up onto the elevated section. Outside, the city opens out: lit windows stacked on lit windows, red lights blinking on the tops of buildings, a highway like a river of headlights going somewhere he isn't.

The carriage lights are bright, and the night outside is dark, and so the window turns into a mirror.

He's in it. Sitting there, collar up, hands in pockets. His own face laid over the city like a sheet of glass. The lights go straight through him. Office towers through his forehead. A neon sign through his cheek.

He looks tired. Not Sunday tired. The other kind. The kind that's been there long enough to become a feature of the face, like a nose.

Usually he'd look away. Look at the floor, or the advertisements, or the back of his own hands. He doesn't like looking at himself for long. Nothing wrong with the face. It's just that it always seems to be about to ask him something.

Tonight he doesn't look away.

He sits there and looks at the man in the window, with the city running through him, all the way to his stop.
