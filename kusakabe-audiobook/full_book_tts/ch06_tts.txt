Chapter six. The Night of a Hundred Demons.

He isn't there when it happens.

That's the first thing he knows about the war. He's in Kawagoe that morning, in a primary school storeroom that smells of floor polish and old gym mats, cutting a small sad curse out of a pile of folded flags. By the time Ijichi's car brings him back through the gate, it's already happened, and everyone is telling it.

Suguru Geto came to the school. In the open. In daylight. With people behind him and a few things that weren't people.

He stood on the grounds and said it out loud. On the twenty-fourth of December, at sundown, he would release a thousand curses in Shinjuku and a thousand in Kyoto. A Night Parade of a Hundred Demons. He made it very clear who the curses were for. People who can't see them. Everyone else could choose a side.

Then he left. Nobody stopped him. Gojo was there for some of it. That part gets told the most.

Kusakabe hears it from four different people before he reaches the staff room. A first-year auxiliary by the gate, white in the face. A Grade two in the corridor, too loud, telling it like a film. The dispatch coordinator, headset round her neck, who says it in one breath and then answers her phone. Okabe, who says it as if it were a procedural problem, which it is, among other things.

Each version is a bit different. The thousand stays the same in all of them.

A thousand, he thinks. They say it like a weather forecast.

He remembers Geto as a name. Years back, a name on lists, the student everyone said would be the other half of Gojo. Then a village, and a number nobody liked to say, and the name came off the lists and went somewhere quieter, where people only said it in low voices in car parks.

Now it's back. Loud.

He puts his sword bag against the wall, sits at his desk in the corner, and looks at the radiator that doesn't work. It ticks.

The twenty-fourth, he thinks, and does the arithmetic without wanting to.

It's a Sunday.

Thursday.

The dispatch board goes up on Thursday. Normally it's a grid of small square handwriting, one job per box. This week there's one line across the whole of the twenty-fourth, in red.

Shinjuku. All available. Kyoto, see Kyoto.

Under it, a list of names. His is about halfway down. Nobody has written "Requested." Nobody has needed to.

He stands in front of it for a while. Other people come and stand next to him and read their names and go away again. Nobody talks much. A Grade three he doesn't know reads the board twice, then takes out her phone and types something with both thumbs, very fast, and puts it away without sending it.

On Friday night he rings Ono Haruka.

She answers on the second ring. "Ono."

"Kusakabe. Sunday. Can you do the afternoon?"

A pause. He can hear a television where she is, a game show, someone winning something.

"It's Christmas Eve," she says.

"Yeah."

Another pause. Not long. Long enough for him to understand she has a plan for her evening, and to understand that she's going to tell him the price instead of the plan.

"Double rate after twelve," she says. "Agency rule. Holidays."

"Fine."

"Until when?"

He thinks about it. Sundown, and then however long a thousand takes.

"Overnight," he says. "Maybe."

"Maybe," she says, as if she's writing it on her hand. "I'll tell the night girl to stay till I come. What do I tell your sister?"

"Work."

"She'll ask."

"Tell her work."

"Fine," Ono says, and hangs up. He stands in his kitchen with the phone. He has said the word "Fine" to a cost again, without doing the sum.

Some habits you keep because they work, he thinks. Some you keep because you're tired.

Sunday morning.

Sunday he goes to his sister's anyway, in the morning, early, before anyone is expecting him.

The night carer opens the door with her coat already on. Lavender. The window chair. His sister is awake and watching the car park, where a man is trying to fit a Christmas tree into the back of a very small car.

"You're early," she says.

She knows him. He takes that and doesn't look at it too hard.

"Yeah."

"It's not afternoon."

"Got work later."

"On a Sunday?"

"Yeah."

She thinks about that. Her right hand moves a little on the arm of the chair, the way it does when she's following something.

"You're always working," she says. Not a complaint. A fact about him, like the weather.

He makes the tea. Three, out. She drinks it with the powder in, slowly, and makes a face at the texture, and drinks it anyway. Outside, the man gets the tree in by putting it through the passenger window. The top sticks out into the street.

She laughs. It surprises both of them.

He stays an hour and a half. At ten past eleven he stands up, and she's already looking at the window again, and that's easier. He says, "See you." She says, "Mm."

At the door the lavender gets him on the way out, the way it always does.

On the train he checks his pocket. The coin is there. It's always there. Today he just wants to know it hasn't moved.

Shinjuku, that afternoon.

Shinjuku at four in the afternoon on Christmas Eve is the brightest place he's ever been asked to die in.

That's how he thinks it, and then he tells himself not to be dramatic, and then he looks around again and thinks it anyway.

The illuminations are already on, though the sky isn't dark yet. Blue lights strung through every bare tree along the avenue. White stars on wires across the side streets. A giant screen on a building showing a perfume advert, a woman walking slowly through snow that isn't real. Shop speakers playing a song about a white Christmas, a song about a lonely Christmas, a song about bells. Couples holding hands. People queuing out of a fried chicken place in a line that goes round the corner. Office workers carrying white cake boxes by the string, very carefully, level, like something that might spill.

Nobody here knows.

The higher-ups have done what they could. Some streets are closed for "gas works." A few blocks have been emptied with police tape and nervous constables. But you can't empty Shinjuku on Christmas Eve. Nobody can. There are too many people, and most of them can't see what's coming. There's no form that tells a city of ten million to go home early because of something it can't see.

His sector is a grid of side streets east of the station. Bars. A pachinko parlour. A ramen shop with a paper lantern. Narrow alleys between the buildings, full of bins and air-conditioning units and pipes.

The auxiliary who briefs him is young, younger than Ijichi, with a laminated map in a plastic sleeve that keeps fogging when she breathes on it. She points at the map. He nods. She points again. He nods again.

"Grade twos there and there," she says. "Two Grade threes on the corner. If anything special grade turns up, don't engage. Call it in."

"Who comes?"

She opens her mouth. Closes it.

"Someone," she says.

"Okay."

"Gojo-san is somewhere west of the station," she says, as if it helps. It helps a little. It's like being told there's a fire station in the city. You don't know where. You know it exists.

She goes. He stands on his corner by the ramen shop with his sword bag over his shoulder. The lantern sways. The air smells of pork broth and cold exhaust. Somebody's phone is playing a ringtone that is also, for some reason, a Christmas song.

He looks at his hands. They're cold. It's December. Everybody's hands are cold. Nobody would notice. He puts them in his coat pockets, and his right hand finds the coin without being asked.

I want to be on my sofa, he thinks, watching a bad drama. A doctor one. Where the surgeon is also somehow a detective.

He'd watch the whole series. He'd watch the one after it. He wouldn't even complain about the ending.

The sun goes down behind the buildings at about half past four. There's no moment you could point to. The blue in the sky just gets thinner, and the blue in the trees gets brighter, until the trees are the only blue left.

Then the screaming starts.

Sundown.

It starts somewhere else first. Two streets away, maybe three. Not one scream. A lot of them, layered, the way traffic noise is layered, so you can't pick out a single voice.

Then the neon flickers. All of it at once, along the whole street, the way a light flickers in an old flat when someone runs the microwave. It comes back. It flickers again.

He feels the first ones before he sees them.

They come out of the alleys. Out of the gaps between buildings, where the bins are, where the pipes run down the walls. They come the way water comes up through a drain in a storm: not from anywhere, just up, all at once. Things with too many legs and things with none. A thing like a heap of wet coats. A thing with a long white neck and nothing at the top of it. They pour into the street under the Christmas lights and the lights shine on them and they don't care.

Most of the people on the pavement can't see them. That's the worst part. They see something. A man's hat blows off when there's no wind. A shop window cracks top to bottom. A woman stops and puts her hand on her chest as if she's forgotten something important. Then a car alarm goes, and another, and the crowd starts to move without knowing why, which is how crowds move.

He takes the sword out of the bag. He doesn't remember unzipping it.

He draws the circle. Or the circle draws itself. After this many years he isn't sure which comes first.

Still on the corner.

There's a family coming towards him down the pavement.

He notices them because they're the only people walking at a normal speed. A father with a little girl on his shoulders. A mother beside them with a cake box held level by the string. They're talking about something. The girl is pointing at the blue trees. They haven't understood yet that they're supposed to be frightened.

Behind them, out of the alley by the pachinko parlour, something unfolds.

It's long. It comes out of the gap between the buildings like a tape measure coming out of its case, flat and fast. At the front of it there's a mouth. The mouth is open, and it's level with the little girl's back.

He's already moving.

That's the strange part, every time. He's already moving, and the thought is still arriving. The thought is something like that's a family or that's not my sector or wait, and it never gets to the end. His feet have gone. His weight has gone. The edge of the circle has run ahead of him down the pavement because he's carried it there, and the thing crosses into it, and his hands answer.

One cut. A sound like wet cardboard tearing. Something cold sprays across his cheek.

The long thing falls apart in the air and is grey smoke before it reaches the ground.

The father has turned round. He's looking straight at Kusakabe. A man in a dark coat in the middle of the pavement, holding a sword, breathing hard. Behind him, nothing. The father can't see what was there. He can only see what's here.

He grips his daughter's legs and runs.

The mother runs with him. She drops the cake box. It lands on its corner, tips, and sits there on the pavement under the blue trees, the string still in a neat loop on top.

They don't look back. Not once. He watches them all the way to the end of the street, and they don't.

Good, he thinks. Keep going. Don't look at the strange man with the sword.

His heart is going like a hand slapping a table.

He picks up the cake box, because it's in the way, and puts it on the windowsill of the ramen shop, under the lantern. Then he turns round.

There are more coming out of the alley.

Later.

After that, the night goes methodical.

That's the only word for it. He's done this enough times to know there are two kinds of fight. There's the kind with a story, where something happens and you have to think. And there's this kind, where you don't think at all, because there's no time and no point. Curse, blade, next. Curse, blade, next. A thing comes into the circle. His hands answer. It goes. Another comes. He doesn't decide anything. He just stays where he is, and the street keeps sending things.

He counts, at first. Out of habit. He gets to fourteen.

Then he stops counting, because counting means thinking about how many are left, and nobody knows that.

The Christmas music keeps going. Nobody's turned it off. The shop speakers just carry on into the dark, a song about bells, a song about a lonely Christmas, a song about bells again. He fights through the whole playlist three times. By the third time he knows where the key changes are.

If I die tonight, he thinks, the last thing I'll have heard is a woman asking for nothing but me for Christmas. I'd like to state for the record I didn't choose this.

Around seven, a curse with a face like a crumpled paper bag gets through the edge of the circle on his left, where his attention has thinned, and something drags across his forearm. Not deep. It stings the way paper cuts sting, out of proportion. His sleeve goes dark and warm and then cold.

He doesn't look at it. Looking at it would be stopping, and stopping is how they get you.

The radio on his collar talks in pieces. Static. A voice. Static.

"Kyoto's the same. Same numbers."

"Anyone near the south exit, we need."

"Gojo-san's gone. Gone where? He just went."

He hears that one and keeps cutting. The fire station has left the city. There's nothing to do with that information, so he does nothing with it.

At some point a Grade two he half knows, a woman with short hair and a long spear, ends up back to back with him for a while at the corner. She's crying. Not loudly. Just steadily, tears running down her face while she fights, perfectly well, her spear going in and out like a sewing machine. Neither of them mentions it. After twenty minutes the street thins, and she nods to him and goes north, still crying, to wherever she's needed. He never learns her name.

Around ten there's a man in a doorway.

A salaryman, fifties, tie gone, a paper Santa hat from some office party sitting crooked on his head. He's too drunk to run and too drunk to be frightened, and he can't see a thing. He sits in the doorway of a closed bar with his knees up, watching Kusakabe cut at empty air, with the patient interest of a man watching street theatre.

Something with a lot of fingers comes up the street towards the doorway. Kusakabe steps across in front of it. One cut. Smoke.

The man claps. Slowly. Three times.

"Very good," he says. "Very good. Is it for the tourists?"

"Yeah."

The man nods, satisfied. He digs in his coat and holds out a mandarin.

Kusakabe looks at it. There's another thing coming out of the alley behind him, and his circle is already turning towards it.

He takes the mandarin and puts it in his pocket, next to the coin, and turns round.

Paid in fruit, he thinks. Better rate than usual.

The neon on the pachinko parlour goes out for good at about nine. The ramen lantern stays lit. The cake box stays on the windowsill.

Hours pass.

It goes on like that for a long time.

Then, sometime after midnight, it changes.

He can't say how. The curses don't stop. But they stop coming at anything. Before, there was a direction to them, a push, like a tide. Now they just wander. They come out of the alleys and stand around under the lights like people who've forgotten why they came into a room. They're easier. They're also sadder, which he doesn't want to notice, so he doesn't.

The radio gets quieter. Fewer voices. Longer silences.

His forearm has stopped bleeding. His shoulders have gone past aching into a sort of humming. His left heel, the shoe he still hasn't had fixed, has finally given up and flaps a little every time he steps.

Grade one sorcerer, he thinks. Saving Shinjuku with half a shoe.

At around three he realises he hasn't felt anything cross the circle for twenty minutes.

He stands there anyway. Another twenty. Then he lets the circle go, slowly, the way you let go of a heavy door, in case it swings back.

It doesn't.

Towards dawn.

The sky does what it does in December. It goes from black to a sort of dark grey that's hardly different from black, and stays like that for a long time, and then suddenly it's morning and you missed the change.

The Christmas lights are still on. In daylight they look cheap and tired, like someone still wearing a party hat on the train home.

He sits down on the kerb.

He doesn't decide to. His legs make the decision for him, and he lets them, because they've earned it. The kerb is very cold. His trousers are wet from something he'd rather not think about. The sleeve of his coat is stiff and brown from the elbow to the wrist.

The street is a mess. A shop window lies in pieces across the pavement, glittering. A bicycle is bent into a shape bicycles shouldn't make. Someone has left a single high-heeled shoe in the middle of the road, standing upright, as if its owner just stepped out of it and kept going. One of the two vending machines on the corner has been crushed flat against the wall. The other is fine. Lit up, humming, every button glowing.

He looks at it for a while.

Then he gets up again, which is worse than sitting down was, and digs in his pocket for coins, and buys a can of hot coffee. The can drops into the tray with a loud clunk that echoes off the empty buildings.

He sits back down on the kerb with it. He holds it in both hands first, without opening it, just for the heat.

Then he opens it and drinks.

It's sweet and thin and tastes mostly of the can. It's the best thing he's had in his mouth all year.

The machine survived the Night Parade of a Hundred Demons, he thinks. Someone should give the machine a medal.

In his pocket, his hand finds the mandarin. He'd forgotten it. He peels it on the kerb, the peel coming away in one long cold curl, and eats it a segment at a time. It's sour. He eats all of it anyway, and puts the peel in his other pocket, because there's nowhere else to put it and the street is untidy enough.

Morning.

People start to come back into the street as the light grows. Auxiliaries with clipboards. Cleaners, the real kind and the other kind. Two constables in reflective jackets, who look at him on the kerb, decide he's somebody else's job, and walk on.

A Grade two he knows by sight comes down the street from the station end. Tanaka, or Tanabe. Thirties, broad, a split lip, his jacket missing a sleeve. He's walking the way people walk when they've just heard something and need to tell it to someone. He stops by the kerb.

"You heard?" he says.

"No."

"The school," Tanabe says. "This lot was a distraction. All of it. Shinjuku, Kyoto. Geto wasn't here at all. He went to the school."

Kusakabe holds the can.

"For the Okkotsu kid," Tanabe says. "The first-year. The one with the special grade. He wanted the curse that follows him around. The girl."

Kusakabe has seen Okkotsu. A thin, quiet boy in the corridors with a sword bag and a way of apologising with his shoulders. And something behind him, sometimes, that the air didn't like. He kept his distance. Most people did.

"The other first-years were there," Tanabe says. "The Zen'in girl. Inumaki. The panda. Geto went through them. They're hurt. Bad, I heard. Alive."

He waits for Kusakabe to say something. Kusakabe doesn't.

"And the kid beat him," Tanabe says. "Okkotsu. Sixteen years old. Beat Suguru Geto. Then Gojo got there and finished it." He shakes his head, and laughs, the way people laugh when something is too big. "That's what they're saying. Okkotsu and Gojo. The heroes of the night."

"Okay," Kusakabe says.

Tanabe looks at him. Looks at the sleeve. Looks at the street, the glass, the shoe in the road, the crushed machine.

"Long one," Tanabe says.

"Yeah."

Tanabe nods and goes on down the street, to tell it to the next person.

Kusakabe drinks his coffee.

He thinks about the panda he's seen from the staff-room window, crossing the yard on its hind legs like a large man in a costume nobody is allowed to mention. He thinks about the Inumaki boy, scarf over his mouth. The girl with the sharp voice. First-years. Not his. He knows them by sight and they don't know him at all.

Alive, he thinks. He holds onto that part.

He thinks about Okkotsu, sixteen, in a courtyard he knows, against a man who said a thousand curses the way other people say good morning.

And Gojo, who was somewhere west of the station until he wasn't.

Heroes of the night, he thinks. Fair enough.

He doesn't feel cheated. He checks, the way you check a pocket, and there's nothing there. It was their night. It was always going to be. Somebody has to be the story. Somebody else has to stand on a corner by a ramen shop in a bad shoe and make sure the story has a city left to be told in.

He doesn't think it in those words. He thinks something more like: Fine. And: My arm hurts.

Still on the kerb.

His phone has been on silent all night. He takes it out.

Two messages. Both from Ono Haruka.

The first is from twenty to seven in the evening. It says: She asked where you were. Told her work. She said "he's always working."

The second is from twelve minutes past six this morning. It says: Overnight done. She slept. I've signed the sheet for double. Merry Christmas.

He reads that last part twice. He doesn't think Ono says Merry Christmas to anyone. He thinks she typed it because it was on the sheet as the date, and her thumb went on without her.

He types: Thanks.

He looks at it. Then he sends it, which is more than he usually does.

The coffee's gone. He holds the empty can for a while. It's still a little warm in the bottom.

The auxiliary with the fogging map comes past. She's lost the map. She has a smear of something across her forehead and she looks about twelve. She sees him, checks his face, checks his name off a list in her head, and keeps walking.

That's the debrief.

Nobody's going to file this, he thinks. Grade one, east of the station, held the corner. Nothing to add.

He looks at the ramen shop. The lantern has finally gone out. The cake box is still on the windowsill, a little damp, the string still in its neat loop.

He doesn't know where the family went. Home, he hopes. Somewhere with the lights on. A little girl telling her mother about the blue trees and not about anything else, because there wasn't anything else. Not for her. He made sure of that.

They'll never know there was a man.

She'll remember the blue trees, he thinks. Decent trade for a sleeve.

A little later.

He crushes the can in one hand. It folds easily, thin metal, a small dry crunch.

He drops it in the bin by the surviving vending machine. He puts the sword back in its bag. He stands, slowly, his back clicking in two places. He walks to the station with his heel flapping, past the blue trees still lit in the daylight, past people starting to come out for Christmas morning who don't look at him at all.

The train is nearly empty. He sits in the corner seat with his sword bag between his knees and his head against the glass and doesn't sleep, quite.

At home he takes off his coat, looks at the sleeve, and puts the whole coat in a plastic bag to deal with another year. He drinks a glass of water standing at the sink. He doesn't make coffee. He doesn't put the television on.

He lies down on the bed, on top of the covers, in his clothes, and looks at the ceiling for about four seconds.

Then he sleeps.

He sleeps for fourteen hours. Through Christmas morning. Through Christmas lunch, wherever people are having it. Through the afternoon and the short winter light and the dark coming back down. Somewhere in that time the phone buzzes once and he doesn't hear it. Somewhere the upstairs neighbour runs a bath.

When he wakes, it's night again, and Christmas is nearly over, and nobody has thanked him for anything.

He lies there and thinks about that, briefly. Then he turns over.

Nobody needs to.
