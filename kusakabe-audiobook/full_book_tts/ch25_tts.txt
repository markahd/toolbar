Chapter Twenty-five. Sunday.

There's a cupboard in the hall he doesn't open.

The narrow one by the front door. The vacuum cleaner lives in it, and an umbrella with a broken spoke, and a box of things from a desk he cleared out in some other year. Behind all of that, upright in the corner, there's a long shape wrapped in an old cleaning cloth.

Two pieces. He knows which way up they are. He put them there himself, in January, and shut the door, and hasn't opened it since.

He could throw it out. Steel is steel. A broken sword is just a length of something that used to be useful, and the bins go out on Thursdays.

He walks past the cupboard every morning on the way to his shoes. His eyes don't go to it. His hand doesn't go to the handle. There's a new sword on the wall in the other room, and he's mostly used to the weight of it now. Mostly.

Not ready to look at it, he thinks. Not ready to bin it either. Very decisive man.

That morning. It's April, and the letter is still on the kitchen table, where it's been for three weeks.

Thick paper. Brush script. A red seal at the bottom, pressed so hard it's left a dent in the table. It came from the dojo he was never invited to, from men he has met twice in his life, both times standing up.

It says, in a great many more words than it needs, that he is head of the New Shadow Style.

It also says the old vows are lifted. The rules about the circle. Who could be taught it, and by whom, and what they had to promise before anyone showed them anything. Struck out, all of it, by the same kind of men who wrote them, in the same kind of ink. He can teach it to anyone he likes now. Nobody has to promise anything.

He read it standing up, the first time. Then he sat down and read it again, which he has never once done with a letter.

He looks at it now, over the top of a cup of instant coffee that tastes the way it always has.

Promotion, he thinks. The one thing I spent my whole life avoiding.

It doesn't come with more money. He asked. Okabe went through two binders and said it wasn't a grade, as such. "It's more of a position."

"Unpaid."

"Honorary," Okabe said. Which is the same word with its shirt tucked in.

On Tuesdays and Thursdays now there are students in the yard who are learning the circle. Not many. They draw it too wide and drop it the second anything frightens them. He tells them that's normal. It's the most encouraging thing he's said to anyone in years, and he only says it because it's true.

Last Thursday a second-year he's still learning the name of asked him what they used to have to promise.

"Doesn't matter now."

"But what was it?"

He thought about it. "Not to show it to anyone outside the style."

"That's it?"

"That's the short version." The long version took a whole afternoon and a great deal of kneeling. He doesn't miss it.

The boy looked at him as if he'd been cheated of something. Kusakabe knows the feeling. He just never thought he'd be on the other side of it.

He's late for all of them.

After coffee. He gets dressed by the window, because the light's better there.

Shirt. He does the buttons from the bottom up. Halfway, his fingers go over the scars without meaning to, the way you'd run a thumb along a seam to find where it starts.

Two of them. Long. Across his chest, one above the other, not quite parallel. Pale now, pink at the edges when it's cold. They pull when he reaches for a high shelf, or lifts something awkward, or laughs, which isn't often enough to be a problem. Otherwise they keep to themselves.

He doesn't look at them in the mirror. He knows where they are. Same as the cupboard.

He does the last button.

On the way to the door he stops in the middle of the room and looks up. Corner. Above the wardrobe. The stain is exactly the shape it was, give or take. It's been patient with him. More patient than most things.

He takes out his phone and writes an email. It says this.

Dear Mister Sato. There is a water stain on the ceiling above the wardrobe. It has been there some time. Kusakabe.

He reads it once. He sends it.

Nothing happens. The ceiling doesn't fall in. The stain stays where it is. Somewhere across the city a man he's never met will open it on Monday and sigh.

Three and a half years, he thinks. Probably a record. Nobody's going to give me anything for it.

He puts his shoes on and goes past the cupboard without looking at it.

Sunday. The train is half empty.

Out of the window, spring is happening to other people's property. A row of allotments by the tracks, someone's tulips standing in a row as if for a school photograph. A sports ground where a man in a cap is pushing a little cart that paints a white line on the grass, very slowly, very straight. A school with its gates shut and one window open.

Across the aisle a boy of about ten is explaining a video game to his grandfather, level by level. The grandfather nods at all the wrong moments. Nobody else in the carriage is listening. Kusakabe is, a bit. It sounds like a very difficult game. The boy is winning it anyway.

There's a carrier bag between his feet. Rice crackers, the soft kind. Her tea. A new bottle of lavender for the shoe cabinet, because it's been two months, and because some things you do on time even if you don't do anything else on time.

His right hand goes into his coat pocket on its own. It's done that for years. Checking.

There's a key in there. A receipt he forgot to throw away. That's all.

He leaves his hand there for a stop or two anyway. Then he takes it out and puts it on his knee.

Her building. He rings the bell, then lets himself in.

The old lavender has nearly given up. He can only just smell it. He takes the bottle off the shoe cabinet and puts the new one in its place and stands there a second while the smell catches up with him.

Ono comes out of the kitchen with her coat already on. She never takes it off for long.

"Good today," she says. No hello. "Ate all her breakfast. Asked for you at nine."

"Nine?"

"I said Sunday. She said it was Sunday. She was right."

He bends to take his shoes off. Something pulls across his chest and lets go. He keeps his face where it is.

Ono watches him do it. She doesn't ask. She takes the rabbit notebook out of her bag and writes something in it, small and upright, and puts it on the counter where he'll see it.

"Tuesday," she says, and goes.

He reads it. Brother here ten fifteen. Stiff.

Fair, he thinks.

In the front room. His sister is in the window chair.

The window's open a little way, the first time this year. The air coming in smells of somebody's washing and the car park warming up. The blanket on her knees is the thin one. The light falls across her hands, and the side of her face.

He's ready to wait. He's always ready to wait.

She turns her head before he's in the room.

"Atsuya," she says.

Just like that. No minute. No looking him over first. No father's name. Straight away, as if he were someone who came round all the time and she'd been expecting him.

"Hey," he says.

"You're late."

"Train."

"Mm." She doesn't believe him. She doesn't mind.

"Open that more," she says, and lifts her chin at the window. "It's nice out."

He reaches up for the top catch. It's stiff. It's always been stiff. Across his chest the two lines pull tight, all the way along, and he stops with his arm up and waits for them to finish.

Then he gets it open. The air comes in properly, warm, with a bit of grit in it.

"There," she says, satisfied, as if she did it.

He goes and makes the tea.

The bag goes in. He counts to three. The bag comes out. The water is barely green, like the idea of a leaf. He makes his own from the same bag, which is worse than hers, and carries both through, and holds her cup while she drinks.

She takes a sip. She thinks about it.

"Better," she says. "You're getting the hang of it."

He has made it exactly the same way for years.

"Thanks."

He sits down on the stool beside her chair. Carefully. She notices that. He can see her notice it.

She looks at him for a long moment. Properly. The way she'd look at a coat in a shop window, deciding whether it was worth what they were asking.

"You look tired," she says.

"Yeah," he says. "Long few months."

"Were you working?"

He doesn't answer straight away.

It's all there, for a second. Not in order. Not as a story. Just there, the way things are in the circle, all at once, at arm's length.

Halloween, and a sky with nothing in it. A slab of stone, and the dark under it. A paw held out, and his hand going to it. A girl from Kyoto with her knees locked. Ice in his eyelashes. A felt bear with one ear, sitting on a pile of papers. A boy at a line in the dirt, not looking down. A calendar with one blue circle on it.

Shinjuku.

She waits. She's patient, today. Her good hand is resting on the arm of the chair, near his.

"Yeah," he says. "Bit of work."

She nods. As if that's a perfectly good answer. As if it covers it. Then she turns back to the window.

At the window. He looks where she's looking.

There's a path that cuts across the corner of the car park towards the station. People use it as a shortcut, though there's a sign asking them not to. Two salarymen go along it, jackets over their arms, working on a Sunday and not happy about it. Three students with tennis rackets, all talking at once. A woman with a small brown dog that wants to stop at every single wheel, and does, and she lets it.

Nobody looks up.

Nobody knows there's a flat here, or a chair, or a woman in it who knew her brother straight away this morning. Nobody knows what anyone in this window did, or didn't do, or where they were in December. They just go to the station.

He doesn't need them to look.

He drinks his tea. It's terrible. He finishes it.

Later he'll do the medicine. Later he'll write in the notebook, under Ono's line, in his handwriting, which hasn't improved: Knew me straight away. He'll look at that for a while.

He won't cross it out.

His circle is still small. It always was. A flat on the ground floor with lavender by the door. A few students in a yard who drop it the second they're scared. A school of swordsmanship he never wanted, with his name at the top of a letter. A cup of tea, made too weak, the way she likes it.

That's all. Arm's length.

For a long time it felt like a place to hide.

Sitting here, with the window open and nobody looking up, it doesn't. It just feels like enough.
