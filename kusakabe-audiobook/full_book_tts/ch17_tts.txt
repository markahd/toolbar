Chapter Seventeen. The Count.

The first thing still standing is a postbox.

Red, square-shouldered, a little crooked on its post. Grey on top with dust, like everything, but red underneath, and upright. It's the first thing in a long time that's the shape it's meant to be.

Kusakabe looks at it as they go past.

Last collection, seven forty-five, he thinks. Missed it.

After the postbox, a wall with a window in it. Then a building with all its floors. Then a lamp post that's on, the light coming down in a cone full of drifting grey, as though it's snowing very slowly and only there.

Panda stops under it and looks up.

"Power," he says.

"Some."

They go on. The ground starts being a road again. There's glass on it, and stones, and a lot of paper, and one shoe, but it's flat, and it goes somewhere. Kusakabe's legs notice before he does. They stop checking every step.

The sword is still in his hand. He looks down at it, and then he puts it away. It goes into the scabbard first time. He's a little surprised by that.

At the top of Meiji-dori.

The van is at the top of Meiji-dori, where the radio said it would be. There are three vans now, and two cars with their headlights on, angled in across the road to make a sort of room out of light. Inside the room are people. Some standing. More sitting. More than that lying down.

Nobody's shouting. That's the first thing he notices. Earlier tonight everyone was shouting. Now people talk in ordinary voices, quite low, the way they talk in a hospital corridor when the doors are open.

On the side of the nearest van, somebody has been writing in marker pen.

Names. Two columns of them, down the white panel, in thick black capitals. The left column is long and goes all the way to the wheel arch. The right column is shorter. There's no heading on either.

A young auxiliary is standing at the van with the pen. Black suit, white shirt gone grey, a cut along his jaw that someone has closed with three strips of tape. He's writing the name of a woman sitting on the kerb. She spells it for him. He writes it in the left column.

Then he sees Kusakabe.

"Name?"

"Kusakabe. Grade One."

The auxiliary writes it, in capitals. Not very straight. He's run out of room at the bottom and has started a second line beside the first.

He looks at Panda. He opens his mouth. He looks at the pen.

"Just Panda," Panda says.

"Right. Sorry." He writes Panda. He looks at it for a moment, as if it might need a surname.

"Inumaki," Panda says. "Toge. Second year. Have you got him?"

The auxiliary runs the cap of the pen down the left column. Then, more slowly, the right one.

"No," he says. "Not on here. Doesn't mean." He doesn't finish it.

"Zen'in Maki?"

"No. Sorry. People are still coming in."

Panda nods. He stands there looking at the van, at the two columns, for longer than reading them would take.

Kusakabe doesn't read the right-hand column. He knows what it is. He doesn't need it to have a heading.

By the vans.

The radio inside the van is on. Somebody has turned it up so everyone can hear, and nobody can, quite. It comes in pieces.

"Dogenzaka, two more, need a..."

"The one at Shibuya Hikarie, is anyone..."

"Ijichi-san, repeat, Ijichi-san was..."

Kusakabe looks up.

"Stabbed, repeat, he's out, they got him out, he's..."

A long hiss.

The young auxiliary has gone still with the pen in the air. He waits. Everybody near the van waits.

"Alive. He's alive. Over."

The auxiliary lets out a breath. He puts the cap on the pen and takes it off again.

"He drove me here," he says, to nobody. "This afternoon. He said sorry about the traffic."

Of course he did, Kusakabe thinks. He'll apologise for this too. To the nurse. For bleeding on her.

It's not funny. It's the nearest thing to it there is, so he keeps it.

A moment later.

"You."

A woman is pointing at him. Fifties, short grey hair, a lamp strapped round her head that makes it hard to see her face. Blue gloves. Blood on the gloves up to the wrist.

"Can you lift?"

"Yeah."

"Then lift." She points the other way, down the road, where the dark starts. "They're bringing them up from the edge. We're short of hands. Go with him."

Him, it turns out, is a man in a fire brigade jacket that doesn't fit, holding one end of a folding stretcher.

Kusakabe looks at Panda.

"I'll go and dig," Panda says. "There's a team going back in. They want someone who can move the big pieces."

"Okay."

"You all right?"

"Yeah."

Panda looks at him the way he does. Then he goes, round the back of the vans, towards the grey. He moves differently out there. Faster. As if, now that he's got something to lift, the rest of him knows what it's for.

Kusakabe takes the other end of the stretcher.

Before they go, he looks south.

From up here, at the top of the slope, he can see it. Not all of it. The dust is still hanging, and there's no proper light. But enough. Enough to see where the buildings stop being buildings. Where the city just ends, and the grey begins, and goes on.

It isn't ragged. That's what he can't stop looking at. You'd expect it to be ragged, the edge of a thing like that. Bits standing, bits fallen, the way a sandcastle goes when the tide gets it. It isn't. The edge runs away from him on both sides in a long, clean curve. As if somebody had stood in the middle with a length of string and a pencil and drawn it.

A circle. Someone else's. A great deal bigger than his.

Everything inside it, cut. Everything outside it, mostly fine. A postbox. A lamp post. A van with names on its side.

So that's what it looks like, he thinks. From outside.

He doesn't like the thought. He puts it down with the others.

"Ready?" says the fire brigade man.

"Yeah."

Down at the edge.

It's easy work. That's what's wrong with it.

You walk down to where the light runs out. Someone has already got a person out of the rubble and onto the ground. You put the stretcher down beside them. You don't lift them; the medics lift them, with a board and a count. One, two, three. Then you pick up your end.

Then you walk back.

A person is heavier than they look and lighter than you're ready for. The weight comes through the poles into his hands, and up his arms, and settles in his shoulders, and it moves. It shifts when they breathe. He can feel it through the handles. It's the only way he knows, sometimes, that they are.

The fire brigade man is called Mizuno. He says so on the first trip, over his shoulder, as if it's a thing you ought to exchange. He's an auxiliary. The jacket was on the back seat of a fire engine with nobody in it, and he was cold. He doesn't say whose it is. He doesn't seem to want to know.

Mizuno walks in front. Kusakabe walks behind, where he can see the face.

He doesn't look at the faces. He looks at the man's back, and the road, and the place where the light starts again.

Fifty-two steps from the edge to the vans. He counts them on the first trip without meaning to. On the second trip he counts them on purpose. Fifty-two. On the third it's fifty-four, because they go round a car.

The man on the third stretcher is awake. He keeps saying he's fine and trying to sit up, and Mizuno keeps saying, "Lie down, sir," in a voice so patient it might be a recording. The man lies down. Then he tries to sit up again.

"Where's my phone," he says. "My wife's going to ring."

"Lie down, sir."

Kusakabe walks.

Fifty-four.

They put him down by the vans with the others. The woman with the head-lamp is already there, kneeling, and she doesn't look up.

"Again," she says.

They go again.

Later, on the road.

It's on the fifth trip, or the sixth, that the other names start.

Not on the radio. In the road. People pass each other carrying things, and as they pass they say whatever they've got, quickly, the way you'd hand someone a bag on a staircase.

"The Zen'in girl, the one with the glasses..."

He's walking backwards over a kerb. He doesn't turn round.

"Burnt. Bad. The fire one got her. The one with the head like a..."

"She was with the Zen'in men, the old one, they were all..."

"Took her out in the first car. She was talking. She was swearing, actually."

Then they're past, and it's just the road again, and the weight in his hands.

Swearing, he thinks. Good. That's good.

He tries to see it and stops himself. He's carrying someone. He needs his eyes for the kerb.

But he sees one thing anyway, without asking to. The third step from the bottom, at the yard. The practice sword lying across it, handle towards where he sits, the tape on the grip gone grey with her hands.

There won't be one there tomorrow.

He doesn't know when there will be.

Fifty-two, he counts.

Later.

Somewhere around one o'clock, the radio says a name he knows.

He's at the van when it happens, putting his end down. Mizuno has gone to get water. The radio is talking about a team in the south, and a street, and a list, and then a voice comes in over the top of it, a different voice, tired and very clear.

"Confirm Nanami, Grade One, Nanami Kento. Confirmed. Over."

That's all.

No one near the van reacts. Most of them don't know the name. A medic carries on cutting a sleeve. The woman on the kerb has her eyes shut.

The young auxiliary, at the side of the van, looks at his pen.

Then he steps along to the right-hand column. He has to crouch a little; it's further down. He writes slowly, in the same thick capitals as all the others. Nanami. K.

He stands back and looks at it.

Kusakabe looks at it too.

"Sure?" he says.

The auxiliary turns. He seems surprised anyone asked.

"They said confirmed." He swallows. "Someone saw. It was a curse. The one that does things to people. The shapes of people." He stops. "That's all I've got. Sorry."

"Okay."

Kusakabe stands there.

Six letters and one. Black on white, on the side of a van, in a stranger's handwriting. Not very straight, because the man is tired and the van isn't flat.

See you.

It's just there. Not as a memory. As a sound. A finger and thumb at a knot, and two words, and a pale suit going off down a wet street. As if somebody had pressed play on it, in his head, without asking.

See you.

He's waiting for something else to come after it. He doesn't know what. It doesn't come. It's just the two words, and then the space where they were, and then the two words again.

"You," says the woman with the head-lamp, from somewhere behind the light. "Are you lifting or not?"

He turns round.

"Lifting."

He picks up the end of the stretcher. Mizuno is back. They go down the road.

On the road again.

Fifty-two.

He thinks about the bakery.

He didn't mean to. It just arrives, the way the two words arrived. A small place near a station. Opens at seven. The owner starts at four. The breaded sandwich. Not the melon bread.

He was going to go. One Sunday. He never said so, out loud, to anyone. He'd just had it in mind, the way you have a thing in mind for later, and later is a place you think will always be there.

You could still go, he thinks. It's still there. The bakery didn't die.

He knows he won't. He knows it the way he knows the number of steps.

They put down the stretcher. They pick up another.

See you.

He thinks about a beach that's longer than it needs to be. A house near the sea. More than a hundred and forty books that somebody was saving for evenings. He wonders who gets them now. Whether they'll be read. Whether anyone will even open the boxes, or whether they'll just go on to the next place, still waiting, a hundred and forty evenings nobody's going to have.

Then don't work Sundays, Nanami said.

He doesn't. Almost never. It's past midnight now, the first of November, and Sunday is three days off, and he means to be at a ground-floor door on Sunday, ringing the bell before he uses his key. He's kept that one. He'll keep it.

The man who told him to is a name on a van.

Everything else they'll take, he said.

He's walking backwards over the kerb again. He gets it right. He's had practice.

And there's another thing, under all of it, smaller and colder, that he doesn't want to look at and looks at anyway. He told one person. Once. At three in the morning, in a break room, by a vending machine. I've got a sister. The only person at the school who knew.

Now nobody does.

He's back where he started. Nobody knows anything about him at all.

It should feel like safety. That's what he built it for.

It doesn't feel like anything. It just feels like the list got shorter.

Fifty-four.

Behind the vans.

Panda comes back a while later.

Kusakabe is sitting on an upturned plastic crate behind the second van, because the woman with the head-lamp told him to sit for ten minutes, and he did, because it was easier than arguing. He has a bottle of water he hasn't opened. His arms have that light, buzzing feeling they get after carrying, as though they're expecting the weight to come back.

Panda's fur is darker than before. Wetter. There's something dark on his forearms that isn't dust. He walks round the van slowly and stops in front of Kusakabe, and doesn't sit.

"I found someone who carried Toge," he says.

Kusakabe looks up.

"An auxiliary. A woman. She was on the team that got him out. Before. Near the station." Panda's voice is level and low. "He's alive. They've taken him. They've got him somewhere they can work on him."

"Okay."

"His arm," Panda says.

He stops.

Kusakabe waits.

"The left one," Panda says. "It's gone. It was the..." He makes a small movement with one paw, a flat, sideways movement, in the air between them, and then stops making it. "When everything came down. When it all got cut. He was in it."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything.

There isn't anything. He goes looking, the way he went looking under the rock, and there's nothing there to say. Okay is wrong. Yeah is wrong. Every word he's got is wrong and most of them are short.

So he sits.

Panda sits too. Not on a crate. On the road, a few metres off, with his back against the van's rear wheel. Not next to him. Near.

The boy who says little, Kusakabe thinks.

He doesn't finish the thought for a while.

He thinks about a bench outside a clinic. A tin of lozenges. The wrappers. Inumaki folds the wrappers when he's finished, into small careful squares, smaller and smaller, and puts them in his pocket. Kusakabe has watched him do it a dozen times. Two hands. You can't do it with one. He's never thought about that before. He's never had to.

Says little. And now has less to say it with.

Inumaki doesn't use words to talk. He uses rice-ball names, and a phone, and his hands. A hand tilted for depends. A zip pulled down to the chin when it matters. A thumb on a screen, typing thanks in lower case. Half of everything that boy says, he says with his hands.

Now he's got half the hands.

Kusakabe sits with that.

It doesn't do anything. It doesn't get bigger or smaller. It just sits there with him, on the crate, behind the van, the way the stretcher weight sits in his shoulders after he's put the stretcher down. Still there. Still the shape of what he was carrying.

Panda doesn't say anything either.

That's something he'll think about later, much later. That Panda could have said a great many things just then, and didn't. Could have said, we should have been there. Could have said, if we'd gone south. Could have said, if we'd gone down. He could have said a lot of things, and every one of them would have been fair.

Panda sits against the wheel with his paws on his knees, and looks at the road, and says none of them.

After a while the radio says something about a team at Dogenzaka needing someone strong.

Panda gets up.

"That's me," he says.

"Yeah."

Panda stands there a moment. He looks at the unopened water bottle in Kusakabe's hand. Then he goes back round the van, towards the grey, and doesn't look back.

Kusakabe opens the bottle. He drinks half of it. He sits a little longer.

Then he gets up, and goes round to the side of the first van.

The young auxiliary is still there with the pen. He's been there, as far as Kusakabe can tell, the whole night. He's writing a name in the left column, and his hand is shaking a little, so the letters lean.

Kusakabe holds out the bottle. Half full.

The auxiliary looks at it as if he's never seen one.

"Drink that," Kusakabe says.

"I'm fine."

"I know. Drink it anyway."

The auxiliary drinks it. All of it, in one go, with his head back, like a child. Then he looks at the empty bottle, embarrassed.

"Thanks."

Kusakabe takes the bottle back from him, so he won't have to work out where to put it. He doesn't look at the right-hand column on his way past. He knows how long it is. He knows one name on it.

Then he goes back, because the ten minutes are over, and the woman with the head-lamp is already looking for him.

After that.

They run out of stretchers before they run out of people.

So he carries a door instead, with Mizuno, an office door with the handle still on, a woman lying on it holding the handle with one hand as if it might open. Fifty-two.

He holds a torch for a medic who's putting a line into a man's arm, because her head-lamp has died. He holds it very still. She doesn't thank him. She says, "Left a bit," twice, and then, "There," and then, "You can go."

He walks a boy of about twelve, in a skeleton costume, up and down the line of people sitting on the kerb, looking for his mother. They don't find her. The boy doesn't cry. He just says, "She was wearing a crown," to every woman they pass, as though that should be enough. Kusakabe leaves him with an auxiliary who has a list of children. The boy gives his name to her very politely. Kudo. She writes it down.

He finds Tanabe, or Tanaka, sitting on the bumper of the third van with a dressing over the swollen eye.

Tanabe looks up at him with the other one.

"You held your stairs," he says.

It isn't a question. Kusakabe doesn't know what it is.

"Yeah," he says.

Tanabe nods. He looks at the right-hand column on the side of the first van. He's quiet for a while.

"I didn't go back down," he says. "In the end."

"No."

"Nobody did. Nobody could have. You hear that?" He's still looking at the van. "That's what they're saying."

Kusakabe hears it. He doesn't know what to do with it, so he leaves it where it is.

"Eye all right?"

"It's there." Tanabe touches the dressing. "That's about all I'm asking of it tonight."

That's all. Kusakabe goes and picks up his end of the next thing.

See you.

Later still.

Somewhere after two, the woman with the head-lamp sends him off the line.

Not to rest. To look. There's a side street running east off Meiji-dori, a block down, where the power's still out. Somebody thinks they heard someone. Nobody's been down it since the ground stopped moving.

"Look," she says. "Shout if there's anyone. Don't be a hero."

"Wasn't planning to."

She looks at him for the first time all night, properly, under the lamp. Whatever she sees, she doesn't say.

"Good," she says. "We've got enough of those in the right-hand column."

He goes.

A block away.

The side street is dark.

Not the dark under the rock. This dark has edges. Shapes of buildings, still mostly up. Shop shutters, some down, some half down, one ripped out of its rails and lying in the road like a dropped page. A bicycle rack with no bicycles. Glass underfoot, so much of it that every step sounds like walking on sugar.

He walks the middle of the street. He lets the circle out, a little. Not to fight anything. Just to listen.

Nothing crosses it. Nothing alive. He walks the length of the street and back again, and calls once, "Anyone?" and his voice goes off between the buildings and doesn't come back with anything.

Nobody, he thinks. Good. Or not good. Just nobody.

He's turning to go when he sees the light.

Halfway down, set back in the doorway of a closed shop. Pale, steady, very white. It's the only thing on the whole street that's lit.

A vending machine.

It's standing there in the dark as if nothing has happened. Front panel glowing. Two rows of small cans behind the glass, each one with its little price underneath. A row of buttons, lit red for hot. The glass is cracked across one corner, a long thin line. Otherwise it's fine. It's humming. He can hear it from the middle of the road.

Somewhere under all this, a cable still runs to it. Or a battery. Or it's just too stubborn to stop. He doesn't know how they work. He's never once wondered.

He walks over to it.

He stands in front of it, in its light. It lights him up, too, the front of his coat, his hands, the dust in the creases of his knuckles. The black cloth round his left hand, three times and a tuck, filthy now.

He puts his hand in his coat pocket for change.

There's paper in there. Folded, soft, a square of something waxy. He doesn't take it out. He knows what it is. He moves his fingers past it.

In the other pocket, coins. He picks them out by feel. Some small ones. And one that's bigger, and colder than the others, as though it's been in that pocket longer. It has. He knows which one it is. It's just a coin.

He leaves that one where it is, and feeds in the small ones.

He presses the button for coffee. Hot, black, the one with the plainest can. Something drops behind the flap with a heavy, ordinary clunk.

Then his hand goes back to the coins.

He watches it do it. It goes into the pocket, finds the cold one, and puts it in the slot, and adds two small ones, and presses the same button again. As if this is just what happens. As if this is what you do, at a machine, at night, at the end of a long job.

He's never bought two in his life.

Clunk.

He bends down and takes them out. One in each hand. They're hot, almost too hot to hold, the way they always are at first. He stands there in the doorway with the machine humming at his back.

He opens the first one with his thumb, and drinks some. It's bad. It's very bad. It tastes of the can and of sugar he didn't ask for, and it's the right temperature for the night, and he drinks it slowly.

The second one he doesn't open.

He holds it.

It's hot in his left hand, through the cloth. He can feel the heat going into the cut on his knuckles and staying there. He doesn't put it down. He doesn't put it in his pocket. He doesn't know who it's for.

Down at the end of the street, past the dark, there are headlights, and voices, and the van with the two columns on its side. Someone will be looking for him soon. The woman with the head-lamp. Again.

He'll go in a minute.

He stands in the light of the machine, in the dead street, drinking a bad coffee out of one hand. In the other, the second can slowly stops being hot. Then warm. Then it's just the temperature of his hand.

He holds it anyway.
