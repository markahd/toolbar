Chapter Twenty-One. Execute the Boy.

Sakaguchi has the same recorder.

Small, black, the size of a packet of gum. He puts it on the table between them and turns it so the little red light faces Kusakabe. The way he did in May, outside a clinic, with a boy coughing into his collar in the next room.

Same rimless glasses. Same navy suit. No tie.

"Kusakabe-san. Thank you for coming in."

"Fine."

It's a Thursday. Mid-November. A room at headquarters on the third floor, with a number on the door and no name. A window that looks straight at the wall of the next building, near enough to touch if it opened.

"This isn't about your conduct," Sakaguchi says. "I want to say that first. I know the last time we spoke it was."

"Okay."

"The Office is taking accounts from everyone who saw the vessel on the night of the thirty-first. While it was under the King of Curses' control." He opens the leather folder. "You're down as having been on Meiji-dori. With Panda."

He says Panda carefully, the way you'd step round something on a floor. Panda is not, this month, a name anyone at headquarters says comfortably.

"Yeah."

"Can you tell me what you saw."

Kusakabe tells him.

It doesn't take long. A street. A burning bin. Something dropping out of the dark. Four eyes. Two orders. Then nothing in the road.

"Did it harm anyone in your presence?"

"No."

"Did it speak to you directly?"

"It spoke to the street."

Sakaguchi writes that down. His handwriting still doesn't slope.

"And you didn't engage."

"No."

"Why not?"

Kusakabe looks at him.

Sakaguchi looks back, quite mildly, and waits. He isn't trying to catch anything. He just has a box on the form and the box wants a word in it.

"It told us not to move," Kusakabe says.

Sakaguchi writes that down as well.

Still in the room on the third floor. Then he turns the page.

"There's a second part," he says. "The Office is recording the views of Grade One staff who were witnesses. On the sentence."

Kusakabe already knows. It went round the school on Monday in four versions, and by Tuesday they'd agreed with each other.

The boy was sentenced to death in June. Gojo stood in front of it and said later, and later held, because Gojo was the one saying it. Now Gojo is sealed in something nobody can open, and later has turned out to belong to him, and not to the boy. The sentence is back. It never really went anywhere. It was just waiting in a drawer for the man holding it shut to leave the room.

"It's been reinstated," Sakaguchi says. "You'll have heard."

"Yeah."

"Some staff have asked for it to be deferred. On the grounds that the vessel wasn't in control at the time." He says this in the same voice as everything else. "Others haven't. I'm asking each witness whether they support the sentence being carried out. And, if so, on what timescale."

He waits.

Kusakabe looks at the window, at the wall of the next building. A pigeon is sitting on the one bit of ledge the spikes don't cover, looking very pleased with itself.

He thinks about Shibuya.

Not the whole night. One thing. The edge he saw from the top of Meiji-dori at one in the morning, where the ruin stopped. Round. Deliberate. All those streets, inside that line. All those people who'd come out for one night in a costume.

That came out of a boy. Through his hands. While he was somewhere else.

And it could come out again. Any time. The thing that stood in Meiji-dori and looked at all of them as if they were weather is still in there, waiting for a door to be left open. It doesn't care about any of them. It barely noticed they were there.

The next time, it might notice.

"Yes," Kusakabe says.

Sakaguchi writes. "You support it."

"Yes."

"Timescale?"

"Soon."

Sakaguchi's pen stops above the paper. "Can I put a little more than that?"

Kusakabe thinks about it. Not about the answer. About how many words it needs.

"Before it happens again," he says.

Sakaguchi writes that. Then he looks at it on the page for a moment, and Kusakabe can't tell what he thinks of it, because Sakaguchi gives everything the same depth of look. It's the one thing about him that's always been fair.

"Most of the people I've seen have said the same," Sakaguchi says. Not as comfort. As information. "Almost word for word."

"Then it'll be easy to file."

Sakaguchi's face doesn't change. It never does. That might be agreement. It might be the opposite.

"Yes," Sakaguchi says. "It will."

He reaches out and turns the recorder off. The red light goes. Kusakabe hadn't noticed how much he'd been looking at it.

Outside, afterwards. He walks back to the station the long way, round two blocks he doesn't need, and goes through it on the way.

Not cruelty, he thinks. I don't want anything to happen to a boy. I don't want anything to happen to anyone. That's the whole point.

That's true.

One life against the next Shibuya. That's not a hard sum. Anyone would do it.

That's true as well. Almost everyone did.

It's a decent argument. It's got numbers in it. It's the kind of argument people sit in rooms and nod at.

He walks a bit further, and then, at a crossing, with nothing to do but wait, he does the thing he's always been bad at, which is look at it from underneath.

It's the same feeling.

That's what he finds under there. Not the argument. The feeling the argument is standing on. The cold sliding thing in his stomach. He had it at the top of a staircase on Meiji-dori, with a man with one eye swollen shut telling him a floor number. He had it in a corridor in June, at the first flash of pink hair, ducking into an empty classroom. He had it when he stood in a road and was very carefully furniture.

Avoid B five F. That's all it ever says. Don't go near it. Make it so you never have to.

Now it's put on a better coat. Something with a collar. It says public safety, and timescale, and the next Shibuya, and every word of it is true. But it's the same thing underneath, wearing it.

Make it so you never have to be in a room with that again, it says.

The light changes. He crosses.

He doesn't change his mind. That's the thing. He looks at it from underneath, and sees exactly what it is, and it doesn't make any difference. He still wants it done. He'd still say soon. Seeing the fear doesn't make the fear wrong.

It might be both, he thinks. Scared and right. People manage both all the time.

He gets on the train. He doesn't sit down.

The next day, at school. It's the woman from dispatch who tells him about Okkotsu.

She's writing on the board in her small square hand. Most of the board is empty now.

"They've brought Okkotsu back," she says, without turning round. "Did you hear?"

"No."

"Special grade. They flew him in. He's got it."

She doesn't say what. Nobody has to say what, this month.

Kusakabe stands by the board with his hands in his coat pockets.

Okkotsu. He remembers him the way you'd remember weather. A thin first-year in a white uniform, a year ago and more, going along corridors with his shoulders up round his ears, as if the corridor were something he'd borrowed. And something with him that you felt in your teeth. Kusakabe used to wait for him to pass.

Then the Night Parade, and his name at dawn. Then abroad. Then a photograph in the homeroom binder, stamped in red.

He'd be seventeen now.

They've sent a boy, Kusakabe thinks. To do it to a boy.

It sits wrong. He notices that it sits wrong. He also notices, right behind it, quick and small and very relieved, the other thing.

Not me, though, he thinks.

They didn't ask him. Of course they didn't. A Grade One with no technique, a reprimand, a pencil note in a margin. He's the last name anybody would put on that list, and he's never been so glad of his file.

Better him than me. He thinks it, and then he hears himself think it. He doesn't take it back. There's no one to take it back to.

"When?" he says.

The woman from dispatch caps her pen. "He has to find him first." She looks at the board, at the gaps in it. "The boy didn't come back. After. Someone says he's still out in it. In Shibuya. On his own."

On his own, he thinks.

"Hm," Kusakabe says.

He goes back to his desk. He sits down and tries, for no reason he can name, to picture the boy's face.

He can't.

He's had a whole year. He's been in the same buildings, on the same paths, under the same roof. And what he has is this: pink hair across a courtyard. A hood. The back of a head, in the grey at dawn, bent forward over its own knees. And a face in Meiji-dori, with black marks across it and two extra eyes, that wasn't the boy's at all.

The only time he's seen it up close, somebody else was using it.

He doesn't know what colour the boy's eyes are when they're his. He doesn't know what his voice sounds like up close. He doesn't know if he's frightened now.

You don't need to know the boy, he thinks. You only need to know what's in him.

That's what he told himself all summer. It held up all summer.

It holds up now, too. It just takes a bit more leaning on.

On Monday the ginkgo in the front courtyard has let go of everything.

It does it once a year, in a single night. You come in and the tree is bare and the ground is gold.

He crosses the courtyard through it. It's the courtyard where the girl threw a leaf, three weeks ago, and somebody laughed, and he turned round.

Maki is back.

He sees her from the top of the path above the training yard. She's standing in the middle of the packed earth with her staff, and for a moment he thinks she's training, and then he sees she's leaning on it.

She's never leaned on anything.

The left side of her face is dressed. A square of gauze from the cheekbone down, taped at the edges, and more of it going down her neck into her collar, where he can't see the end of it. Her left forearm is bandaged to the wrist. What he can see of the skin round the edges of it is shiny, and pink, and the wrong kind of smooth. Her glasses are on. One arm of them has been bent and bent back.

They let her out early. Or she got dressed and went.

Panda is on the bottom step.

He's sitting the way he used to, legs out. He sees Kusakabe on the path, and lifts a paw, and then puts it down again, and looks at Maki.

Maki has a sheet of paper.

In the training yard. She doesn't wait for him to sit.

She comes across the yard to the foot of the steps, slowly, the staff taking some of each step. She holds the paper up to him, at arm's length, so he has to come down two steps to take it.

He takes it.

Request for Deferral of Sentence. Then four paragraphs, typed. He reads the first one. The undersigned, students of Tokyo Jujutsu High, respectfully request. He doesn't read the others. He doesn't have to.

At the bottom, two names.

Zen'in Maki. Fast, hard, the pen almost through the paper.

Inumaki Toge. Slower. Careful. A signature written by someone who's had to learn which hand is going to do things now.

Panda's isn't there. Panda doesn't officially exist this month.

"Ogawa says it'll go in a drawer," Maki says. Her voice is rough. Smoke, maybe. Or just the dressing pulling when she talks. "He's probably right. It goes in a different drawer if a Grade One signs it. One they open."

She holds out a pen.

He looks at the pen. Then at the paper. Then at her.

"No," he says.

The pen stays where it is, for a moment. Then she lowers it.

"Why?"

"I already gave them my view."

Panda, on the bottom step, has gone very still. Not frozen-still. The other kind. Listening.

"What view," Maki says.

He could not answer. He's good at that. He could say it's complicated, which is a sentence that's never once been true when anyone said it. He could say Traffic.

He doesn't. He finds he doesn't want to lie about this one. It's the one thing he's sure of.

"That it should be quick," he says.

The yard is quiet. Nobody says anything.

Up at the main building, somebody is trying to start a leaf blower. Kusakabe hands the paper back. Maki takes it without looking at it, and folds it once, and holds it against her side.

"He's fifteen," Panda says.

He says it quietly, from the step. Not to argue. As if it's a fact Kusakabe might have mislaid.

"I know."

"It wasn't him. In Meiji-dori. You were there. You saw."

"It was his body."

"That's not the same."

"It's the same body the next time."

Maki laughs. One short breath through the nose, no sound in it. It isn't a laugh.

"You've never spoken to him," she says.

He doesn't answer.

"Not once. Not all year. You went round the outside in the rain so you wouldn't walk past his door." She looks at him through the bent glasses. "I saw you. You were soaked. You said it was the rain."

"It was raining."

"The walkway was dry."

He doesn't say anything to that. There's nothing to say to it. It was dry.

"You hid from him since June," Maki says. "And now you want him dead before you have to stop."

That isn't fair. It's also not wrong. He stands on the step with both of those things and finds he can't put either of them down long enough to answer.

"It's not about me," he says.

It comes out flat. He hears how it sounds. Like a man telling you there's nothing in his pockets while holding them shut.

Maki hears it too.

"No?" she says. "Do you remember Masaru? The one with the flask. On the stool. In the rain."

He remembers.

"He said it'd be tidier. If I had an accident. Save everybody a file." She shifts her weight on the staff. Something in her face moves under the gauze, and she waits for it to stop. "You stood up. I didn't hear what you said. Panda did. He told me after. He said it was the only time he'd ever seen you get up quickly."

Panda doesn't say anything.

"So tell me how this is different," Maki says. "A kid nobody asked for. Something in him the grown-ups are scared of. It'd be tidier."

It is different, he thinks. You didn't kill a district. You don't have a thousand years in you looking out through your eyes.

And then, right behind it: He didn't kill a district either. Something in him did, while he was gone.

And behind that: She didn't ask to be born a Zen'in. He didn't ask to eat a finger. He did it to save somebody. Ogawa told you that in June. You heard.

He doesn't say any of it. He could say the first one. It's the strongest. But he can feel the other two standing right behind it. If he opens his mouth, they'll come out as well, in the wrong order, and he'll have argued her side for her.

"It's different," he says.

"How."

He looks at the yard. The storehouse. The line of bare cherry trees at the far end, black against the white sky.

"It just is."

Maki looks at him for a long time.

It isn't the look from April, when she called him lazy. That one had heat in it. There was something she wanted from him, and she was angry he wasn't giving it. This one doesn't want anything. She's finished asking. It's the look you'd give a name on a list, just before you put a line through it.

"Okay," she says.

She turns round and goes back across the yard. Slowly. The staff taking some of each step.

On the bottom step. Panda stays where he is a moment longer.

"Sensei."

"Yeah."

"He'd probably agree with you." Panda is looking at his own paws. "Yuji. If you asked him. He'd say they were right. He'd say it was his fault." A pause. "That's sort of the worst part. He'd let them."

Kusakabe doesn't know what to do with that. He hadn't thought about what the boy would say. He hadn't thought about the boy saying anything. In his head the boy has never once been a person who talks. He's been a door.

"You'd like him," Panda says. "I said that before. You said let's keep it that way."

"I remember."

"You kept it that way."

Panda gets up. He does it all at once, the way he does. He brushes off the backs of his legs. He doesn't look at Kusakabe.

"See you, sensei."

He goes across the yard to where Maki is standing under the cherry trees, and sits down on the cold ground beside her, and she leans on him instead of the staff.

Kusakabe goes back up to the third step and sits.

He could go over there. It's forty metres. He could stand in front of them and say a better thing. There is a better thing. He can feel the shape of it, somewhere about his breastbone. Something about the edge of the ruin, and his hands not being enough.

He'd have to stand in front of two students, one of them with half her face under gauze, and explain why he's frightened, and why that makes him right.

He doesn't go.

He sits there until the leaf blower stops, and then a while after, and then he gets up and goes to his desk.

That night he puts the TV on and doesn't watch it.

A man in a studio pointing at a map of Shibuya with a ring drawn on it. He changes the channel. Cooking. He leaves that.

You could have signed it, he thinks. It goes in a drawer. Ogawa said. It wouldn't change anything.

That's exactly why he couldn't.

If it went in a drawer, it was a lie. A small one. A man putting his name to a thing he doesn't believe so that two students would look at him the way they used to. And if it didn't go in a drawer? If it changed something? If his name on the bottom was the one that tipped it, and the boy lived, and the next time came. And the edge of the ruin was round some other part of the city. Round a ground floor by a window, say.

He'd rather they looked at him like that.

He'd rather it was him they looked at like that.

It doesn't feel brave. It feels like holding something hot because putting it down would be worse.

He turns it off and sits in the dark.

Over the next weeks. The distance doesn't arrive all at once. It settles, a bit more each day, like dust on a shelf nobody's going to wipe.

He teaches. They turn up. That part doesn't change. The arrangement holds. It's just got a crack in it now, the kind you only see when the light is low.

Maki comes to the yard most days, against everybody's advice. She can't train properly. She does what she can. Footwork, one-handed. Balance. Standing on one leg with her eyes shut until the burnt side pulls and she has to stop. She does it at the far end, under the cherry trees, facing away.

She speaks to him when she has to.

"Sensei. I'm going to the clinic at three."

"Sensei. Is the storehouse open."

She's never once called him that. Eight months, and it was always nothing. No name, no title. You're late. Just the sentence, straight at him, as if there wasn't room for anything else in front of it.

Now there's room. She's put sensei in it, very politely, like a bag on the seat beside her on a train.

It's the coldest thing she's ever said to him.

"It's open," he says.

In the mornings, Panda still says good morning.

"Morning, sensei." Every day. In the corridor, at the classroom door, on the path. Not cold. Panda isn't any good at cold. It's just the word, and the voice is a nice voice, and then Panda goes on past.

He doesn't come to the steps anymore.

Kusakabe doesn't notice the first day. On the second he tells himself it's the weather. By the end of the week there isn't any weather, just a dry white sky. Panda is across the yard every afternoon, lying flat out in the dust near Maki, the way he used to lie near Kusakabe. The step below Kusakabe's is empty.

It's a strange thing to miss. Panda never did anything there. Sat with his legs out. Asked a question now and then, and was content with one word back. Was large, and warm, and there, inside the circle, the way a stove is there in a cold room. You don't look at it. You just know which side of you it's on.

Now Kusakabe knows which side it's on. It's on the far side of the yard.

He doesn't call him over.

He thinks about it once, on a Wednesday, with a can of coffee going cold in his hands. Panda. Come and sit. Three words. He could say three words.

He doesn't. He knows what they'd sound like. They'd sound like a man asking to be let off.

Later. Inumaki comes back for a day.

He's not allowed back properly. He's been somewhere else, learning how to do up a coat. He comes in the afternoon, with Panda walking beside him a little closer than he needs to, and stands at the top of the steps.

His collar is zipped to the bridge of his nose, the same as always. Under his jacket, the left sleeve is folded flat and pinned to the shoulder with a safety pin. A big one. The kind you'd use on a nappy.

Kusakabe is on the third step. Inumaki stops beside him.

Kusakabe waits for it. Kelp. Or Bonito flakes. Or the hand tilt, tuna mayo, with the one hand he's got. Something. In eight months Inumaki has never once passed him without a word. Even when there was nobody to say it to, he said it to nobody.

Inumaki looks at him.

He doesn't say anything at all.

It goes on for about as long as it takes to breathe in and out twice. Then he goes down the steps, past the bottom one, across the yard to the cherry trees, and Maki moves her staff so he can sit.

Rice ball words, Kusakabe thinks. He's spent his whole life making sure his words don't hurt anybody.

He's found out that not saying one works too.

In the staff room. There's no news.

Or there's news, in pieces. Okkotsu was seen in Shibuya. Okkotsu wasn't. Someone says he found the boy. Someone says the boy wasn't alone. Nobody says any of it twice, or to Kusakabe directly. It goes round the staff room and past his desk and out again, like a smell from somebody else's lunch.

He doesn't ask.

He finds he's listening for it, though. The same way you listen for a phone you've decided not to answer. Every time the staff-room door opens, part of him leans to hear if the next sentence is the one. It's done.

He tells himself it's so he can stop thinking about it.

He doesn't look too closely at what he'll feel when it comes. He has a fair idea. He felt it once already, in July, in Ijichi's car, when he heard the boy's heart had been torn out. Relief. He's never told anyone that.

He doesn't want to feel that again.

He wants it done anyway.

Both. People manage both.

A Friday. The cold has come down properly now, and the light goes early.

Maki leaves at four for the clinic. Panda goes with her. They go up the far side of the yard, by the cherry trees, where there aren't any steps. Just a slope of dry grass and a path worn through it. Kusakabe has never once seen anybody use that way before. They've used it every day this week.

He stays.

He tells himself he's waiting for the cold to get bad enough to be worth going in. Nobody's going to ask.

The yard empties out the way the ginkgo did. All at once, and then it's just the ground.

He's on the third step from the bottom. The stone is cold through his coat. The storehouse doors are shut. The cherry trees have nothing on them. The packed earth has the marks of Maki's feet on it, one-legged circles at the far end, in the place she's been standing all month with her back to him.

He looks down.

The bottom step is empty. So is the second. So is the one beside him, where nobody ever sat, but which was always, somehow, part of the arrangement.

Caught in the crack between the bottom step and the next one, there's a tuft of fur. Black, with a little white in it. The wind keeps trying to take it, and it keeps not going.

Weeks, and it's still there. Nobody's sat close enough to knock it loose.

The lights come on along the path, one after another, up towards the main building.

He doesn't move. Gate in front of him. Wall at his back. The whole yard in his reach, if he wanted to draw it, and nothing in it to feel.

All those steps. All that room.

Just him on them.
