Chapter five. Days Off.

The leave isn't his idea.

It arrives on a Friday at the end of November, as a printout with a yellow line through the middle of it. Okabe's highlighter. Annual leave, unused: eleven days. Leave not taken by the end of the calendar year will be forfeited. Under that, in Okabe's own careful pencil: Please take at least five before December. The office is concerned about the figures.

Not concerned about him. Concerned about the figures. Kusakabe appreciates the honesty.

"Monday to Friday," Okabe says, standing by his desk. "Dispatch has already moved your files."

"Fine."

"You could go somewhere."

"Could."

Okabe waits, as if Kusakabe might name a hot spring. Kusakabe looks at the radiator. Eventually Okabe goes away, and Kusakabe sits there with the printout, and five empty days open up in front of him like a field with no path across it.

I'll do things, he thinks. Shoes. Laundry. The bank.

He folds the printout in four and puts it in his pocket.

That weekend.

Sunday he goes to his sister's, the same as always. Lavender at the door. Tea too weak. Mrs. Fujita reads him the cat notebook in her quick flat voice and he listens. Nothing in it is new. That's the best kind of week.

Monday he wakes at six ten, because the body doesn't know about leave.

He lies there. Then he gets up, and makes coffee, and takes it to the sofa instead of the window. That's the first decision of the day, and it turns out to be the only one.

He sits.

He has a list. It's in his head, which is the safest place for a list, because nobody can check it. Shoes to the repair place by the station; the left heel has been going for a month. Laundry. The bank, about the card.

He'll do the shoes after the coffee.

The coffee goes cold in his hand. He drinks it cold. It's not worse. That's the most you can say for it.

There's a patch of sunlight on the floor. In late November it comes in low, across the boards, a pale square with the shadow of the window frame in it like a cross. When he sat down it was by the door. By eleven it's reached the leg of the low table. By one it's climbing the side of the sword bag against the wall.

He watches it go. He doesn't decide to. It's just the thing in the room that's moving.

At some point he eats crackers from the packet. At some point he goes to the toilet and comes back and sits in exactly the same dent. The sofa keeps his shape for him while he's gone.

The square of light thins, goes yellow, goes orange, slides up the wall and off it.

By half past four it's dark.

He doesn't turn the lamp on. Turning the lamp on would mean admitting it's evening, and then he'd have to admit what happened to the afternoon.

Shoes tomorrow, he thinks.

The days that follow.

This isn't rest. He knows that much.

Rest is something you come back from. You rest, and then you get up a little more yourself than you sat down. This is the other thing. This is being switched off at the wall. The days don't go anywhere. They just stop being days. He sits inside them the way you sit in a waiting room, without a number, without a magazine, without anyone due to call his name.

He doesn't put the television on. In the evenings, usually, he puts it on so the room has a voice in it. This week he doesn't even do that. The flat is quiet enough to hear the fridge thinking. The neighbour's washing machine goes into its spin cycle through the wall, and the floor hums very faintly under his feet, and then stops.

He doesn't speak to anyone.

Not on purpose. There's just nobody to speak to, and he doesn't go looking. On Monday evening he goes down to the convenience store for rice and eggs and a can of coffee. At the till the boy says the total. Kusakabe holds up his card, and the boy taps it, and that's the whole exchange. He nods. The boy nods. Nobody has to use a voice.

On the way back up the stairs he tries to clear his throat, and it comes out cracked and strange, like a door that hasn't been opened since summer.

Sunday, he thinks. Last word I said out loud was to the night carer. "Night."

He finds he doesn't mind. That's the part that should worry him, probably.

Tuesday morning.

The phone goes on Tuesday morning, at twelve minutes past ten.

It's on the floor by the sofa, face down, because face up it lights every time something wants him. It buzzes against the boards. It turns, very slowly, a few degrees each time, like a beetle on its back.

He leans over and looks.

Fujita.

He watches it ring.

There's no reason not to answer. He's awake. He's the least busy he's been in six years. He could pick it up and say "Yes," and it would take four minutes.

But answering means being a person on the phone. It means being Atsuya Kusakabe, the brother, the one who pays, with a voice and answers and decisions, at ten in the morning, in a week that was supposed to be empty. It means the field gets a path across it, and the path has somebody standing on it, waiting.

It rings out.

The screen says he has a missed call for a while, then goes dark.

She'd text if it was serious, he thinks. She always texts.

At eleven fifty she texts.

Please call when you have a moment. Nothing urgent now. Fujita.

He reads it twice. He notices the now. It's sitting at the end of the sentence like someone standing at the end of a corridor, not quite looking at you.

Nothing urgent, he thinks, and holds onto that part, and lets the other part go.

After lunch.

After lunch it's after I've eaten something. After he's eaten something it's when it's dark, she'll be home by then. When it's dark it's too late now, she'll be tired, she has an early start.

Every one of them is reasonable. That's how it works. None of them is a no. They're all just not yet, lined up one after another, until the day runs out of room.

Wednesday.

On Wednesday an unknown number rings at nine.

He doesn't answer unknown numbers. Nobody does. It's a rule of modern life, like not making eye contact on trains. It rings out. No message.

It rings again at two. Same number. No message.

Insurance, he thinks. Someone selling a better phone plan.

He looks at the Fujita message again. It's still there. He sets a time this time, a real one, out loud in his head like a man setting an alarm. Six o'clock.

At six o'clock he's in the shower, which he started at five fifty-five without thinking about why.

At seven he has the phone in his hand, his thumb over the green button. She'll tell him whatever it is, and he'll have to answer it, and he doesn't know what it is, so he doesn't know the answer. He can't stand on a phone line without an answer.

He puts the phone face down on the floor.

Tomorrow, he thinks. Properly. First thing.

The sofa takes him back without comment.

Thursday morning.

He calls on Thursday at quarter past nine, standing up, in his coat, because sitting down felt too much like the last three days.

She answers on the first ring.

"Kusakabe-san."

"Sorry. I missed your call."

"Calls," she says. Not sharply. Just accurately.

"Yeah."

A pause. He can hear the flat behind her. A spoon on a cup. A radio low in the kitchen. Then a door closing, which means she's gone into the hallway so his sister won't hear.

"Tuesday morning she choked," Mrs. Fujita says. "On her tea. Not badly. She was fine. But it went on longer than it should, and she was frightened, and afterwards she didn't want to eat anything. And I didn't know if I should call the doctor or if you'd want to be the one who decided. So I called you."

He stands very still in his own entrance hall, one shoe on.

"I called the doctor in the end," she says. "He came at three. He says it's the swallowing, it happens, he's written it down. Thicker drinks. He'll come again next week. She's fine." She breathes in. "She's fine, Kusakabe-san. I want to say that first."

"Okay."

"The other thing."

He waits. He knows. Some part of him has known since the word now.

"I've given notice to the agency," she says. "I told them on Monday. They were supposed to tell you. Friday is my last day."

There it is.

He looks at the shoe in his hand. The left one. The heel he was going to get fixed.

"It's not only the calls," she says, quickly, the reasons lining up the way they always do, ready to be read out. "It's my knees. It's my mother, she's in Saitama now, I need to be closer. They've offered me a place nearer her, mornings, a lady who lives with her daughter. I've been thinking about it since the summer." A pause. "But it's the calls too. I'm sorry. I'm not going to pretend it isn't. Three years, I've never once rung you about anything that wasn't something. And Tuesday I stood in that kitchen with her coughing and I thought, if it goes wrong, it's just me. It's always just me."

He could tell her about the sofa. He could tell her he was on leave, that he was right there, that the phone was eighteen inches from his hand. That would make it worse. He doesn't.

"Understood," he says.

"I'm not angry."

"Okay."

"I am a bit," she says. "But I'm not."

"Yeah."

"Friday, then," she says. "I'll do the notebook properly. You'll need to talk to Mr. Hasegawa. At the agency. He's been trying to call you."

Insurance, he thinks. Better phone plan.

"I'll call him," he says.

There's a short silence in which they both hear exactly how much that's worth this week.

That afternoon.

Midori Home Care is on the second floor of a building between a dry cleaner and a shop that sells nothing but umbrellas.

He goes in person on Thursday afternoon, because he called Mr. Hasegawa back at ten and Mr. Hasegawa said It would be easier to do this face to face, in the voice of a man who has learned what happens to families over the phone.

The stairwell smells of steam from the cleaner below. Upstairs is one room with four desks, two of them empty, and a whiteboard covering one whole wall. A grid of days and names in coloured marker. Small round magnets, red, blue, green. He finds his sister's surname near the bottom. Next to it, from Monday, the rows are blank.

Mr. Hasegawa is sixties, thin, with a grey cardigan and a mask pulled down under his chin, where it stays for the whole meeting like a second, smaller chin. He bows. He offers tea. Kusakabe says no, and gets it anyway, in a paper cup, green and much too strong.

"Kusakabe-san. Thank you for coming."

"Yeah."

Mr. Hasegawa sits, opens a folder, and turns it round so they can both see it. It's the care plan. His sister on four printed pages. Her medication. Her mobility. Cognitive: variable. Somewhere in a box near the bottom, his own name, his phone number, the word Family.

"When a lead carer leaves," Mr. Hasegawa says, "the plan goes back for review. That's our policy. It protects the client. We need to sit down with the family, agree the changes, and sign. Until it's signed, I can't put new staff on it." He turns a page. "We called you on Wednesday. Twice. Mrs. Fujita tried too, I believe."

Kusakabe looks at the four printed pages.

There are about forty things he could say. That he was on leave. That he doesn't answer unknown numbers. That his work is irregular, that he's sometimes somewhere a phone doesn't reach, that last month he was in a tunnel in Gunma. Some of it's even true. All of it would take a long time and none of it would change the whiteboard.

"That's on me," he says. "I should've called back."

Mr. Hasegawa stops with his hand on the page.

He looks at Kusakabe over his glasses. It's the look of a man who has sat across this desk from a great many sons and daughters-in-law and heard all forty things, often loudly. He had his own answers lined up.

He doesn't need them. He seems briefly unsure what to do with his hands.

"Well," he says. "Yes. Thank you." He clears his throat. "Let's see what we can do."

What they can do is this. The weekend is covered: the Sunday morning is already on the board, the night carer already rostered through Sunday. Mr. Hasegawa has a woman who could start Wednesday. Possibly someone else for later. But Monday and Tuesday are gone. Those slots went to other clients while the plan sat unsigned.

"I can try to find someone," he says. "But honestly. This time of year. Before Wednesday, I'd be lying to you."

"Fine," Kusakabe says. "I'll do it."

"Both days? The nights as well?"

"Yeah."

Mr. Hasegawa looks as if he'd like to ask what Kusakabe does for a living, and whether it can spare him. He doesn't ask. Kusakabe's grateful. It's the second decent thing anyone's done for him today, and the first was a woman telling him she was a bit angry.

"Then tomorrow," Mr. Hasegawa says, "you can meet the candidates. Here. Ten o'clock. I'll need you to tell them about her."

Tell them about her, he thinks.

He signs the plan where the sticky arrows point.

Friday morning, at the agency.

There are two.

The first is a woman in her forties called Mrs. Kubo, round-faced, with a scarf in three colours and a voice that fills the little room right to the corners. She's lovely. He can see it at once. She's the kind of person who brings biscuits on the first day and remembers your birthday on the third.

She asks everything.

How long has his sister been like this? What happened? An accident, how awful, what sort of accident? Does she have children? A son, oh, and is he? Oh. I'm so sorry. And what about him, is he married, what does he do for work?

"School," he says.

"Oh, a teacher! What do you teach?"

He thinks about it.

"P E"

Mrs. Kubo beams. Mr. Hasegawa, at the end of the desk, writes something down that is probably not P E

Every answer he gives, she hands back with another question attached. She means it kindly. By the end of twenty minutes he feels like a coat that's been gone through for its pockets.

When she leaves, she squeezes his arm. "Your sister's lucky to have you."

He doesn't know what to say to that. So he says nothing. She doesn't seem to need him to.

The second candidate.

The second is Ono Haruka.

Late twenties. Black puffer jacket she keeps on. Hair pulled back hard. A plain watch with the face on the inside of her wrist, the way nurses wear them. She bows the minimum, sits, and looks at him as though he's a form she needs to fill in correctly.

"Ono," she says. "I worked two years in a care home in Adachi. Before that, hospital. Ward assistant."

"Okay."

Mr. Hasegawa slides the care plan across. She reads it. All four pages. She doesn't skim. Her eyes go to the end of each line and back. He finds he's watching her read the way Mrs. Fujita used to watch him read the notebook.

Then she looks up.

"Her pills. Whole or crushed?"

"Whole. The half one you cut."

"After Tuesday, the doctor might want them crushed. I'll ask." She turns a page. "Which hand?"

"Right. Not far. Not always."

"Transfers?"

"There's a way. Mrs. Fujita does it." He tries to show her with his arms, the practised hug, and does it badly, the way he always has.

Ono watches. "I know it," she says. "I'll do it my way. It'll be the same thing."

She doesn't ask what happened to his sister. She doesn't ask about a son. She doesn't ask what he does for a living. She doesn't smile at him once. He realises halfway through that she isn't going to, and that he doesn't want her to, and that the room has gone very easy to sit in.

There are three things he has to tell her. He's been dreading them all morning, in order.

"Some days she doesn't know who people are," he says. "She'll call you something else. A name. Doesn't matter whose. Just answer to it."

Ono nods once. No face. Not oh how sad. Just the nod you give a fact.

"She likes her tea weak. Really weak. Count to three and take the bag out. It'll look wrong. Do it anyway."

Another nod. She writes three on the back of her hand in biro.

The third one is the hardest, and it's the smallest.

"She likes the window. The chair by the window. Even when there's nothing. Even at night." He stops. "Don't move her away from it to make the room easier."

Ono looks at him for slightly longer than before.

"I don't do that," she says.

That's all. Not a promise. A statement about herself, like her height.

When she's gone, Mr. Hasegawa takes his glasses off and rubs his nose. "Mrs. Kubo is very experienced," he says carefully. "Families tend to like her."

"Ono," Kusakabe says.

Mr. Hasegawa puts his glasses back on. He doesn't argue. He gets up, goes to the whiteboard, and puts a green magnet on the Wednesday square.

Friday afternoon.

Friday he goes to the flat for Mrs. Fujita's last shift.

She's at the counter with the notebook. Not the cat one. A new one, the same kind, the same hundred-yen shop. This one has a cartoon rabbit on it. She's been copying things across. Medicines, the doctor's number, the exercises, the toilet handle that still sticks if you don't jiggle it. Every page in her neat uphill pencil.

"For the new one," she says. "Ono-san, is it?"

"Yeah."

"Good. Hasegawa said she's sensible." She closes the rabbit and puts it on top of the cat. "You keep the old one."

He takes it. It's soft at the corners. Three years of it. Ate half. Ate all. Confused p.m. Every Sunday, a few lines in his own worse handwriting.

The bills are by the kettle, the way they always are. He picks them up. The agency's envelope is on top.

He opens it. He doesn't usually open them here. He's not sure why he does today.

It's the first one at the new rate. There's the number, at the bottom, with a box round it. He does the sum this time, standing there, without the calculator. It doesn't take thirty seconds. It takes about four. It's roughly what he'd guessed, which isn't comforting. It just means the guess was right, and the guess was bad.

Fine, he thinks. Still fine. A bit less fine.

He folds it back into the envelope.

Mrs. Fujita is putting on her coat. She does it slowly, one arm, then the other, and stands in the hallway for a moment with her bag on her shoulder, looking at the lavender on the shoe cabinet.

"Thicker drinks," she says. "Don't forget. There's a powder. It's in the cupboard."

"Okay."

She goes to the window chair. She bends down and says something to his sister, low, that he doesn't hear. His sister nods, the polite nod she gives people she can't place. Mrs. Fujita squeezes her right hand and doesn't make a thing of it.

At the door she bows to him. He bows back, lower than he means to.

"Three years," she says.

"Thank you."

It comes out of him before he's decided to say it. Two words he's not good at. She looks almost as surprised as Mr. Hasegawa did.

"Answer your phone, Kusakabe-san," she says. And goes.

Monday, at his sister's flat.

Monday, half past seven in the morning, the night carer hands over to him in the doorway, and goes, and then it's just the two of them.

He calls dispatch from the kitchen while the kettle boils. The coordinator with the headset answers on the second ring. He tells her he won't be in today or tomorrow.

"Reason?"

"Family."

A pause. Keys tapping. "I'll have to put it down as unscheduled."

"Fine."

"There were two jobs for you today. They'll go to someone else."

"Okay."

He can hear her moving his name. Not up a list this time. Sideways, into some other column.

Then he does everything Mrs. Fujita does, and does it worse.

He does the transfer, the hug, from the bed to the wheelchair and the wheelchair to the window chair. She grips his collar with her right hand as if he's a stranger who might drop her. He gets her washed, with the cloth and the basin, slowly, carefully, both of them looking at the wall. He mixes the powder into her tea until it goes the texture of thin custard. She tastes it and pulls a face he hasn't seen before.

"This is wrong."

"Doctor says."

"Doctors," she says, with contempt, and drinks it.

He does the exercises from the rabbit notebook. Lift the arm. Hold. Lower. Again. She does three and then looks out of the window and won't do the fourth, and he doesn't push.

At lunchtime she looks at him for a long time and asks, quite politely, "Are you the new one?"

He thinks about it.

"Yeah," he says. "Sort of."

"You're slower than the other lady."

"Yeah."

"She had a cat," his sister says. "On a book."

Tuesday.

The days at the flat don't disappear.

That's what he notices, by Tuesday. Same grey light as the sofa, same early dark. But they don't go anywhere. They're full of small hard things. Pills at eight, at one, at seven. The toilet, which takes all of his patience and none of his dignity, and less of hers. They both get through it by not looking at each other. Lunch she won't eat. Tea she will. Each hour has a shape. Each hour needs him in it.

He's more tired than he's been all year. He doesn't once look at a clock wondering where the afternoon went.

At night he puts her to bed. Then he turns off the big light and leaves the little one, and sits in the window chair, the blue one, with a blanket from the cupboard over his knees.

The chair is too small for him. It was bought for her. His knees come up too high. His head finds the wing of it at an angle that's going to hurt tomorrow.

He doesn't draw the circle. There's nothing here that needs it. But he can feel the room the way he'd feel it if he did. The bed, three metres away, her breathing inside it. The kitchen, the fridge, the cupboard with the powder. The front door and the lavender on the cabinet beside it. Everything that matters, in one room and a hallway.

Small, he thinks. Could reach all of it with one arm, nearly.

The car park outside is orange under its one lamp. Upstairs, at four, the new dog barks twice and thinks better of it.

He sleeps in pieces.

Dawn.

He wakes because the light has changed.

Not much. The sky over the car park has gone from black to the dark grey of wet slate. The lamp is still on. His neck is wrong. His legs are in a shape legs shouldn't be. Somebody's balcony across the way has a towel on the rail, white, just starting to show.

Wednesday. He knows that before he knows anything else. Ono at eight.

He turns his head, slowly, because of the neck.

His sister is awake.

She's lying on her side in the bed, facing him, the blanket up to her chin, and she's watching him. He doesn't know how long she's been doing it. Her eyes are clear. Not the polite look. Not the stranger look, or the long, patient look she wears when she's talking to their father. It's a different one. He's not sure, for a second, that he remembers it.

"Nii-san," she says.

Her voice is rough with sleep. It's what she calls her brother. It's what she's calling him.

"You'll do your back in," she says. "In my chair."

"Probably," he says.

She keeps looking. She doesn't say anything else. She doesn't need to. He doesn't move, either. His neck is screaming. There's a kettle he could be putting on, a powder to measure, a rabbit notebook to fill in before eight.

In a minute, he thinks.

The grey comes up behind the towel on the balcony. The lamp goes thin and unnecessary. In the bed, his sister lets her eyes close, then opens them again to check he's still there.

He is. He shuts his own eyes, in her chair, by her window, and lets her watch.
