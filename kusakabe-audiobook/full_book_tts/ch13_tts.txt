Chapter thirteen. Halloween.

The job sheet says Standby.

One word, in the dispatch coordinator's small square handwriting. Under it. Shibuya area, north. Thirty-first of October. Six in the evening until crowds disperse. Kusakabe A. Panda, second year.

Every big crowd night, the school puts a few people out. Crowds make things. Drink and noise and too many bodies, a lot of feeling in a small space with nowhere to go. Most nights, nothing comes of it. You stand on a corner for six hours and go home.

Standby is his favourite kind of work. You get paid to be somewhere.

Grade one sorcerer, he thinks. Very highly trained. Paid to lean on a railing.

The others in his homeroom are on other people's lists tonight, on other corners of the city. Only Panda is on his.

Panda has read the sheet over his shoulder in the staff car yard. Panda doesn't say anything at first. Then he does a small, contained thing with his whole body, like a dog that's heard the word walk and is trying to be dignified about it.

"Halloween," Panda says.

"I know what day it is."

"In Shibuya, sensei."

"It's a job."

"It's the one night of the year," Panda says, "I can walk down the middle of the street."

Later that afternoon.

Ijichi drives them in at half past five, through traffic that thickens until it's less traffic than a car park that occasionally moves.

Ijichi has a list on his knee. Five standby points round the station, a pair at each. A number to call. He goes through it twice, in the same words both times, and Kusakabe lets him, because it's clearly helping one of them.

"It's a precaution," Ijichi says. "There's been nothing specific."

"Fine."

"Some unusual readings. Earlier in the week. Around the station. But nothing you could put on a sheet." He looks at Kusakabe in the mirror. "I'd have put it on the sheet."

"I know."

"I'm sure it's crowds."

"It's always crowds."

Ijichi lets them out on Meiji-dori, at the Harajuku end, near the Meiji-Jingumae station steps. North of everything. The far edge of the map.

Good, Kusakabe thinks. The edge of the map is where I live.

Meiji-dori, at the Harajuku end.

The street is already full.

Not full like a morning train. Full like a party nobody organised. People drift. People stop for no reason. People shout to each other across three metres of air as if it were a valley.

And everyone is someone else.

Witches. A great many witches. Nurses with fake blood down their aprons. A row of young men all dressed as the same plumber, moustaches drawn on, one of them already sitting on the kerb with his head between his knees. A man in a full shark suit, the head open at the front, his real face peering out of the teeth like a man looking out of a garage. A boy in a cardboard box painted to look like a vending machine, with a slot, which people keep putting coins into.

At least he's earning, Kusakabe thinks.

He finds a railing outside a closed shoe shop and leans on it. Sword bag over his shoulder. Dark coat. He looks, he assumes, like a tired man waiting for someone who's late.

Panda does not lean on anything. Panda walks out into the crowd like a man walking into warm sea.

It takes about four seconds.

"Oh my God."

"Is that real?"

"It's so good."

Three girls in matching devil horns surround him with their phones up. Panda poses. Panda knows how to pose. One paw on his hip, the other raised, and the girls scream, and then the plumbers want one, all of them, including the one on the kerb, who gets up for it.

"How do you see out of it?" one of the devils asks.

"It's a very good costume," Panda says.

"It talks."

"Battery," Panda says.

Kusakabe watches from the railing. Nobody asks him for a photograph. That suits him.

A woman in a witch's hat stops in front of him, though, a can of something sweet in her hand. She looks at the sword bag. She looks at his face.

"What are you meant to be?"

He thinks about it.

"A teacher," he says.

She laughs, delighted, as if it's the scariest thing she's heard all night, and goes on into the crowd repeating it. A teacher.

Not a bad costume, he thinks. Took me years.

Panda comes back after a while, slightly out of breath, with a small plastic pumpkin full of sweets that someone has given him.

"Sensei." He holds it out. "They think I'm a person in a panda."

"They think a lot of things."

"It's nice," Panda says. He says it simply, the way he says most things. "Walking about. Nobody looking twice." He thinks. "Well. Everybody looking twice. But nicely."

Kusakabe takes a sweet. It's a small orange chew shaped like a pumpkin and it tastes faintly of soap.

"Masamichi would hate this," Panda says, looking out over the street with great contentment.

At the name, the coin in Kusakabe's pocket gets a little heavier. It always does. He doesn't check it.

"Yeah," he says. "He would."

Later, at the railing.

The circle stays folded. He doesn't need it out. He can feel the street the ordinary way, the way anyone could if they were paying attention, which almost nobody here is.

Noise. A sound system somewhere playing something with a lot of bass and not much else. A police officer up on a little platform with a loudspeaker, asking people politely, over and over, to keep moving, which nobody does. The convenience store on the corner with a queue out of the door and a handwritten sign. No toilet.

And underneath it, very faint, a kind of pressure. Like the air before weather. He notices it the way you notice a fridge humming only when it stops.

Except it doesn't stop.

Crowds, he tells himself. It's always crowds.

He looks at his phone. Six fifty-one. Nothing from Ijichi.

He puts it away and watches a man dressed as a banana try to buy a crêpe with a credit card that doesn't work.

A little later.

At seven o'clock, the pressure stops being faint.

He feels it before he sees anything. A drop in his stomach, like a lift going down too fast. Panda's head comes up at the same moment. Panda is looking south, down Meiji-dori, over all the hats and horns and ears.

"Sensei."

"I know."

Then he sees it.

Far down the street, over the rooftops, something is coming down out of the sky. Not falling. Being lowered. A darkness, slow and smooth, like ink poured very carefully into water and somehow staying in one piece. It spreads as it comes. It finds the tops of the buildings. It keeps going.

It's a curtain. He's put up a few hundred himself, in car parks and stairwells and empty schools, small black lids over small bad jobs.

This one is not small.

It goes on sinking until the station is gone under it, and everything round the station. He can't see the edge of it in either direction. Only a wall of black, curved like the top of a bowl turned over the city. The lights inside it go out. Not one by one. All at once, as if someone has simply decided they were never there.

The street goes quiet for a moment, the way a classroom does when a teacher walks in.

Then a few people laugh, because it must be part of it. Some kind of show. A man in a vampire cape takes a photo.

Then the screaming starts, somewhere down near the edge of it, and comes up the street towards them like something being passed hand to hand.

A minute later.

His phone rings. Ijichi.

"Kusakabe-san..." A burst of noise. "curtain, over the station, it's..." Static. "four hundred metres, roughly, they think..." Gone. Back. "not ours. Not one of ours. Please hold your..."

The line drops.

He calls back. It rings out.

A second phone, Panda's, buzzing in Panda's paw. Panda reads it, holding it very close to his face. "Group message. 'Everyone stay where you are.'" A pause. Another buzz. "'Ignore last. Await instructions.'"

An auxiliary he doesn't know comes pushing up the pavement against the crowd, a young man in a suit with a radio clipped to his collar that's talking to itself in short bursts of nothing.

"repeat, civilians cannot..."

"who's at the east side, who's..."

"sorcerers can pass, confirm, sorcerers can pass, civilians can't..."

"a name, they're all saying a..."

The auxiliary sees the sword bag and stops. His face is very white. "Are you...?"

"Kusakabe. Grade one."

"They're saying a name," the boy says. "The people stuck inside. At the edge. They're pressed up against it and they're all saying the same thing. Over and over." He swallows. "They're saying 'Bring Gojo Satoru.'"

"They know who that is?"

"No," the boy says. "That's the thing. They don't."

Then his radio says something that might be his name, and he goes, back towards the black.

Civilians in, can't get out, Kusakabe thinks. Sorcerers can walk in. And somebody wants Gojo.

Somebody has built a very large room, and set a table, and invited one man.

He notices his palms are cold. He wipes them on his coat. It doesn't help. It never helps.

The fear is at his ankles now, like water on a basement floor. That's how it usually starts. A cold line, low down. Something you could step out of, if you went now.

Outside the station.

The crowd turns into a different thing.

Some of it runs north, past them, costumes coming apart as they go. A witch's hat in the road. A shark head, empty, rolling on its side. Some of it runs the other way, towards the curtain, because there are people inside it. Friends. A sister. A boyfriend who went to buy drinks.

Some of it just stands there and films.

Police whistles. Sirens, far off and getting closer and then, somehow, not arriving.

Kusakabe and Panda end up doing the only thing anyone has told them to do, which is stay where they are. That turns out to mean standing at the top of the station steps, keeping people from going down them all at once. It isn't sorcery. It's a job anyone with arms could do. He finds he's grateful for it.

"Don't run," he says, to a group of boys in matching zombie make-up. "Walk."

They walk. They look at Panda and walk faster.

A girl in a nurse's costume is on the steps, sitting, crying into her phone. "She's in there," she says, to nobody, to Kusakabe because he's nearest. "She went to the station to get cash. She's in there."

He doesn't know what to say. He never knows what to say.

"Stay off the steps," he says.

She looks at him as if he's said something unforgivable. Maybe he has. She gets up and goes. He doesn't watch where.

His phone buzzes. A text, not from Ijichi. From the dispatch line. All staff hold position. Do not enter. Further orders to follow.

Two minutes later, from the same number: Disregard. Await team assignment.

Nobody knows, he thinks. That's what this is. A lot of people who don't know, telling each other.

Seventeen minutes later.

At eight fourteen, the radios all say the same thing at once, for the first time all night.

Gojo is here. Gojo has gone in.

Alone.

Kusakabe hears it from the radio, and from Panda's phone, and from a second auxiliary who comes running up the hill to tell them in person, out of breath, as if it were good news.

"Alone?" Panda says.

"Into the station," the auxiliary says. "That's what they wanted. They wanted him."

There's a feeling, when you hear Gojo has turned up. He's had it before. Everybody in the job has had it. It's like standing in a hospital corridor and seeing the senior doctor walk past. The thing is still bad. The thing is now somebody else's.

He waits for the feeling.

It comes. It's thinner than usual.

He's the strongest there is, he thinks. He'll be fine. He's always fine.

Then why did they build a room for him.

He doesn't like that thought. He puts it somewhere and doesn't look at it.

Panda is looking south, at the black dome, very still, his round head tilted a little to one side.

"He'll be fine," Panda says.

"Yeah."

"He's always fine."

"Yeah," Kusakabe says. He wonders which of them that was for.

Then the orders.

The orders, when they come, come all at once, and from Ijichi, whose voice is very calm in the way it only gets when nothing is.

The curtain isn't the only one. There are others, inside it. Smaller ones, layered, like a set of boxes. Some of them let sorcerers in. Some of them don't. Somebody inside is holding them up: curse users, on the upper floors, at the exits, keeping them standing. Curses too. A lot of curses. Nobody knows how many. Gojo is inside the station and whatever is happening to him is happening where nobody can see.

The teams are to go in and take down the curtains. Find whoever is holding them. Open the way.

"Kusakabe-san, you and Panda-kun from Meiji-Jingumae. Down into the subway. Follow the line south, towards Shibuya. Clear what you find. There'll be..." Static. "at the..." Static. "on the radio as you go."

"Ijichi."

"Yes."

"How many are in there?"

A pause. A long one, for Ijichi.

"I don't know," Ijichi says. "I'm sorry. Nobody does."

The line holds, for once. Behind Ijichi, someone is shouting a set of coordinates and someone else is shouting that they're wrong.

"Okay," Kusakabe says, and hangs up.

He looks at the top of the steps going down into the station. The tiled walls, the lit sign, the escalator still running for nobody.

I could be on a railing, he thinks. I was on a railing. I was good at it.

The fear is at his knees.

"Sensei?" Panda says.

He unzips the sword bag. "Down," he says.

Down in the station.

The station is lit and empty and wrong.

All the lights are on. The ticket gates are open, the little barriers folded back as if the whole system has given up asking for money. A newsagent's shutter is half down, magazines still on the rack under it.

They go down to the platforms. Nobody. A train standing with its doors open, all its lights on, a paper bag on one of the seats. A screen telling them, in cheerful letters, that the next service is delayed.

Panda jumps down onto the track bed first and offers a paw. Kusakabe climbs down by himself, which takes longer, and Panda waits without comment.

Then they walk south. Into the tunnel. The rails gleam a little way ahead in the work lights, and then don't.

It's a long walk. Most of a kilometre. His shoes on the gravel. Panda's feet, softer. The radio an auxiliary clipped to Panda's collar, saying things in pieces, none of them about here.

"Dogenzaka side..."

"can't get through, it's keyed against..."

"anyone on the Fukutoshin..."

"That's us," Panda says, and presses the button, and says, "Panda. We're on the Fukutoshin. In the tunnel." The radio doesn't answer. Panda waits, and then lets go of the button. "They'll have heard."

"Sure."

Somewhere ahead, the tunnel is darker than a tunnel should be. A flat black across the whole round mouth of it, from the track bed to the roof. The edge of the big curtain, reaching down through the ground the way roots do.

They stop in front of it.

Sorcerers can pass, he thinks. That's what the boy said.

He puts his hand out. The black is cool and gives like the skin on cold milk. Then his hand is through, and his arm, and he follows it.

On the other side, the air is thicker. Like a room where too many people have been breathing for too long. And there are sounds now, ahead, that aren't the radio.

He lets the circle open.

Past the curtain.

They come along the roof of the tunnel first.

Five. Six. Low things, flat, many-legged, moving fast and close together. Grade threes, maybe fours. Small. A lot of them. His circle feels the first one cross the line before his eyes find it, and his hands answer. The sword is out and back, and the thing comes down on the rail in two parts and goes to smoke.

Then the others are in the circle all at once, and there's nothing to think about. That's the one mercy of it. Edge, weight, turn. Something wet across his cheek. Something grabbing at his coat and letting go because it has no hand any more. Behind him, a sound like a sack of rice hitting a wall, and then another. He knows without looking that it's Panda, and that Panda is fine, and that the things Panda is hitting are not.

It takes about forty seconds.

When it's done he's breathing hard and there's a nick on the back of his left hand that he didn't feel arrive. The tunnel smells of burnt hair.

"Sensei. You okay?"

"Fine."

He wipes his cheek with his sleeve. His hand is shaking. Only a little. Only the left one.

Small ones, he thinks. That's the bad news. Small ones means there's a reason they were here. Small ones run in front of things.

The fear is at his waist. He can feel it there, cold and level, and he can feel that it isn't going to go back down.

Further on.

There's a service door in the tunnel wall, half open, light behind it. Beyond it, a concrete passage. The passage is not empty.

Civilians. Twenty, thirty, sitting against the wall, some still in costume. A man in a gorilla suit with the head off, holding it in his lap. A girl dressed as a cartoon cat, with a cut on her forehead. A woman in office clothes who was just going home.

They look up at Kusakabe. Then they look at Panda.

Nobody says is that real this time.

"Are you police?" the woman in office clothes says.

"Something like that."

"We came down from the street when it went dark. We thought we could walk out along the tracks." She points back at the tunnel. "But there's a..." She doesn't know the word. Nobody does. "It's black. Down there, and up on the stairs. You can't go through it."

"I know."

"What is it?"

He thinks about what he could say. It's a barrier made of cursed energy, keyed to keep you in. Somebody upstairs is holding it up. They're doing it to catch a man who's stronger than anyone alive, and nobody can tell me what's happened to him.

"We're dealing with it," he says.

It's a terrible answer. It's the only one he's got. She nods as if it's a good one, because she needs it to be.

Panda crouches by the girl with the cut. He gives her the plastic pumpkin of sweets. She holds it in both hands and doesn't eat any.

The stairs.

The stairs at the end of the passage go up into black. Not the big curtain. A smaller one, cut across the stairwell at the half landing, flat and dull as a blackboard.

Kusakabe puts his hand on it.

It doesn't give.

He pushes. It's like pushing a wall. Not hard, exactly, more like the thing simply has no interest in him at all. Keyed against. That's what the radio meant. This one's not for keeping people in. This one's for keeping people like him out.

"Someone's holding it," Panda says.

"Yeah."

"Up there."

"Yeah."

He makes himself feel past it, with the circle, as far as it'll go, which is not very far. One arm and one sword. That's all it's ever been.

But at the very edge, just at the turn of the stair, on the other side of the black: something. A person. Cursed energy, thin and steady, like a tap left running.

Panda goes back along the passage and comes back with a fire extinguisher off the wall.

"Panda."

"It's not a sorcerer," Panda says. "It's an extinguisher."

He throws it. It doesn't go through. It hits the curtain and stops and drops, the way a ball drops off a net. But on the other side, at the edge of his circle, the person flinches. Moves. Comes down a step to look.

And the curtain, for a second, thins.

Kusakabe goes through it the way you go through a door that's about to close. He doesn't decide to. He's just on the other side.

A young man. Twenties. A hooded jacket with a skull on it, which might be a costume and might not. A strip of paper in his hand, covered in writing, that's what the curtain's coming from. He sees Kusakabe and his mouth opens.

The sword goes once. Not through the man. Through the paper, and the two fingers holding it.

The man screams. The curtain comes apart behind them, like smoke in a draught.

Then Panda is there, and the man is on the floor, and not moving, and breathing. Kusakabe checks that. He checks it twice.

He stands on the stairs with two fingers on the step by his shoe and the man's blood on his blade. Not a curse's. A person's.

He'd have killed every one of them down there, he thinks. He'd have stood here and held the door shut until whatever's up there came down.

It doesn't help much. He wipes the blade on his coat.

Below them, the civilians are getting up. The woman in office clothes is at the bottom of the stairs, looking up at him.

"Wait," he says. "Then come up behind us. Not before."

She doesn't say thank you. She doesn't need to. She nods.

The fear is at his chest. It's in his breathing now. Every breath, he can feel it there, sitting on the top of the lungs.

At the top of the stairs.

They go up first. The stairs come out in the basement of a shopping building, shutters down, mannequins in a window in their Halloween capes, and an escalator up to the street, still running.

The exit at the top is held. He feels them before he sees them, from the bottom of the escalator: three, four, crowded into the light at the head of it like something waiting for a bus. Bigger than the ones in the tunnel. One of them much bigger.

The radio on Panda's collar says, "upper levels, all exits, repeat, curses and users on all..." and then nothing.

"Thanks," Panda tells it.

He goes back and tells the civilians to sit on the stairs. They sit. It's strange how often people do what you tell them when you have a sword and they don't.

Then he goes up, and Panda goes up beside him.

He doesn't remember much of it after. The big one's weight. The way it came down the escalator steps and the steps kept moving under it, so that for a moment it was walking and staying in the same place, like a man on a treadmill. The circle snapping round him tight as a belt. His blade going in somewhere soft and catching and having to be pulled. Panda's shoulder hitting the thing from the side. A noise from his own mouth that he doesn't recognise. Then the cold, hard stop of it, and the grey, and the escalator still running, carrying a little drift of ash up to the top and tipping it off.

He's on one knee at the top. He doesn't remember kneeling.

"Sensei."

"Yeah."

"Sensei. You're bleeding."

He looks. There's a line across his forearm, through the coat, not deep. It's the same arm as last time. Consistent, he thinks. At least I'm consistent.

"It's nothing."

Panda looks at it, then at his face, for a long moment. Then Panda nods, and holds out a paw.

Kusakabe looks at it. Then he gets up by himself, one hand on the rail, which takes longer. Panda lowers the paw and waits, and doesn't say anything about it.

At street level.

They wave the civilians up. They go out past the two sorcerers in a long, quick, frightened line. The gorilla holding his head. The cat girl holding her pumpkin. The woman in office clothes holding the hand of someone she didn't know an hour ago. Out into the street, towards the auxiliary he can see waving a torch at the next corner. Still inside. Everyone is still inside.

Kusakabe and Panda come out last.

They're inside now. Properly inside. On the street, in the middle of it all, somewhere north of the station.

The noise is the first thing. Far off, but not far enough. Sirens with nowhere to go. Something like thunder, under the ground, that he feels in his feet. A long scream that stops. Then the smell. Smoke, and something sweeter under it that he doesn't want to name.

The street is full of the leftovers of a party. Cans. A broken plastic sword. A food stall on its side, its paper lanterns still lit. Somewhere, a sound system is still playing the same bass line, to nobody.

His phone has no signal. Panda's radio hisses and says nothing.

Nobody knows, he thinks. And now we're in it, and we still don't.

The fear is at his throat. He swallows, and it's still there. He thinks if he opens his mouth it'll come out, and so he doesn't open his mouth. That's all right. He's never been much of a talker.

Panda stands beside him, enormous and calm and very still, fur spiked with something dark across one shoulder. He isn't looking at the street. He's looking up.

Kusakabe looks up too.

There's no sky. There are no streetlights above the second floor. The tops of the buildings go up into nothing and stop. No glow of the city. No aircraft. No moon. Just the curtain, from one side of the world to the other, flat and close and perfectly black.

Like a sky that someone has taken all the stars out of. Carefully. One by one. And not told anyone.
