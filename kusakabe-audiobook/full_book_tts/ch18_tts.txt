Chapter Eighteen. Uzumaki.

The second can goes in his coat pocket in the end.

He can't drink it. He can't give it to anyone. He can't put it back in the machine, because machines don't work that way, and neither does anything else tonight. So it goes in the left pocket, where it sits against his ribs, the same temperature as he is, like something he's been carrying for years.

The side street has gone quiet in the way it does between things. The machine hums in its doorway. Somewhere up the hill a van door slides shut. The curtain overhead, close and black.

It's after two. The first of November. Nobody has said so. He worked it out from his watch, which came through the building. That's more than the building did.

Panda finds him there, standing by the machine.

"Sensei."

"Yeah."

"Yuji's still going." Panda's head is turned south, towards the station, his torn ear flat. "Him and the big one from Kyoto. Against the patchwork one. The one that changes people."

Kusakabe knows which one. A school, an assembly hall, a curse that changes people's shapes. A sealed file with Nanami's name on it. The thing that killed Nanami tonight, if the auxiliary had it right. He heard that an hour ago, in pieces, and he hasn't put the pieces together yet. He isn't going to do it here.

"Radio says anyone who can," Panda says.

Kusakabe looks up the street, towards the vans and the stretchers. Then at Panda, who isn't asking him anything. Who's just telling him where he's going, in case he wants to know.

Somebody should go back and carry, he thinks.

Somebody is. The medic has hands enough. She said so, more or less, when she sent him down here.

"Fine," he says.

On the way south. They meet her two streets on.

She comes round a corner at a run, a sword in a black scabbard held tight against her hip, and nearly goes into Panda. Her thumb pushes the guard up an inch before she sees who it is. Then it slides back down.

"Panda-kun." Her whole face lets go. Then she sees Kusakabe, and it pulls itself straight again, the way a student sits up when a teacher walks in. Which one has. Sort of.

She's young. Blue hair to her shoulders, the Kyoto uniform, dust down one side of it as if she's been knocked over and got up and not had time to brush off.

"You're Kusakabe-san," she says. "From Tokyo. New Shadow Style."

He has no idea how she knows that. Then he looks at her hands.

Left hand on the scabbard just below the guard. Thumb ready. Right hand loose, a little above the hilt, not touching it. Weight forward on the balls of the feet. It's how they teach it. Exactly how they teach it. A different dojo, probably, a different teacher, ten or fifteen years on. The same thumb.

"Miwa," Panda says. "Kasumi Miwa. Kyoto, second year. She's nice."

"I'm looking for Todo," she says. "And Itadori-kun. Somebody said they were fighting the special grade. The patchwork one. Down there." She points south with her chin, because her hands are busy.

"Same," says Panda.

She falls in on Panda's other side, and the three of them walk.

She's shaking. Not much. A fine steady shake in the hands, the kind you only see because the scabbard tip moves. She sees him see it, and grips harder to make it stop. It doesn't stop. It just moves up into her wrists.

He knows that one. He's done that one for years.

"I should say," she says, after a bit, to the road. "I'm not very strong. Just so you know. Before anything. I'm mostly good at holding the line."

Nobody answers. She seems to need to fill the space, so she does.

"I only do this for the money, really. That sounds bad. It's just, there's my brothers, and..." She stops. "Sorry. That's not relevant."

Something in him sits up at that, and looks at her, and then sits back down.

It's relevant, he thinks. He doesn't say it.

"Do you think they're all right?" she says. "Itadori-kun and Todo?"

"Don't know."

She nods, as if that was a proper answer. It's the only one he's got.

South of the station. The ground opens out where a block of buildings used to be.

Fires here and there, low. Pale concrete. A crater with soft edges, as if something had been pressed into the city with a thumb. It's lighter here than it should be under the curtain, because so much is burning.

He sees the boy first.

Pink hair, grey with dust. Kneeling, his back to them, head down, shoulders going up and down. That's all. No face. Kusakabe has gone a whole night, a whole year, without seeing that face, and it seems he'll go a bit longer. Something in him is grateful for that. Something in him isn't.

Against a heap of rubble to the left, a very big young man is sitting with his legs out in front of him. One of his arms ends at the wrist in a wad of red cloth. He's awake. He's grinning, which seems wrong, and also seems to be the kind of thing he'd do.

And in the middle of the open ground, a man in a monk's robes is bending over something on the ground.

The something is grey. Patched. It's trying to crawl away, and it isn't getting very far. Whatever it was earlier tonight, it has been beaten down to something about the size of a dog, dragging itself through the dust on hands that keep changing shape.

The man straightens up.

Kusakabe sees his face, and his stomach goes cold and flat.

He knows the face. Not from life. From a file. From a photograph that was pinned up in the dispatch office for a whole year. The corners curled and the colour went orange, and nobody looked at it because everybody already knew. Suguru Geto. Special grade curse user. The man who declared war on everyone, and then sent the Night Parade to collect.

Dead. Last Christmas Eve. Everybody knows that too. Gojo finished it himself.

Same long black hair, tied up. Same heavy earrings. The same robes, the dull gold and black of them. A pleasant face. Calm and pleasant, like a man who's come to talk to you about your insurance.

And across the forehead, from one side to the other, a line of black stitches. Neat. Small. Like a seam on a coat.

Dead men don't have stitches, he thinks. Dead men don't need them.

Panda has gone completely still beside him.

"That's Geto," Panda says, very low. "And Geto's dead."

"Yeah."

That's all either of them can manage. It's like looking at a house you know has been empty for a year and seeing a light on upstairs. Not frightening, at first. Just wrong. Wrong in a way that gets into the back of your knees.

The man looks down at the crawling thing with something like fondness.

"You did well," he says. "You really did."

The voice fits the face exactly. Kusakabe has never heard it before, and he'd know it anywhere, and that's worse.

The man holds out his hand. The grey thing on the ground stops crawling. It folds. It goes in on itself, over and over, smaller and darker, until it's a ball about the size of a plum, sitting in his palm.

He puts it in his mouth and swallows.

Kusakabe's throat moves with it, the way your throat moves when you watch somebody take a pill. He wishes it hadn't.

The boy on his knees doesn't look up.

Out in the open ground. The man turns. He isn't looking at them, particularly. He's looking at the boy.

He lifts his hand again, palm out, and the air starts to turn.

"Maximum," he says. Pleasantly. As if reading out the next stop on a train. "Uzumaki."

It comes out of nothing, in front of his hand. Everything he just swallowed, and everything he's ever swallowed before that, curses folded into curses folded into curses, pulled in to one point and wound tight. Black, and every dark colour there is, turning on itself. Tighter. Tighter. The way a wet towel tightens when you wring it, until you think it'll tear.

Kusakabe feels it before he's properly seen it.

The circle comes up on its own, and the whole edge of it is leaning. Grit drifts sideways across the ground. The flames on the low fires bend towards it like a crowd bending towards something that's fallen in the road. His coat tugs at him, gently, the way a child tugs. The air itself twists, and there's a sound in his ears that's mostly pressure, a high thin note, like a finger on the rim of a glass, getting louder.

He knows what it's for. It's for the boy on his knees.

And between the boy and the man, there's Miwa.

He didn't see her go. She's just there, twenty metres ahead of him, in front of the kneeling boy, her sword out. She's in the stance. Her own circle is up, small and round and properly drawn, a good one. Somebody taught her well.

And she's stopped.

Not waiting. Stopped. Knees locked straight. Shoulders up round her ears. Her face is white, and her eyes are fixed on the thing turning in the man's hand, and she isn't going to move out of the way. She can't. He can see it from here. He can see it in her knees.

Her circle is going to be a sheet of paper held up in front of that.

One tenth of a second.

That's what there is. Not a moment. Less than a moment. Not enough room for a thought. Not enough room for a sentence. Not, I heard they've already got people down there. Not, Traffic. Not, in a bit. Nothing he's ever said to get out of anything would fit in the gap.

So nothing does.

He's there.

He doesn't remember the twenty metres. He's just there, in front of her, between her and the turning dark, and the circle is around both of them, and the blade is out.

The spiral comes into the circle.

And his body answers it the way it's answered everything for twenty years. Not a decision. A habit. The same draw he's done ten thousand mornings, alone, in a cold room, until his shoulders ached and then until they didn't. The same small step. The same angle. Every bit of it was put there a long time ago, one morning at a time, by a man who wasn't thinking about tonight at all.

He doesn't try to cut it. You don't cut that. Nobody could.

He meets it at the edge of the circle, at an angle, with the flat of everything he's got, and he turns it.

Like a stone in a fast river. The stone doesn't stop the river. The stone doesn't fight it. The stone just stays exactly where it is, and is exactly the shape it is, and the river, all that weight of it, finds that it would rather go round.

The spiral goes round.

It goes past his shoulder with a scream like metal being torn, past Miwa's head, close enough to lift her hair, and on into the dark behind them. Something there that was part of a building isn't any more. The sound of it comes back to him a long moment later, a deep soft crump, and then a rain of small stones.

Then it's quiet.

He's still standing.

His arms are shaking. Not his hands. All of it, from the shoulders down, the way a rope shakes after a weight has come off it. His palms are buzzing like a struck bell. He can't feel his fingers. He looks down and they're still closed round the hilt, both hands. The blade is still in one piece, which he finds hard to believe. He can't tell from feeling alone whether he's holding it or it's holding him.

Behind him, Miwa sits down in the dust. Not falls. Sits. All at once, like a coat sliding off a hook.

He doesn't turn round. He can feel her there. Inside the circle. Breathing.

A moment later. The man in the robes is looking at him.

Properly. Not through him. Not past him at the thing behind. At him, with real attention, his head on one side.

It's the first time tonight anything that size has really looked at him. He'd thought being looked through was the worst of it. He was wrong. This is worse. This is being noticed.

"Simple Domain," the man says.

He sounds pleased. Genuinely pleased. Like a man who's gone back to a street he knew and found a shop still open that he'd been sure had closed years ago.

"And New Shadow Style, at that. Well."

Kusakabe says nothing. His arms are shaking too hard to say anything with.

"It's rare," the man says. "Somebody of your generation, using real sorcery. Proper sorcery. The old kind." The stitched forehead creases, very slightly, like a man reading the small print. "I'm impressed. I mean that."

Impressed, Kusakabe thinks. A dead man is impressed with me. Put it on the file. Next to Froze.

The man looks at him a moment longer, as though he might remember the face. Then he doesn't seem to need to. He's already looking somewhere else.

Afterwards, in the quiet. Kusakabe stands there, and the fear arrives.

Late. All at once. The way the pain from a stubbed toe arrives a full second after the noise, and then makes up for lost time.

His legs go loose at the knee. His heart is going so hard he can feel it in his teeth. His mouth is full of a taste like pennies. He's standing in the open, in front of a dead man with a sewn-up head, with a girl from Kyoto he met twenty minutes ago sitting in the dust at his heels.

What am I doing here, he thinks.

No. That's not the question. He knows what he's doing here. Panda said anyone who can, and he said fine.

Why didn't I avoid that, he thinks.

That's the one.

Because he would have. Any other night of his life. He's the best there is at it. He'd have seen the spiral and his feet would have found the way out before his head had finished reading the situation. He'd have been behind a wall. He'd have been at the edge of things, looking at the ground, being nobody in particular. He's been doing it all night. He was doing it at the top of a staircase five hours ago, in his ordinary voice.

So where was that?

He goes looking for it. For the moment where he chose. The way you look for a step on a staircase you don't remember climbing.

The spiral. The girl's knees. Then the turn.

There's nothing in between. Not a gap. Not a door. Nothing he could have stood in and thought it over.

It didn't come to the part of him that decides things. The part that says Traffic, and turns the radio down, and takes the long way round. It never got that far.

She was already inside, he thinks. Inside the circle.

That's as far as he gets with it. It doesn't turn into anything. It just sits there, a small hard thing he hasn't got anywhere to put.

He puts it down for now. Somewhere he'll find it.

Seconds later. Someone else arrives, fast, from the side.

A young man. Pale, thin, his hair tied up in two short bunches, a dark band of something across the bridge of his nose like a stripe of paint. He's shouting before he's even stopped moving. Something about brothers. Something about you, at the man in the robes, in a voice that's been torn open.

There's blood in the air around him. Not spilled. Moving. Drawn out of his hands in long lines, hard and straight as wire, bending where he tells them to.

Kusakabe has seen blood used like that once before. A boy from a minor branch of the Kamo, nineteen, pleased with himself, in a railway tunnel. This is the same thing done by someone who means it with every part of him.

The man in the robes laughs, and turns to meet him, and the two of them go at each other in the middle of the open ground.

Kusakabe doesn't follow it. He couldn't, and it isn't his. Lines of red flicking across the edge of his circle. A turning darkness. The ground jumping once under his feet.

He reaches back without looking and gets a fistful of Miwa's collar and pulls her up and behind him. She comes. She's light. She doesn't argue. Panda is on her other side already, big and grey and solid, his shoulder touching hers.

"Back," Kusakabe says.

They go back. A step. Two.

He means to keep going. He means it quite strongly.

Still in the open ground. The cold comes first.

He thinks it's his hands. They've been numb since the spiral, so he doesn't trust them. Then he breathes out, and the breath goes white.

He looks down. Frost on the rubble. Spreading. Fine and fast and very pretty, white feathers growing out across the grey stone in every direction, the way frost grows on a car windscreen in January, except you can see it happen.

There's someone standing near the man in the robes who wasn't there before.

That's the part that gets him. It didn't cross his circle. It didn't come from anywhere. It was simply there, the way a word is suddenly on a page you've already read.

White hair, cut short. A pale face. Pale, quiet clothes. He can't tell how old. He can't tell if it's a man or a woman. After a moment he stops trying. It doesn't matter. The white-haired one is looking at the three of them now, without hurry, the way you'd look at a mess on a table that someone else ought to have wiped up.

The white-haired one says something to the man in the robes. Quiet. Not to them. He doesn't catch it.

Then the cold arrives properly.

It comes up from the ground. His feet first. One moment they're cold, and the next he can't feel them, and when he looks down there's ice up to his ankles, clear and thick, with dust caught in it.

He tries the circle. The cold crosses it everywhere at once, the way the air does. There's nothing to meet. Nothing to turn. You can't move a stone out of the way of the weather.

It doesn't climb so much as close, like a hand. He feels Panda go stiff beside Miwa, mid-word, a sound starting and not finishing. He feels Miwa's hand close on the back of his coat and stay closed.

His breath stops being white, because it's stopped going anywhere.

Save a kid, he thinks, with the last of his face. Get turned into an ice sculpture. Very dignified.

Then everything is white.

Inside the ice. It's not dark, at least. He's had enough dark for one night.

It's white. A cloudy, bluish white, with light coming through it from somewhere, and shapes on the other side that might be fire, or might be people, or might be nothing at all.

He can't move.

That's fine. He's had practice. He stood still for a king, earlier, because a king told him to. He lay still under a building because he didn't want to be found. Now somebody else has done the holding for him.

Third time tonight, he thinks. At least this one isn't my idea.

He can't feel his hands. They had a head start.

Sound comes through the ice very slow and very far off, like sound through a wall in a hotel. A crack. A rush of something. The man's voice, still pleasant, saying something long. The other one, the young one, shouting. Then not shouting. Then a long low noise from somewhere a long way away, as if the whole city has let out a breath.

He thinks about Miwa's hand on the back of his coat. He can't feel it. He decides it's still there.

He thinks, quite calmly, that if this goes on much longer he'll be very cold, and then he'll stop minding, and that'll be that. He thinks it the way he'd note the forecast.

Then he thinks about the lavender by her door. It'll want replacing in a fortnight. Nobody else knows that. It isn't written down anywhere.

He doesn't like that thought. He keeps it anyway. It's something to hold.

Later. He can't tell how much later. He hears the engine before anything else.

A deep, rough, rising roar, coming up the street. He thinks it's a motorbike. A big one. Somebody riding a motorbike into the middle of this, which seems like the act of an idiot, or someone who's never been frightened of anything.

It stops. It wasn't a motorbike. He doesn't know what it was.

Then a voice. A woman's voice, loud and warm and completely relaxed, as if she's walked into a bar where she knows everybody.

"So," she says, to someone. "What kind of woman is your type?"

He can't see who she's asking. He doesn't think they answer.

Who asks that, he thinks. Here. Now.

There's only one person he's ever heard of who would. A special grade who never comes to meetings. Who's never on any dispatch board. Who's been abroad for years, doing whatever she likes, and answers to nobody. People at school say her name the way they talk about the weather in another country.

Yuki Tsukumo, he thinks. The only sorcerer in Japan who's better at not turning up than I am.

And she's turned up.

Then a lot of noise he can't make into anything. Then quiet.

Then a knock, right in front of his face. A sharp one. The white in front of him goes grey, then cracks across in a long line like a frozen pond. Then it's falling away in plates, and there's air, cold and full of smoke, and light.

A woman is standing in front of him. Very tall. Long fair hair. A jacket. A grin like she's just been told a good joke and is saving it for later.

"Morning," she says. "Hold still a sec."

He would laugh, if his face worked.

"Been doing that," he says. His voice comes out cracked down the middle.

She does laugh. Loud, with her head back. Then she puts the flat of her hand on the ice round his chest and it breaks, all of it, at once. He falls forward out of it onto his hands and knees on the rubble.

Dawn.

He doesn't know when the curtain came down. Some time while he was inside the ice. When he gets his head up, there's sky above Shibuya again. A real sky, with nothing over it.

It's grey in the west, still. In the east it's going pale. Then a thin wash of yellow along the broken edges of what's left of the buildings. Smoke going up straight into it, in a lot of thin lines, because there's no wind.

He's sitting on a slab with someone's blanket round his shoulders. He doesn't know whose. It smells of engine oil.

Thawing is worse than freezing. Nobody tells you that. Freezing just takes things away. Thawing gives them back, all of them, at once, with interest. His feet burn. His knees ache in the bone. Water runs out of his hair and down the back of his collar. The pins and needles in his hands have got so bad they've gone all the way round to feeling like fire.

Panda is a few metres off, shaking himself like a dog out of a river, spraying meltwater in a wide ring. He stops. Shakes again. Looks at Kusakabe.

"Cold," Panda says.

"Yeah."

"You okay, sensei?"

"Ask me later."

Panda nods, as if that's fair, and sits down very heavily on the rubble beside him, and steams gently in the first of the sun.

Miwa is on his other side. Someone has given her a blanket too. She's holding a cup of something hot in both hands and not drinking it. Her knees are pulled up. Her sword is across them, sheathed. Her eyes keep going back to the place where she stood.

"Kusakabe-san," she says. "Thank you. I couldn't, I just, I couldn't make myself..."

She doesn't finish. Her face goes tight.

He thinks about what to say. There are a lot of things. Most of them are long.

"Keep your knees soft," he says.

She looks at him.

"When it's big. They lock. Keep them soft. Then you can move."

She looks down at her knees. Then back at him. Then she nods, once, very seriously, as though he's given her something she's going to put somewhere safe.

That's fine. That's a good one. One sentence. Two, if you're counting.

Yuki Tsukumo comes over the rubble towards them, hands in her jacket pockets, picking her way along as if she's walking on a beach. She stops in front of him and looks down, interested, the way someone looks at a dog that's done something clever.

"You're the sword guy," she says. "With the circle. They told me."

He doesn't ask who.

"So," she says. "What kind of woman is your type?"

He considers it. He's very tired.

"One who lets me sleep."

She laughs again, the big laugh, and claps him once on the shoulder hard enough to hurt. Then she goes off across the rubble towards the pink-haired boy, who is sitting now, somewhere off to the left, with his head in his hands. Kusakabe doesn't look over. Not yet.

He puts his hand in his coat pocket.

The can is still there. Frozen solid. The side of it has split open, and there's a fat brown ridge of coffee ice bulging out of the split, like toothpaste out of a tube someone stepped on. He takes it out and looks at it. Then he puts it down on the slab next to him, very carefully, standing up. Somebody might want it. Nobody is going to want it.

The sun clears the top of something broken. Light comes across the ruins, low and gold, and lands on him.

His teeth are chattering. He can't make them stop. He doesn't try.

He holds his hands out in front of him, in the light, and looks at them.

Red, and grey with dust, and shaking. A blister coming up across one palm where the hilt was. The black strip of cape still round the knuckles of the left, wet through, three times round and a tuck.

He turns them over. Turns them back.

He looks at them for a long time, the way you'd look at a pair of hands on somebody else. Somebody he doesn't know very well. Somebody braver.
