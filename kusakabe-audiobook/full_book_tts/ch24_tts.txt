Chapter twenty-four. December Twenty-Third.

The order is read out at nine on Sunday morning, in the big room on the ground floor of the main building, by a man Kusakabe has never seen before.

The man has a sheet of paper and a voice that has been told to stay level. He keeps it level. There are about thirty people in the room, on folding chairs and along the walls. Nobody takes their coat off.

Gojo isn't there. Somebody says he's resting. Somebody else says that means eating.

The man reads it out the way you'd read the terms on the back of a ticket.

Tomorrow, at the agreed time, at the agreed place, Gojo Satoru meets the King of Curses. Alone. Everybody else waits at a distance that has been worked out by people with instruments. If Gojo wins, that's the end of it.

If he doesn't, the next one goes in. Then the next. In an order. The man reads the order. It isn't long.

Kusakabe's name is on it. Not near the top. Not near the bottom. Somewhere in the part of the list where you'd only get to it if things had gone very badly for everyone above.

Middle of the table, he thinks. Story of my life.

He looks at the backs of heads. Okkotsu is on the list, high up. The boy is on it. Other names he knows from corridors, and a few he only knows from the colony sheet. Most of them are younger than his coat.

The boy is three rows in front, sitting very straight, the way you'd sit in an exam. When his name is read out he nods, once, to nobody. As if somebody had asked him whether he was free on Monday.

Okabe is by the door with a clipboard, ticking people off as they came in. He hasn't got a cardigan on. Kusakabe notices that, and wishes he hadn't.

Nobody asks a question. The man folds his paper. People stand up, and the chairs scrape, and the room goes back to being a room.

He goes out to the station.

It's Sunday.

His sister's flat, a little before noon.

Ono opens the door before he's finished ringing.

"You're early," she says.

"Yeah."

She doesn't ask. She hands him the rabbit notebook and puts her coat on while he reads it. Sunday, slept OK, ate half, asked what day, told her Sunday, she said good. No full stops. There never are. Underneath, in the same small upright hand: Brother came. Already written. She's started putting it in before he arrives.

He doesn't say anything about that.

"Wednesday?" Ono says, at the door.

He looks at the notebook. At the line she's going to write on Wednesday, before he gets there.

"Wednesday," he says.

She nods and writes it on the back of her hand, the way she wrote three once, a year ago, about the tea. Then she goes, with a word to his sister on the way past and none to him, and the door closes quietly behind her.

In the front room.

His sister is at the window.

The blue chair. The car park. Other people's balconies. On the second floor across the way somebody has put up a string of Christmas lights round the rail, the kind that run through all their colours and start again. In daylight you can barely see them. They're going anyway.

"Is it you?" she says, without turning round.

"It's me."

"Atsuya." She says it as if she's checking it against something. Then she turns her head, and looks at him, and it fits. "Ono said it was you. At the door."

"It was."

"Ono says everything first." She doesn't sound as if she minds.

He takes his coat off. He puts the bag on the kitchen counter. Bread, the small tin of thickening powder, a bag of the soft rice crackers she can manage. And a box he bought at the chemist's by the station, without stopping to think about it, because it was by the till.

He opens it in the hall.

The diffuser on the shoe cabinet is about two-thirds gone. The sticks are dark at the tips. He's been changing it every two months for years, and this one has three weeks left in it, maybe four.

He changes it anyway. Takes the old one to the kitchen. Puts the new one where the old one was, and turns the sticks over so the dry ends go in, and stands back.

It's very strong for a minute. Lavender, cheap and loud, the whole hall full of it.

"That's new," she says, from the window.

"Yeah."

"It's not time."

"It was going off."

She sniffs, thinking about it. "It smells like a hotel."

"It'll calm down."

"Everything does," she says, and goes back to the balcony with the lights.

Early afternoon.

He makes the tea.

The bag in, and out again before it can do anything. Then the powder, stirred until the tea gives up and goes thick. It looks like nothing at all.

He holds the cup for her. She takes three sips and says it's too strong. He says sorry. It's the most reliable conversation he has.

Then he crushes the pills on the saucer with the back of the spoon and stirs them into a spoonful of yoghurt, and she eats it, and makes the face.

He does the rest of the jobs. There aren't many left that Ono hasn't done. He wipes down the arms of the chair. He finds the spare batteries in the drawer and changes the ones in the wall clock, which was losing a minute a day. Nobody had noticed. He did.

Then there's nothing to do, and he sits down in the other chair, and they look out of the window.

The lights on the balcony go red, then green, then a blue that's almost white, then red again.

"They leave those on all night," she says.

"Do they."

"All night. Every night. Since the first." She sounds offended on behalf of the electricity. "Who's that for? Nobody's looking at three in the morning."

You are, he thinks. He doesn't say it.

After a while she says, "My hair's a mess."

It isn't, much. Ono will have done it this morning. But he gets the brush from the bathroom, the one with the soft bristles, and stands behind the chair.

He's not good at it. He's never been good at it. Her hair is fine and grey at the front now, and longer than it used to be, and he goes slowly, from the ends upwards, the way the nurse showed him once and he's never forgotten.

"You pull," she says.

"Sorry."

"You always pulled."

He doesn't know if that's true. He doesn't ask. He goes slower.

She closes her eyes. Her head tips back a little against his hands. Outside, the light goes down another notch, and the lights on the balcony start to mean something.

He keeps brushing long after it's done.

At about five she says, "Are you going somewhere?"

He stops the brush.

"Work," he says. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow's Christmas Eve."

"Yeah."

She thinks about that. He watches her think about it in the window, her reflection over the car park, the lights going through her shoulder.

"Somewhere cold?"

"Probably."

"Wrap up, then."

"Okay."

That's all. She doesn't ask what the work is. She never has, not once, in all the years, even on the clear days. He used to think it was the illness. He thinks now it might just be her.

He puts the brush down on the windowsill.

He doesn't say anything else. There's nothing to say that she'd keep. Anything he told her tonight, she'd have to be told again on Wednesday, and again the Sunday after, new each time. That's no kind of thing to give someone.

So he says what he'd say on any Sunday.

He tells her the man downstairs has painted his door. He tells her the upstairs dog has gone quiet, and she says "Good," with real feeling. He tells her it's going to be cold this week.

And then, because the window's there, and the car park, and the dark coming down, he says, "When it's warm. The sea."

She looks at him.

"What sea?"

"You wanted to go. You said. I could push you along the front."

She thinks about it. He can see her looking for it, somewhere inside, and not finding it, and deciding it doesn't matter.

"There's always too much sand," she says.

"Yeah."

"All right, then. When it's warm."

He nods. He doesn't know if it counts, a promise made to someone who won't remember it. He makes it anyway. Somebody should be holding it.

The night carer comes at seven. He never usually stays that long.

He stands in the hall with his coat on while she takes her shoes off. The lavender has calmed down. It's just the hall again.

He goes back into the room.

His sister is looking at the lights. He puts his hand on her good hand, on the arm of the chair, for about as long as he'd put a cup down.

"Your hands are cold," she says.

"Always are."

"Get some gloves."

"Okay."

"Night, then."

"Night."

He goes. In the hall, he stops with his hand on the door, and breathes in once, and goes out.

That evening.

The train back is half empty.

People with shopping bags. A man asleep with his mouth open and a wrapped present held upright on his knees, both hands round it, as if it might try to leave. A schoolgirl going through vocabulary cards, her lips moving. On the door, a printed notice about evacuation routes that someone has already started to peel off at one corner.

Six stops. He counts them, for once. He doesn't know why. He's done this journey every Sunday for years and never once counted.

At the fourth stop the man with the present wakes up, looks round in a panic, sees it's still in his hands, and goes back to sleep.

Good, Kusakabe thinks. Hold on to it.

On the walk from the station he goes into the convenience store and pays the bills.

It's Sunday. He pays them on Mondays, at seven, at the store near the school. He has for years. Tomorrow at seven he won't be near the school.

The girl at the till scans them one by one without looking at him. The care agency. The flat. Electricity. Gas. Her phone. His phone. The machine thinks about it, and prints a long white receipt.

He looks at it for a moment. Then he puts it in the bin by the door, the way he always does, and goes home.

At home.

The pen is on the kitchen counter where he left it on Friday, next to the calendar.

In case, he thinks. Well. Here's the case.

He gets the notebook out of the drawer. The old one, with the cat on the front, that Mrs. Fujita filled with everything in case. There are pages left at the back. He sits down at the kitchen table, which he doesn't use, and writes.

Not a letter. He wouldn't know how to start one.

An address. A ground floor flat, six stops from here. Ono Haruka's number. Midori Home Care, Mr. Hasegawa. Which bank. Which bills, on which day. Tea: count to three. She'll say it's too strong. It isn't. The diffuser, every two months. The window lock on the right, which needs pushing up before it'll turn.

He reads it back.

Nobody at the school knows about any of this. Nobody's known since a man in a pale suit stopped being anyone. If it comes to it, somebody from the school will let themselves into this flat with a box. They'll find this. And then somebody will know.

He leaves the notebook open on the table. He puts the pen on top of it, so it doesn't close.

He goes to bed at eleven, because that's what you do.

Get some sleep before Sunday, a man said to him once, in a wet street at dawn, tying his tie. He never did get the order right. It's Sunday night now, and the sleep isn't coming either.

At one he's still awake. At two he gives up and goes and lies on the sofa instead, in the dark, with his coat over him, because the coat is nearer than the blanket.

The flat is very quiet. The fridge. Somebody's heating ticking in the wall.

Someone across the street is up late too. Their window puts a pale square on his ceiling, a bit crooked, and the top corner of it just reaches the stain.

He looks at it.

Same shape. It's always the same shape. He's been meaning to report it for two years.

He's been saying two years for over a year now. It's three. It's nearly three. Mr. Sato has probably forgotten he owns this ceiling.

He laughs. Once, quietly, at nothing. It surprises him in the dark, the sound of it.

Grade one sorcerer, he thinks. Going to fight the end of the world tomorrow. Possibly. Still hasn't sent the email.

Still on the sofa.

Tomorrow.

He doesn't have to make himself think about it. It's right there.

Gojo, and the King of Curses. Somewhere in Shinjuku, at a time agreed between them, like two men who've booked a court. A year ago, in that same city, somebody told him Gojo was west of the station, and that had been enough. You didn't have to think about anything else. Gojo was west of the station.

He doesn't know who wins. Nobody does. That's what the list is for.

And if it isn't Gojo, then the next one goes in, and the next. Okkotsu. The boy. The others. One after another, into whatever's left. Until there's nobody above his name.

He thinks about the size of what he felt in Shibuya. A weight with no edge, that everything leaned towards. He thinks about his circle, which is the reach of one arm and one sword, standing in front of that.

One arm, he thinks. Against that.

He lies there.

Later.

He thinks about running.

He doesn't pretend he doesn't. There's nobody here to pretend for.

It's not even hard. That's what he notices. He's spent his whole life finding back doors, and this one is the easiest he's ever seen. It's standing open.

A bag. One bag. He doesn't own much. The bank card. Cash from the machine at the station, as much as it'll give. The first train in the morning, the other way from Shinjuku, west, then north.

His sister. He'd have to go and get her. The chair folds. He's done the transfer a thousand times, the practised hug, her arm round his neck. He'd need a taxi to the station. A carriage with the space for a wheelchair. He knows which end of the platform that is. He's looked, without knowing why he was looking.

Somewhere small. Somewhere with a mountain behind it, like the photograph for January. A little red shrine at the bottom. A ground floor flat. A new agency that doesn't know him. A new carer with a new notebook, who'll write Brother came in a different hand.

He works out the money. He's good at the money now. It doesn't last forever. It lasts long enough.

And nobody would come. That's the plain truth of it. Who'd come looking? A Grade one with no technique, with a reprimand on his file and Froze? in the margin. Somebody would put a line through his name on a sheet of printer paper, and somebody else would move up one.

Who'd miss me, he thinks. Not sorry for himself. Just asking. Okabe. For the forms.

He thinks about it for a long time.

He thinks about it properly, the way he did with the Hayama job, in columns. He thinks about which bag. He thinks about whether she'd mind the cold up there, and decides she'd mind the train more, and that she'd forget the train by Wednesday.

He thinks about what he'd say to Ono. Nothing, probably. He'd just not ring. He knows exactly what that costs. He's done it before.

The square on the ceiling goes out. Whoever was awake across the road has gone to bed.

He thinks about it in the dark for a while longer.

Then he thinks about Panda holding up the rock.

He doesn't decide to. It's just there. The way it always is now, when he gets near the bottom of something. Grey light pouring in. Dust coming down. A slab he'd made his peace with, lifted off him like a lid, and Panda underneath it with both arms up, fur matted, one ear torn.

Sensei. You alive?

He hadn't been, really. Not in any way that counted. Panda had come looking anyway. Had kept lifting things until one of them was him.

He thinks about Miwa, behind him, alive.

He didn't decide that either. He was just there, in front of her, and the spiral went round. He can still feel her hand in the back of his coat, holding on, in the ice.

He thinks about the boy.

Earnest. Idiotic. Fifteen. A boy who carried a monster round inside him for months, and had it walk about in his face, and had to stand in a yard afterwards and find out who'd wanted him dead for it. And still bows all the way down. And still says thank you, for a tap on the wrist with a practice sword. For a bean on the forehead.

Tomorrow he goes in. He's on the list. He'll go in when his name comes up, with his feet doing what they're told now, without looking.

Kusakabe taught him that. A week. Six afternoons.

It isn't much. It's what he had.

And the others. He goes through them without meaning to, the way you'd count heads on a stretcher run.

Maki, somewhere, with half her face still healing and her name on a sheet that says not to discuss it. She'll be there if she can stand. She'll be there if she can't. Inumaki, with one sleeve pinned, who used to say salmon to nobody and now mostly says nothing. Panda.

Panda, on the third step yesterday, at dusk. Not near. Next to. Not one word between them, the whole time, and the light going, and the boy in the yard going up to the line and back.

He'd thought, sitting there, that it might be the first of a lot of evenings like that.

He'd still like it to be.

And then Yaga.

He reaches for the coin, out of habit, lying there in the dark. It's nearly nothing now. He can barely find it.

He paid most of it in one night in November, and never said, and never will. The rest went in a yard in the hills, with nobody there to say it was paid. He thought that might be the end of it. You can't owe a dead man.

It isn't the end of it. There's still a little left. He can feel the shape of it.

It's the shape of a bear who calls the old man Masamichi. It's the shape of a school the old man kept standing for years, mostly by being too stubborn to let it fall. It's the shape of a list read out this morning, and the children on it, every one of them taught in rooms Yaga kept open.

That's the last of it. That's where it goes.

Fine, he thinks. Fine, old man.

Somewhere after three.

The kids are going to fight tomorrow, he thinks. With their lives. They're children.

And I'm the adult.

That's all it is.

It isn't courage. He'd laugh at anyone who called it that. He's not brave. He's never been brave. He's a man who's been frightened his whole working life and turned up anyway, mostly late.

It's just a fact. It sits in his chest like a stone, and it doesn't move, and there's nothing to argue with. You can argue with a feeling. You can talk a feeling round, give it a few days, put it on the sofa. You can't talk round a stone. It's just there, the size it is.

He lies there with it for a while.

Then he gets up.

The sword is on the wall by the door, on its two hooks, where it lives when he's home.

He takes it down. He sits on the edge of the sofa with it across his knees, and turns the lamp on, and draws it a hand's width out of the scabbard.

He checks the edge. Thumb across it, never along. The cloth. The oil, the little bottle that never seems to get emptier. He wipes the blade down from the guard to the tip, slowly, and holds it to the lamp, and looks along it.

It doesn't need it. He did it on Friday. He'd know if it needed it.

It's not a good sword. It's never been a good sword. It's a plain one, bought with his own money when he made Grade two, from a man in a shop in Kanda who didn't ask what it was for. It's been reground twice. It has a small nick near the guard from a car park in Kawasaki that he's never had taken out. It's the only one he's ever needed.

He runs the cloth over the nick. Still there. Some things you leave.

That isn't why. His hands need something to do. They've always been the sensible part of him. They'd rather be doing this than lying still.

He does it twice. Then he puts it back, and it goes home with the small sound it always makes, and he sits there holding it.

Four o'clock. Five. He doesn't lie down again. He doesn't need to. Whatever the sofa used to do for him, it isn't going to do it tonight, and he finds he doesn't hold that against it.

The window goes grey at about six.

Not light. Just less dark. A cold, flat grey, with the buildings across the road coming out of it slowly, as if somebody were drawing them in pencil.

He makes the coffee.

It's as bad as it always is. He drinks it standing at the counter, looking at the calendar. The bridge in the snow. The two small people with the umbrella. The blue circle round the twenty-fourth.

Today, then, he thinks.

Somewhere below, a car starts and idles. A door. Somebody's alarm going, two floors down, for a job that's still on. Monday. For most of the city it's just a Monday, the last one before the holidays, with a bit of shopping still to do.

He envies them. He doesn't mind envying them. It's an old habit, and a harmless one.

He doesn't finish the coffee. He leaves the cup in the sink, with an inch in the bottom.

I'll rinse it later, he thinks.

He puts his coat on. His shoes. He checks his pockets, out of habit. Keys. His flat. Hers. The phone, charged, on loud, because he said he'd answer.

He stands in the middle of the room.

Probably going to die, he thinks. Almost conversationally, the way you'd mention the weather to a man at a bus stop. Probably. Not definitely.

Shikata ga nai, he thinks.

It can't be helped. People say it about rain, and late trains, and the price of things. He's said it about a good deal less than this.

It's never fitted anything better.

He lets the circle out.

Not because there's anything in the room. There's nothing. A sofa, a cup in the sink, a notebook open on a table. He just wants to feel it.

It goes out round him the way it always has. Quietly. To the same edge it's had for years. The reach of one arm and one sword. He can feel the sofa just inside it, and the table just outside, and the cold coming off the window. Nothing in it but him.

It's small. It's plain. There's no name in it, no clan, nothing anybody handed him. Just years. The same small circle, drawn and drawn again, until it stopped being something he did and became something he was.

It's everything he has.

He lets it go.

The notebook is still open on the table, the pen lying across it. He looks at it. He leaves it where it is. If he comes back, he'll shut it himself, and nobody will ever have read it, and that'll be fine.

He picks up the sword.

He opens the door. The cold comes in off the landing, sharp and clean, and the grey is getting lighter at the end of the corridor.

He walks out the door.
