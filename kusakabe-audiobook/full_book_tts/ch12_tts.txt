Chapter Twelve. Rumours.

The first time he hears it, it's a wet Tuesday in June, and it comes from Ogawa.

Ogawa is a Grade two in his late fifties who has been at the school longer than the radiators. He teaches basic warding to whoever is put in front of him, keeps a toothpick in the corner of his mouth that he never seems to use, and knows everything first. Nobody knows how. Kusakabe's theory is that he doesn't go home.

"Sendai," Ogawa says, to the staff room, which at this hour is Kusakabe and a kettle. "High-school boy. Ate one."

Kusakabe is at his desk in the corner with a monthly eligibility form. He doesn't look up.

"Ate one what."

"A finger."

That's when he looks up.

The fingers.

Everyone in this line of work knows about the fingers. You learn about them early, the way children on the coast learn where the high ground is.

Twenty of them. Sukuna's. The King of Curses, a thousand years dead and still not finished with anyone. Nobody could destroy the fingers, so they were sealed and scattered, and some of them are used as wards, because something that poisonous keeps smaller things away.

Nobody eats one. It isn't a thing people do. It would be like drinking bleach to see what colour it is.

"He's dead, then," Kusakabe says.

"No." Ogawa moves the toothpick to the other side. "That's the thing. He's fine. Walking around. Talking. Sukuna's in him, and he's fine." He says it with the bright, unhealthy pleasure of a man who has news nobody else has yet. Not pleasure about the boy. Pleasure about the news. "Gojo's got him."

Gojo's got him, he thinks.

Kusakabe has heard that sentence about a lot of things. In his experience it means the thing is now better and worse at the same time.

He goes back to the form. Maki: eligible for Grade two accompaniment. He looks at the line for longer than it needs.

Later that week.

The rest of it comes over the next few days, a piece at a time, from people who weren't there.

The higher-ups sentenced the boy to death. Straight away. Nobody argued, apparently, because there was nothing to argue about. A child with the King of Curses inside him is not a child. It's a door.

Then Gojo argued.

Nobody knows exactly what Gojo said. Ogawa thinks he shouted. The dispatch coordinator, who heard it from someone at headquarters, thinks he didn't have to. Either way the sentence wasn't cancelled. It was suspended. The boy will be allowed to live until he has eaten all the fingers. Then they'll kill him, and Sukuna with him, all at once.

"Waste not," Ogawa says.

Kusakabe walks home that evening slower than usual.

So that's the plan, he thinks. Feed a schoolboy the King of Curses one piece at a time. Then kill him. On a schedule.

And that's the version somebody fought for.

He can guess what the other version was. Old men who don't raise their voices. Over by the weekend.

He finds he can't decide which version he'd rather work next to.

Still June.

The boy is fifteen. His name is Itadori Yuji. He's enrolled. He's a first-year, in Gojo's class, with the Fushiguro boy and a new girl from the countryside who is said to carry nails and a hammer and to have opinions about Tokyo.

Yaga tested him. That's the story. Sat him down in the office with one of the dolls, the kind that hit you if your cursed energy wavers. The boy didn't flinch and didn't waver, and Yaga let him in.

"He passed," Ogawa says.

"Course he did," Kusakabe says. "He's got the best cursed energy in the country inside him."

Ogawa laughs. Kusakabe wasn't joking.

That night he lies on his back and does what he always does with a new job. He works out the grade.

Grade one is a lot. People forget that, because of Gojo, because of how easily some things happen around Gojo. A Grade one can walk into most places and walk out again. Special grade is different. It isn't the top of the ladder. It's the thing that kicks the ladder over.

And now one of them is sitting in a classroom on the first floor, behind a fifteen-year-old's face, being taught theory.

He's not even in the room, he thinks. He's in a boy who's in the room.

That's not better. That's just more walls.

The old building.

He sees the boy once, that June. Or he sees the hair.

It's at the far end of the long corridor in the old building, past the noticeboards. Gojo is walking ahead, talking with his hands. Beside him, a head of pink hair, short, bright as a warning label. A school uniform with a hood. A voice that carries, laughing at something Gojo said, the easy laugh of someone who hasn't yet learned what this place is.

Kusakabe stops.

There's an empty classroom on his left. He goes into it. He stands just inside the door, by a stack of chairs, and reads a timetable from April until the voices have gone past and down the stairs.

His circle is out. He didn't let it out. It's sitting at the length of his arm, the way it does on jobs.

He pulls it back in. It takes a moment longer than it should.

It's a corridor, he tells himself. You're in a corridor at school, hiding from a first-year.

Yes. Correct. That's what's happening.

Some weeks later.

At the start of July, Ijichi drives him to a job in Chiba and doesn't talk for forty minutes, which is normal, and then talks, which isn't.

"I drove them," Ijichi says.

Kusakabe waits. He's heard the outline already. Everyone has. A first-year dead on a job, ten days ago. He hasn't heard it from anyone who was there.

"The first-years. Last month. To the detention centre." Ijichi's hands are at ten and two on the wheel, exactly. "There was a womb. In the building. Turning into something. They said Grade one, possibly more. They sent the three of them. First-years. Three weeks in."

"Who sent them?"

Ijichi doesn't answer. That's an answer.

The rest comes out in short pieces, between junctions. A juvenile detention centre, the inmates gone or dead. The building twisted inside. The thing in it was special grade by the time anyone looked properly. The boy with the finger in him sent the other two out and stayed. Then something happened that Ijichi only knows from the reports. Sukuna came up, and fought, and won, and wouldn't give the body back. And the boy, from inside, made him give it back anyway. The only way he could.

"His heart," Ijichi says. "Sukuna took it out. Before he went under again. So the boy came back to a body with no heart in it."

He indicates left, carefully, though there's nobody behind them.

"He's dead," Ijichi says. "Itadori-kun. He's dead."

Kusakabe looks out of the window. Rice fields. A heron standing in one of them, on one leg, as if it's been told to wait there and has no plans to ask why.

He waits to feel something. Something comes.

It's relief.

It's not large. It's the feeling of a hand he didn't know was clenched coming slowly open. A boy is dead, and Kusakabe's shoulders come down half an inch, and he notices them do it, and doesn't like noticing it, and they stay down anyway.

Fifteen, he thinks. Good kid, probably. Bad luck. And I'm sitting here glad the door shut.

He doesn't say anything. Ijichi doesn't seem to need him to.

"Gojo-san was away," Ijichi says, after a while. "When it was decided. He came back and found out." He swallows. "He said he'd talk to me about it later."

"Has he?"

"Not yet." Ijichi turns into a car park. "I've been waiting. It's been quite a long later."

Summer.

The summer goes on.

That's the strange part. A boy dies, and the summer doesn't notice. The cicadas start. The training yard dries out and goes hard under the feet. Maki leaves the practice sword on the third step every afternoon and he picks it up, and gets hit, and occasionally doesn't.

The Fushiguro boy and the girl with the hammer walk round the school with faces like closed shutters. Kusakabe gives them the space you give anyone who's carrying something heavy up a stair.

Panda tells him, one afternoon on the grass, that he never got to meet the boy.

"Everyone says he was nice," Panda says.

"Yeah."

"I'd have liked a new first-year. Someone to be older than."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything. There's nothing in him he'd want Panda to hear.

The only person in the building who seems untouched is Gojo. Which, Kusakabe thinks, is either the worst thing about Gojo or the most Gojo thing about him, and he can't tell which. Gojo walks the corridors humming. He buys snacks in bulk. Once, in August, Kusakabe passes him at the gate with a carrier bag from a rental shop, the plastic stretched square around a stack of DVDs.

"Film night," Gojo says, cheerful, without stopping.

"Right," Kusakabe says.

He doesn't think about it again. Later, he'll wish he had. It would have saved him a surprise. Not much else.

Then September.

In September there's something in Kanagawa.

It arrives as less than a rumour. A school. An assembly hall. A curse that does things to people's shapes. A boy who died, or a lot of people who died, depending on who's telling it. Nanami's name is on the file, and the file is sealed.

Ogawa doesn't know anything about it. That's how Kusakabe knows it's bad.

Still September.

The Goodwill Event with Kyoto is in September too. Two days. The Kyoto students come to Tokyo, and the two schools spend the first day trying to beat each other in the woods behind the school, and everybody calls it an exchange.

His three are on the team. Maki has been training for it as if it's personal, which, since her sister is on the other side, it is.

He's supposed to watch. Every teacher in the building is supposed to watch.

The week before, the dispatch coordinator puts a sheet in front of him. A dam in Ibaraki. Something in the overflow tunnel. Two days, possibly three.

"Somebody has to cover the region," she says, "while everyone else is watching children hit each other."

"Fine," he says.

She looks at him as though she's waiting for him to argue. He doesn't. He takes the sheet. Walking away, he notices that he's not disappointed, and that this is not because he dislikes watching children hit each other.

It's the woods, he thinks. Two schools. One of them with him in it. No.

Wait. He's dead. He's not in it.

Still no.

Ibaraki.

The thing in the overflow tunnel is slow and stupid and takes most of a day to find. He cuts it in the dark with water running round his ankles.

At four in the afternoon, the young auxiliary driving him back gets a call. She listens. Her face goes very still.

"There's a curtain over the school," she says.

"Whose?"

"Nobody knows. Curse users. Curses." She's already turning onto the expressway. "It's over the woods. They can't get in. Gojo-san can't get in."

That last part sits in the car with them for a while.

It's two hours to Tokyo. By the time the radio says it's over, they're still forty minutes out. When they come up the hill, the gate is open and there are cars everywhere, and lights in the trees. People are walking in pairs with the slow, careful steps of people who've been running.

Behind the school, where the woods go up the slope, something is missing.

He stands at the top of the path and looks at it in the last of the light. A line has been taken out of the forest. Straight. Clean. Trees on either side, and between them nothing, a long bare gap running up the hill, as if someone had laid a ruler across it and pressed down.

Gojo, he thinks. Got in eventually, then.

He can smell it. Raw wood, and turned earth, and something underneath that he doesn't have a name for and doesn't want one.

Back at the school.

The pieces come in over the next three days, from everyone, all at once and none of them the same.

A special grade. Here. In the woods behind the school, with the students. A curse made of trees. Or flowers. Or both. Ogawa says it had branches growing out of its eyes. A Kyoto auxiliary says no, the eyes were fine, it was the arms. The dispatch coordinator says it didn't matter what it looked like, it nearly killed a Kyoto third-year and the boy.

"Which boy?"

She looks at him.

"Itadori," she says. "You didn't hear?"

The training yard.

He hears it from Panda, in the end. On the grass, the next afternoon, Panda flat on his back with patches of fur missing on one side and a bandage on one ear.

"He's alive," Panda says.

"Who."

"Itadori." Panda turns his head. "Gojo-sensei hid him. All summer. Training him. Nobody knew. Not even Fushiguro. Then on the first day of the event he wheeled a big box into the meeting, and Itadori jumped out of it and said surprise."

Kusakabe looks at him.

"He jumped out of a box."

"It was very awkward," Panda says happily. "Gojo-sensei was supposed to explain first. He didn't. So Itadori just stood there holding the box lid, and the Kyoto lot thought it was a trick, and Kugisaki cried and then pretended she didn't."

"Right."

"He's fun," Panda says. "You'd like him."

"Doubt it."

"You haven't met him."

Kusakabe watches the cherry trees, which are just starting to go yellow at the edges.

"Let's keep it that way," he says.

Panda doesn't laugh. He looks at Kusakabe for a moment, the way he sometimes does, as though he's turning something over to see the label. Then he turns his face back to the sky.

Later.

What Panda doesn't tell him, Ogawa does.

Inumaki used his voice on the special grade. Told it to go away, or to stop, or both. Against something that size, it was like shouting at a lorry. It went. He coughed up enough blood that somebody had to help him walk.

Kusakabe finds him the next morning on the bottom step, collar zipped to his nose, a fresh white dressing visible under the line of his jaw.

He sits down on the third step. He doesn't ask. Asking would mean Inumaki having to answer.

After a while, Inumaki takes the flat tin of lozenges out of his pocket and holds it up, sideways, and gives it a small shake. It rattles. Half full.

"Yeah," Kusakabe says. "Good."

That seems to be the whole report. It's better than most he reads.

Maki, who fought her sister in the woods, says nothing about any of it. Kusakabe doesn't ask her either. She leaves the practice sword on his step that afternoon, the same as always, and hits him rather harder than usual, and he lets her.

Afterwards.

The staff room talks about nothing else for a fortnight.

A Kyoto third-year called Todo, the size of a door, fought the special grade alongside the boy and now calls him brother. Nobody knows why, including, apparently, the boy.

The curse users took things from the vaults while the curtain was up. Sealed things. Some say wombs. Some say fingers. Ogawa says both. Nobody from headquarters has said anything, which, again, is how you know.

The curtain was made to keep one person out, and it was Gojo, and it worked for a while.

The second day of the event was baseball. Somebody tells Kusakabe this as though it's an ordinary thing that happened after the other thing. Tokyo won.

And there's the other rumour. The ugly one. That before the event, the old man who runs the Kyoto school told his students to find the vessel in the woods, now that he was alive again, and kill him. Quietly. An accident in a training exercise. Nobody would have been surprised.

Ogawa tells this one in a whisper, which is unlike him, and then looks at the door.

Kusakabe sits with it at his desk afterwards.

He knows what he's supposed to think. That it's disgusting. Old men sending children to kill a child. And it is.

But underneath that, in the place where he keeps the things he doesn't say, there's a smaller voice, and the smaller voice is saying: I understand them.

Not the method. The fear. Somebody looked at the King of Curses walking around a school in a hoodie, and did the sum, and didn't like the answer.

He doesn't like that he understands it. He goes on understanding it anyway.

The end of September.

Gojo finds him at the end of September, by the vending machines on the ground floor, which isn't hiding, but is near it.

"Isn't it great?" Gojo says.

He doesn't say what. He doesn't have to. He's practically glowing. Blindfold on, hands in his pockets, rocking slightly on his heels like a man waiting for applause.

"Great," Kusakabe says.

"He's good, you know. Really good. He's going to be really, really good." Gojo leans on the machine. "You should come and watch him train sometime. He's got legs like you wouldn't believe. Threw a shot put halfway across a stadium, back at his old school. Before any of this."

"I'm busy."

"You're always busy."

"Yeah. It's a lot of work."

Gojo grins. It's not quite the grin he had last winter, handing out praise in the staff room. There's something underneath it that's harder and doesn't move.

Kusakabe hears himself say, "And the other one."

Gojo doesn't pretend not to understand. That's something.

"The other one stays where he is," Gojo says.

"Until."

"Until nothing." Gojo pushes off the machine. "I'm here."

He says it lightly. He says it as though it's a fact about the building, like the stairs or the roof. And the thing is, it is. Kusakabe has seen what happens when Gojo is here. He saw a line taken out of a forest.

Gojo pats him on the shoulder and walks off, humming.

Kusakabe stands by the machine and doesn't buy anything.

That's the whole plan, he thinks. "I'm here."

It's a good plan. It's probably the best plan anyone's got.

It only has the one part.

A few weeks on.

October. The boy is at the school now, properly. Not hidden. Not in a box.

Kusakabe starts to know where he is the way you know where a draught is coming from in a room. Without looking.

He knows the first-years train on the far side of the yard in the mornings. He knows the boy eats in the canteen at twelve sharp and goes back for more. He knows it the way he knows the layout of a job.

He keeps his distance. It isn't hard. A school is mostly corridors, and a corridor is mostly a choice about when to walk down it. He leaves the staff room two minutes later. He comes back by the outside stairs. Once, at the end of the long corridor, he sees pink hair coming the other way. He stops at a noticeboard and reads a fire drill procedure from start to finish, until the voices have gone round the corner.

Fire drill, it says. Assemble by the bins. He's worked here six years and never known that.

His circle doesn't come out on its own again. He's careful about that. He keeps it folded small and tight.

But he can feel himself doing it, all day. Like holding a door shut with his shoulder, in a room where nobody else is pushing.

The staff room again.

Ogawa, at the kettle, mid-October.

"Funny thing," Ogawa says. "I sat in on one of his theory lessons. Covering for Gojo. He asked me what happens to curses after you exorcise them. Where they go."

"What did you say?"

"I said nowhere. He said that was sad." Ogawa moves the toothpick. "The King of Curses is sitting in that boy, and he thinks it's sad what happens to curses."

Kusakabe waits for the rest. There isn't any. Ogawa just shakes his head, as if the boy is a puzzle that's got a piece missing, and pours his tea.

Through those weeks.

It's not panic. That's the thing he'd say, if anyone asked, which nobody does.

He sleeps. He eats. He takes the jobs on the board and comes back from them. He spars with Maki and goes home and sits on the sofa and gets up again.

The fear is just there. Steady. Quiet. Like the sound of your own breathing, which is always going and which you only hear when you think about it. And then for a while you can't stop hearing it.

A fifteen-year-old boy with a thousand-year-old monster folded up inside him, asleep, or pretending to be. And the only thing between the monster and everything else is the boy's own will, and Gojo.

Two things. A boy and a man.

He's seen what can happen to a boy. He heard about it in a car in July.

On Sunday, at his sister's, he checks the lock on the window twice.

She watches him do it from the chair.

"Expecting someone?" she says.

"No."

"You did that twice."

"Yeah."

She turns back to the window. "You always were a worrier," she says, which isn't true, and which he lets stand.

Earlier that month.

Nanami tries to tell him about the boy once, at midnight in an empty office building in Shinagawa. Kusakabe finds a reason to change the subject.

He's built a picture of Itadori Yuji in his head, piece by piece, over five months. He could draw it for you.

Pink hair. Fifteen. From Sendai. Eats a lot. Thanks people for things. Strong legs. Jumped out of a box. Died once. Fought a special grade in the woods. A Kyoto giant calls him brother. Thinks it's sad what happens to curses. Laughs loudly in corridors.

Every one of those pieces came from someone else's mouth.

He could pick the boy out of a crowd by the hair. He couldn't tell you what colour his eyes are. He couldn't tell you what his voice sounds like when it isn't laughing, or whether he's left-handed, or which foot he puts his weight on when he's frightened.

He knows how to read a person in about four seconds. He's never let himself have the four seconds.

It's fine, he thinks. You don't need to know the shape of a door. You just need to know what's behind it.

A wet morning.

Once, in late October, it rains properly for the first time in a month, and he changes his route.

His usual way from the staff room to the second-years' classroom goes along the covered walkway between the buildings. Dry, short, and past the first-years' door.

That morning, as he comes out of the staff room, he hears the first-years ahead of him on the walkway. The girl, loudly, complaining about something. The Fushiguro boy, flat, saying one word. And the third voice, louder than both, laughing.

He stands in the doorway for a second.

Then he turns round and goes the other way. Down the back stairs, out the side door, along the outside of the old building where there's no roof. Round the storehouse. Up the slope. It takes five minutes longer. It's raining the whole time.

He comes into the classroom with his hair flat and his shoulders dark with water.

Maki looks up from her desk. She looks at his hair. At the window. At the covered walkway outside it, which is dry.

"You're wet," she says.

"Rain."

She looks at the walkway again. Then at him. For a moment he thinks she's going to say something.

She doesn't. She goes back to her book.

Panda, who is lying across two chairs at the back, says nothing either. But he's watching. Kusakabe can feel it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

He sits down and opens the textbook to a page he's taught twice.

Not in front of the kids, he thinks. Not like that.

He doesn't do it again. Not that particular way. He gets better at timing instead.

Days later.

The last week of October. Clear and cold and very blue.

He's crossing from the staff room to the car yard with his sword bag over his shoulder. A job in Saitama, small, done by evening. Ijichi will be waiting.

The path takes him along the edge of the courtyard in front of the old building. There's a ginkgo in the middle of it that drops its leaves all at once every year, on one particular night, as if it's agreed something with itself. It hasn't yet. It's gold all the way through and holding on.

Under it, three first-years.

The girl is sitting on the low wall, swinging one foot, explaining something with her hands. The Fushiguro boy is standing with his hands in his pockets, looking at the sky, pretending not to listen. And between them, his back half turned, a boy with pink hair, in the hooded uniform, bent over, laughing so hard he has to put his hands on his knees.

It isn't a big laugh. It's just a kid laughing at something his friend said. It carries across the courtyard, very clear in the cold air, and the girl throws a ginkgo leaf at him, and he laughs harder.

Kusakabe stops at the edge of the courtyard.

He could walk straight across. It's twenty metres. The path goes right past the tree. He could nod, the way he nods at everyone, and keep walking, and that would be that. Four seconds.

He stands there long enough to know he isn't going to.

The boy straightens up, still grinning, and starts to turn this way.

Kusakabe turns first, and takes the long way round.
