# Chapter 19. The Corridor

Okabe is waiting at the top of the stairs with the form already in his hand.

That's the first thing that's different. Okabe doesn't wait for people. People come to Okabe's desk and stand in front of it, and Okabe finishes the line he's writing before he looks up.

The second thing is the jacket. A dark suit jacket, buttoned, a little tight across the middle. No cardigan. Kusakabe has known him for years and has never once seen him without a cardigan. He looks like his own photograph in a newspaper.

"Kusakabe-san."

"Okabe."

"Statements." He holds out the form. "Everyone who was inside the curtain. The Office wants them by five."

Kusakabe takes it. Two pages, stapled at the corner.

"Today," Okabe says.

"I know what by five means."

Okabe nods. His eyes go to Kusakabe's right sleeve, where the dressing on the forearm makes a small square shape under the cloth. Then to his left hand. The black strip from the cape is gone. It's white clinic tape now, neat and anonymous. Okabe doesn't say anything about either. He writes something on his clipboard. Kusakabe can't see what.

"Room six is free," Okabe says. "Second door. There's tea." A pause. "It isn't good."

"Fine."

"I'm told everyone's is late. This week." He says it to the clipboard. Then he goes down the stairs, one hand on the rail, carefully, like a man who hasn't slept in his own bed for several nights and has stopped expecting to.

*Five days,* Kusakabe thinks. *That's what it took to get Okabe out of a cardigan. Five days and a district.*

---

Room six has a table, four chairs, a whiteboard with the ghost of somebody's diagram on it, and a flask of tea on a tray with paper cups.

He pours one. It comes out the grey-brown of a puddle in a car park and tastes of the flask. He drinks it anyway. He sits with his back to the wall and the door in front of him, and puts the form on the table, and reads it.

*Account of Movements, 31 October.*

Name. Grade. Assigned position. Time of entry to the curtained area. Times at each subsequent position, with reasons for any change. Persons encountered. Injuries sustained. Injuries observed. Information received during deployment. And at the bottom, a large empty box with *Further Remarks* printed over it in a small, polite font, as if it doesn't want to put anybody under pressure.

He fills in his name. That part he's good at.

*Grade 1.*

*Standby, Shibuya (north). With Panda (second year).*

Time of entry. He thinks about that one. The tunnel, the train standing with its doors open. He writes a time that's roughly right and means nothing.

Then the positions. The tunnel. The service passage. The stairs up into the shopping building. The staircase on Meiji-dori. South. North again. The street with the hat shop. The rest he puts down in the order it happened, which isn't the same as the order he remembers it in.

*Reasons for any change.*

He writes *Ordered* three times, and it's true three times.

He writes *Withdrew* once. He looks at it for a while. It's true as well.

*Persons encountered.*

He writes *Panda (second year)* again. Then a curse user, tied up with a sash. Then Tanabe, or Tanaka. He writes *Tanabe (Grade 2)* and leaves it. If it's wrong, someone will put a line through it, and then at least one person in the building will know the man's name.

He notices, about halfway down the second page, that Panda's name is in every box. Every position. Every change. Every list. It runs down the form like the stitching down the side of a bag.

*Information received during deployment.*

*Radio. Fragments.*

He stops there with the pen on the paper.

There was a man with blood in his eyebrow, at the top of some stairs, who told him exactly where not to go. That isn't a fragment. That's a sentence. It had a floor number in it.

He doesn't write it down.

He lifts the pen and moves on to *Injuries sustained*, and doesn't look back up the page, which is the same as writing it down, only quieter.

*Forearm, cut. Left hand, cut. Left side, bruising.*

Then, because there's no box for it and he has to put it somewhere: *Frozen (ice), duration unknown. Released by special grade Yuki Tsukumo.*

He reads that back.

*Grade 1 sorcerer,* he thinks. *Account of Movements. Movements: none, for a while. I was a block of ice.*

*Further Remarks.*

He leaves it empty. The box looks relieved.

---

The tray on Okabe's desk is already full when he puts his form on top of it. Thirty of them, maybe more, squared off at the corners. Every one of them two pages. Every one of them someone's whole night, in the order they decided it happened.

His goes on the top. It doesn't look any different from the others. That's the idea.

The lift on the second floor has a handwritten sign taped to it. *Out of order. Please use the east stairs.* The handwriting is careful and very upset. He goes along the corridor towards the east stairs.

It's a long corridor. Grey carpet down the middle, polished wood either side of it, doors on the left with numbers and no names, windows on the right looking down on a courtyard where nobody ever goes. The radiators are on too high. The whole building smells of radiators being on too high, and of paper, and under that, faintly, of something medical, as though half the people in it have come straight from a clinic. They probably have.

At the far end, by the last window, two men are standing with their backs half to him.

One is old. Grey hair cut close, a dark wool coat folded over one arm, a face that's been pinched in at the mouth for so many years it's set that way. The other is younger, forty perhaps, with a cardboard file box held against his stomach like a tray of drinks.

They're talking quietly. Not quietly enough. The floor is the kind that carries.

Kusakabe doesn't slow down. He doesn't speed up either. He keeps the same pace, the one he's been walking at all his life, the one nobody remembers.

"Signed this morning," the old man is saying. "All of them. Nobody held back."

"Death, then."

"What else would it be."

"For Geto."

"For conspiring with Geto Suguru. For Shibuya. For half a city." The old man turns his coat over on his arm, as if it's the coat that's bothering him. "You were in the room on Friday. You heard."

"I heard what they said. I didn't hear what they had."

The old man doesn't answer that.

Kusakabe passes a door. Then another. The carpet takes his footsteps and gives nothing back.

"They have what he can do," the old man says, after a moment. "That's enough. Not the dolls. The other kind. Cursed corpses that don't need him. With their own souls in. That get up and walk about and make up their own minds. Nobody else alive knows how, and he's never once told anyone. Not in twenty years of being asked nicely."

"So it's the method they want."

"It's the method they're frightened of. An army that doesn't need paying and doesn't need feeding and doesn't need him. You'd be frightened too."

*With their own souls in.*

Kusakabe's feet keep going. Left, right. The same pace. He's quite proud of them, in a distant way, the way you'd be proud of a dog that sits when the doorbell goes.

Twenty minutes away by train, on a ground floor, by a window, there's one of those. Sitting on a lap.

He keeps walking.

"And he's run."

"Before the order got to him. Walked out of his own office."

"So how do they get him?"

"The panda." The old man says it like a word for an object. "They've got it downstairs. In the old rooms, sealed. He made it. He's been sewing its ears back on for years. He'll come for it."

"And if he doesn't?"

The old man shrugs. The coat moves with him.

"Then it's evidence," he says. "One of his. The only one they've got their hands on. They'll open it up and see how it's done."

They notice Kusakabe then. He's three metres away. Two.

The younger one shifts his box. The old one gives him the look the old ones always give him, the one that starts at his face, finds nothing on the list, and goes back to the window.

Kusakabe nods.

"Afternoon."

They nod back. Neither of them knows who he is. A Grade 1 with no technique, a coat, a sword bag, a bandaged hand. One of thirty forms in a tray. It's taken years to be that unremarkable. It's the one thing he's built that's never once let him down.

He goes past them and through the door to the east stairs, and lets it close behind him on its slow arm.

He goes down the stairs at the same pace he came along the corridor.

He doesn't stop on the landing. He doesn't stop at the bottom. He doesn't stop at the door, or on the front steps, or at the gate. A guard he's never seen before checks the photograph on his pass against his face and waves him through.

He doesn't stop until he's two streets away, at a red light, with nothing to do but wait for it.

Then he stands there and finds his jaw aches from holding it shut.

*Not my business,* he thinks.

The light changes. He crosses.

---

The trains are running, mostly.

The line through Shibuya isn't. A sheet of paper taped to the ticket machines explains why without explaining anything. *Service suspended between the following stations until further notice. We apologise for the inconvenience.* Somebody has drawn a small sad face in biro in the corner. Somebody else has crossed it out.

He takes the long way round, two changes, and gets off a stop early and walks.

The city has changed its walk. That's the only way he can put it. People still go to work. They still carry bags and look at their phones. But they go faster, and closer to the walls, and they look up more. A woman at a crossing keeps glancing at the sky over the rooftops as if it might be about to go dark at four in the afternoon. It's the second time Kusakabe has seen someone do that today.

A small boy in a yellow school cap waits at the same crossing, holding a fold of his mother's sleeve. When the green man comes on he doesn't move until she does. Nobody tells him to. He's just learned it, this week, the way the whole city has.

An electronics shop has a wall of televisions in its window, all on the same channel. Footage from a helicopter. Grey. A great untidy field of grey with smoke going up from it in thin lines, and the ticker running along the bottom too fast to read. From up there it doesn't look like anything. It looks like a quarry.

He doesn't stop to watch. He knows what it looks like from underneath.

A convenience store has a sign on the door. *Water: two per customer.* Inside, the shelf where the bottled water goes is bare metal. The boy at the till is reading the back of a cup of noodles as though it might have the news on it.

There are photographs on the lamp posts. He sees the first one and then he can't stop seeing them. A man in a suit at a wedding. A girl with a cat on her shoulder. *Have you seen.* A phone number written very large. He keeps his eyes on the pavement after the third one, and counts the lamp posts instead, and loses count.

*Not my business,* he thinks. *Not my fight.*

He says it again at the next corner, in case it didn't take the first time.

---

His flat is exactly where he left it.

Same door. Same lock that sticks if you turn the key too fast. Same smell of a room nobody's been in all day. The jar of instant coffee by the kettle. The sofa.

He puts the sword bag down against the arm of the sofa and sits.

He doesn't take his coat off. He'll do that in a minute.

The clock on the cooker says five to five.

*Right,* he thinks. *Let's go through it.*

He goes through it.

Yaga is a grown man. Older than Kusakabe, and harder, and better at nearly everything. He's known for twenty years, at least, that what he does is the sort of thing they kill people for. He knew it in Kusakabe's kitchen. He knew it at the window, at that flat on the ground floor, when he was very clear that it was not the boy back. He did it anyway. That was his choice. He made it with his eyes open. He's not a man who needs rescuing. He's a man who walked out of his own office before the order got there. Wherever he is now, he'll have thought about this longer and better than Kusakabe can on a sofa.

Not his business.

Panda can lift a building. Kusakabe's seen him do it. Panda is strong and quick and patient, and he's lived in that school his whole life, and people like him. Somebody will speak up for him. Gojo would, if Gojo were anywhere. Somebody else will.

Not his business.

And the other thing. The thing under everything else.

There's a reprimand on his record with seven months left to run. A complaint from May. A letter on cream paper from the Zen'in. A pencil note in a margin that says *Froze?* That's what's on the file. That's a file that gets read carefully. A man with that file doesn't get a reprimand for this, or a meeting in September, or a deferred allowance. He gets the other thing. The thing the old man in the corridor said in two words.

And then who pays Ono. Who rings the bell and then uses his own key. Who makes the tea wrong.

And under that, smaller and colder: the method. They want the method. They're frightened of anything that has its own soul in it and wasn't made by them. If anyone, ever, starts asking questions about what Yaga made, and who for, and where it is now, then the safest thing a man can be is somebody nobody's ever looked at twice.

He's spent his whole life becoming that. He's very good at it. He just proved it in a corridor.

*Not my business. Not my fight.*

He sits back.

It's a good argument. He's checked it from every side. There's nothing wrong with it.

He sits there with it.

---

The light goes.

It goes slowly, the way it does in November, without making any announcement about it. The window turns from white to grey to the dark blue of a school uniform. He doesn't put the lamp on. Across the road, a light comes on in somebody's kitchen, and a woman moves back and forth in it, cooking something, with her back to the street.

Upstairs, somebody drags a chair across the floor. Stops. Drags it back.

His coat is still on. The sword bag is still against the arm of the sofa. His hands are in his lap. He looks at them in the last of the light and they look like hands.

The clock on the cooker says five forty.

Then six.

He doesn't think about anything in particular. He's very good at that too. The argument is finished. It's been put away. It just stays out on the table, in the dark, where he can see it.

And somewhere in that hour, without asking, his back remembers the cold.

---

Not a thought. A feeling, low down, between the shoulder blades. Grit. The flat cold of stone under him. Dust in the back of his throat, the chalk taste of it.

Then the dark. The dark with nothing in it.

Then the grinding.

He doesn't try to stop it. He's sitting on his own sofa with his coat on, and he's also lying on his back under a building, and both of those are happening at once and he lets them.

The line of light. The slab tilting. The grey sky.

And Panda's face, above him.

Upside down, from where he was lying. Dust caught in the fur round the eyes, so thick it had gone into little grey lines, like the creases in an old man's face. The torn ear. The mouth set flat with the weight of what his arms were holding. And the eyes, looking down. Not angry. Not anything, really. Just looking, to see if there was still somebody there.

*Sensei. You alive?*

He's gone over it since. He's had five days. And there's one thing about it he keeps coming back to, the way your tongue keeps coming back to a chipped tooth.

Panda didn't ask him anything.

Not one question. Not where he'd been when it came down. Not why he hadn't shouted. Not why the man who was supposed to be in charge of him had been lying in a hole in the dark with his sword drawn. Waiting for someone else to deal with it. Not what had really been on that radio at ten to nine.

He just lifted the rock. And then he held out his paw. And then he walked in front, in case.

*They'll open it up and see how it's done.*

Kusakabe's hands close in his lap.

---

He thinks about Takeru.

He doesn't mean to. It just comes in behind the rest, the way it always does, as if it was waiting at the door for its turn.

The boy on her lap. A Sunday afternoon, the window bright behind them. His feet hanging over her knees, nowhere near the floor, knocking against her shin now and then when he forgot himself, and her not minding. Her not minding. Her face, which most days doesn't know what day it is, knowing exactly what that was.

Yaga's work. The other kind. Exactly the thing the old man in the corridor is frightened of. With its own soul in.

Yaga never asked for anything for it. Never once. Not money. Not a favour. Not a word in a corridor. He just did it, and went back to his office, and sewed ears on bears, and gave Kusakabe a homeroom as though it were a thing anyone might be given.

And now his work is in a room downstairs, sealed, with a sign on the door. Waiting for someone senior to come down with a list and some instruments.

The debt is where it always is.

For years it's been something he carries. A weight. A thing to check on trains, and in the dark, to make sure it's still there. He's never once thought of it as something he could hand over. There was never anywhere to hand it.

He sits there in the dark with his coat on and feels the shape of it.

It's exactly the shape of a sealed door.

---

*Damn it,* he thinks.

He says it out loud, quietly, to the room.

"Damn it."

The room takes it without comment.

He sits for one more minute, because he's earned that much. He looks at the argument on the table. It's still a good argument. Every word of it is true. It just doesn't seem to be the part of him that's deciding.

Then he stands up.

His knees take a moment. His left side takes longer. He's still got his coat on. He's been wearing it for an hour and a half on his own sofa, which probably says something about him, and he doesn't stop to ask what.

He picks up the sword bag. He puts the strap over his shoulder.

At the door, he stops, and turns, and leaves the lamp on. He doesn't know why. So the room looks like somebody lives in it, maybe. So there's something to come back to, if he comes back.

He locks the door behind him. The key sticks. He takes it more slowly, and it turns.

He goes out.

---

In the morning, Panda is free.

Kusakabe learns it at twenty to seven, standing at the window in a T-shirt, from his phone, which buzzes once on the windowsill beside the cup.

It's Ogawa. Ogawa doesn't use punctuation and doesn't go home.

*bear's out. last night sometime. nobody knows who. upstairs going mad*

He reads it twice. He puts the phone back on the windowsill, face up.

The kettle clicks off behind him.

He makes the coffee the way he always makes it, which is badly. One spoon, a bit more than one. Water just off the boil, because he never waits for it to settle. A stir that's mostly for something to do. It comes out the colour it always comes out, and smells the way it always smells, and tastes, when he tries it, exactly as bad as it did last Tuesday. He's grateful for that in a way he couldn't explain to anyone. Some things you want to stay exactly where they are.

He stands at the window with it.

The street is quieter than it was before Shibuya. Fewer people. The ones there are walk faster. A woman in a padded coat puts a bag of recycling out by the post, looks up at the sky the way everybody does now, and goes back in. A delivery van stops, thinks about it, and goes on without delivering anything. Two crows are working on a split bin bag on the corner with the patience of men who are paid by the hour.

The phone buzzes again. Not a message this time. A call.

*Okabe.*

He lets it ring twice, which is fair, and picks up.

"Kusakabe-san." Okabe's voice sounds the way the jacket looked. "I'm sorry to ring so early. I'm working through a list."

"Okay."

"Can you tell me where you were last night? From about six."

"Home."

A pause. He can hear a pen.

"Is there anyone who can confirm that?"

"No."

The pen again. One word, he thinks. Short.

"Thank you, Kusakabe-san," Okabe says. Then, after a moment, in a different voice: "It's a long list."

Kusakabe doesn't know if that's meant to be a comfort. He doesn't think Okabe knows either.

"Okay," he says.

Okabe rings off first. That's never happened before. It's a morning for things that haven't happened before.

He puts the phone down on the sill again and drinks some more of the coffee.

---

There it is, then. He looks at it the way he'd look at the street.

If anyone ever finds out, that's the end of him. Not a margin note. Not a letter on cream paper, or a meeting in a room with a number on the door. The end. The kind they write down once, with a date beside it, and carry out quietly, by somebody senior, and never mention again. He knows exactly what it is. He heard it in a corridor yesterday, in two words, from a man holding a coat.

He looks at that for about as long as it takes to drink half a cup of very bad coffee.

Then he drinks the other half.

It doesn't feel like much of anything. No drums. No speech. It feels like Monday morning. Like paying the bills at the convenience store at seven, before anyone's in, the way he always does. Card in. Card out. The receipt in the bin by the door. Done. Nothing to show for it, and nothing to show anyone.

Panda is out. Somewhere. Wherever Panda would go. That's Panda's business now.

Nobody knows who.

Nobody's going to.

Somewhere at headquarters, in a tray on a desk, there's a form with his name at the top and Panda's name in every box. Under *Further Remarks*, nothing at all. It's the most accurate thing he's ever filed.

On the corner, one of the crows gets the bag open at last and pulls out something long and white, and the other one immediately wants it.

---

He finishes the coffee standing up.

There's a ring of it dried in the bottom of the cup, the way there always is. He takes it to the sink and runs the tap until the water comes warm. He rinses the cup, and rubs the ring out with his thumb, and the water runs down over his wrist and over the white tape on his left hand and he lets it.

He turns off the tap.

He shakes the cup once, and looks at his hands.

Yesterday, in the dark, on the sofa, they looked like hands. A few days ago, at dawn, in what was left of Shibuya, with his teeth going, they looked like somebody else's. Somebody braver. He didn't recognise them at all.

He recognises them now.

He puts the cup upside down on the rack.

His hands are steady.
