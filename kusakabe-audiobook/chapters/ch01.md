# Chapter 1. Six Ten

The alarm goes off at 6:10.

He set it to 6:10 years ago, on the theory that ten extra minutes would make him a different person in the morning. It hasn't. He's kept it anyway.

Kusakabe Atsuya lies on his back and looks at the ceiling.

It's the ceiling of a flat that is neither nice nor bad. And in the corner above the wardrobe, the water stain. Brown at the edges, paler in the middle, roughly the shape of a country he's never been to.

He's been meaning to report it for two years. The landlord's name is Sato. He has the email address saved somewhere.

*Grade 1 sorcerer,* he thinks. *One of the strongest in the country, apparently. Can't be bothered to email the landlord.*

The stain hasn't grown. He checks most mornings. It's the closest thing he has to a hobby.

He gets up because the body decides to. It's the only reliable thing about him.

---

Kettle. Mug. One heaped spoon of instant coffee from a jar whose label promises *rich roast aroma.* It smells like a cardboard box that got rained on. He makes it anyway. Better coffee would mean a machine, and a machine would mean cleaning a machine.

He drinks it standing at the window.

Below, the street is waking up. Salarymen in dark suits, walking fast, briefcases swinging. One of them is already on the phone, apologising to someone with his whole body, bowing to the pavement.

Kusakabe watches them over the rim of the mug.

Ordinary people with ordinary problems. A boss who shouts. A train that's late. A mortgage, a quarterly target, a colleague who steals the good stapler. He envies them in a vague, tired way, the way you envy people in a warm café when you're standing outside in the rain.

Nobody ever asked a salaryman to walk into a building full of curses before breakfast.

*Then again,* he thinks, *nobody ever asked me either. I just never left.*

The man on the phone has stopped bowing. He stands at the crossing with his free hand pressed to his forehead. The light turns green. He doesn't move. The crowd parts around him like water round a post, and then the light turns red again, and he's still there.

Kusakabe finishes the coffee.

*Same.*

---

He has no innate technique.

In the jujutsu world, being born without a technique is like being born without a surname. The clans don't look at you. The higher-ups don't look at you. Nobody hands you a scroll or a family name or a seat at the table. You turn up with nothing, and if you want to stay, you build something with your hands.

What he built is the New Shadow Style. Years of drawing the same small circle around himself until his body could do it in its sleep. Simple Domain, the old men call it. The reach of one arm and one sword. Anything that crosses the line, he feels. His hands answer before his head has finished asking the question.

It's not glamorous. It's a craft, like plastering a wall. You practise until you can't get it wrong, and then you keep practising.

He leaves the mug in the sink.

*Later.*

---

He's late leaving. He's always late leaving.

It's the part in between. Shirt. Socks. The sword in its bag. His phone, which he forgets on the counter and goes back for. The window, which he didn't open, and goes back to check anyway.

By the time he's on the street, the salarymen have thinned out and the delivery vans have taken over.

The walk to Jujutsu High takes twenty-five minutes. He goes the long way, along the back of the shrine and up the hill through the trees. The short way has a crowded crossing, and he doesn't like crowds. Too many people inside the circle without asking.

It's late October. The ginkgo trees along the shrine wall have gone yellow all at once, the way they do, like someone flipped a switch overnight. The fallen leaves are already squashed on the path. They smell faintly of something gone off.

An old man is sweeping the leaves into a pile. The wind puts the top of the pile back on the path. The old man starts again.

*There's a lesson in that,* Kusakabe thinks. *I'm not going to learn it.*

---

The school sits at the top of the hill behind a long wall and a lot of trees, pretending to be a religious college.

He doesn't go in through the main gate. The main gate is for students and visitors and people who want to be seen arriving. He goes round the side, past the storehouse and the bins, to the staff yard where the cars are kept.

Somewhere off to the left, from the direction of the training grounds, there's a thud and a yell and a burst of laughter. First-years, probably. He hears a girl's voice say something sharp, and a much deeper voice say, "Ow, okay, fair." He doesn't look. Not his class. Not his problem.

Ijichi is waiting by the car.

He's standing very straight beside the black sedan with a folder pressed to his chest. He checks his watch as Kusakabe comes round the corner, then checks it again, as if the watch might have changed its mind.

"Kusakabe-san. Good morning."

"Morning."

"We were scheduled to leave at eight."

"Yeah."

Ijichi waits. Kusakabe doesn't add anything. It's eight twenty-six. They both know it's eight twenty-six. There's nothing to add that would make it eight o'clock.

"Right," Ijichi says. "That's fine. It's a forty-minute drive. We'll still be within the window."

He opens the back door. Kusakabe gets in.

Ijichi is one of the few people at the school Kusakabe doesn't mind. He's anxious, overworked, and polite to the point of pain, and he never asks a question he doesn't need the answer to. He drives carefully. He apologises to other drivers when they cut him up.

*If anyone in this place deserves a pay rise,* Kusakabe thinks, *it's the man who drives the people who deserve pay rises.*

He doesn't say it. Saying it would start a conversation about pay, and he can't afford that either.

---

The briefing happens at a red light.

Ijichi passes the folder back over his shoulder without turning round. "It's a Grade 2. Probably. The assessment team weren't certain."

Kusakabe opens the folder. A photograph of a five-storey apartment block, grey concrete, balconies with rusted rails. The kind of building that went up quickly in the seventies and has been coming down slowly ever since. A few windows have newspaper taped over them from the inside.

"Owner's been trying to clear it for demolition for three years," Ijichi says. "Most of the tenants have moved out. Four households left, all on the ground floor of the east block. They've been complaining about knocking."

"Knocking."

"At night. On the walls. On the ceilings. Sometimes on the doors. Always three times." Ijichi's hands adjust on the wheel. "One of the tenants is an elderly woman. She's been sleeping in her kitchen with the light on. The demolition crew went in last week to survey the upper floors and two of them walked off the job. One of them says something knocked on the inside of his hard hat."

Kusakabe looks at the photo. Then at the next one, a stairwell, taken with a flash. The walls are covered in small dents. Hundreds of them. Neat, evenly spaced, like a pattern on fabric.

*Three times,* he thinks. *Every time. Someone wants to be let in.*

He closes the folder.

"Anyone hurt? Badly."

"No. Not yet. Hence the probably."

"Okay."

The light turns green. Ijichi checks all three mirrors and pulls away as gently as if the car were full of eggs.

Kusakabe watches the city go past. Pachinko parlours. A shuttered bakery. A man walking six small dogs, all wearing the same small coat.

*Nobody's going to know about this one,* he thinks. *That's the job, mostly. Making sure nobody knows.*

He closes his eyes for the rest of the drive. He doesn't sleep. He just stops looking.

---

The building is worse in person.

It stands in a patch of weeds behind a chain-link fence with a demolition notice cable-tied to it. The notice has been there long enough to fade. The east block is the one on the right. On the ground floor, four windows have curtains. One has a potted plant on the sill, a small, stubborn geranium, the only thing in the whole lot that looks alive.

The rest of the building looks like it's holding its breath.

Ijichi stands by the gate with his hands together. "I'll lower the curtain once you're inside. The tenants have been told it's a gas inspection. They've been asked to stay in their flats."

"Fine."

"Do you need anything? Before you go in."

Kusakabe thinks about it. A different job. A different life. Ten more minutes in bed.

"No."

He unzips the bag and takes out the sword.

Behind him Ijichi says the words, low and fast, and the curtain comes down over the building. The sky goes dark the way a room goes dark when someone stands in the doorway. The traffic noise drops away. The geranium on the windowsill turns the colour of ash.

He walks to the stairwell door. It's propped open with a brick.

---

Inside, it smells of damp and old cooking oil and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in a bag. The mailboxes in the entrance hall are all hanging open.

He stops at the bottom of the stairs and lets the circle out.

It isn't a thing he does, exactly. It's more like a thing he stops not doing. He lets his attention settle into his skin and then a little past it, the reach of one arm and one sword, all the way round. Floor. Air. The cold metal of the handrail. The draught on the back of his neck from the open door.

Nothing inside it but him.

He goes up.

First floor. Second. The dents start on the second-floor landing. Small round marks in the plaster, three at a time, three at a time, like someone patient with a hammer. They get closer together as he climbs. By the fourth floor they're almost touching.

On the fourth-floor landing, he stops.

Somewhere above him, something knocks.

Three times. Polite. Unhurried.

Then, from the wall to his left, three more. Closer.

*Okay,* he thinks. *You know I'm here. Good. Saves time.*

His palms are cold. They always are. He's done this more times than he can count and his palms are still cold every single time, as if the body is filing a formal complaint that nobody upstairs is reading.

The knocking moves. It goes along the wall, through the wall, round behind him. He doesn't turn. Turning is what it wants. He keeps his eyes on the stairs and his weight low and his hand on the sword, and he waits.

It's a long wait. Twenty seconds. Thirty.

He thinks, idly, about the man at the crossing this morning, standing still with his hand on his forehead while the light changed.

Then the wall behind him opens.

He doesn't see it. He feels it. Something crosses the line of the circle at the back of his left shoulder, fast and thin and wrong, and his body has already turned and drawn and cut before the thought *behind* has finished forming. There's a sound like a wet branch breaking. Something long and pale with far too many knuckles hits the floor of the landing and curls up like a dead spider.

Then the rest of it comes out of the wall.

It's bigger than the photographs suggested. A hunched, grey thing, a body made mostly of arm, the arm made mostly of fist. It's got no face, only a sort of flat, listening patch where a face would go. It raises what's left of the fist and knocks on the air between them. Three times.

*No,* he thinks. *Not coming in.*

It lunges. It's quick, quicker than a Grade 2 should be, and for half a second he feels the old cold drop in his stomach. Then it's inside the circle, and the half-second doesn't matter, because the circle doesn't wait for him to be brave. The blade goes where the line says it should go. Once, across. Once, down.

The knocking stops.

The thing comes apart slowly, the way smoke does when the room is still. The dents on the walls stay where they are. Those were real. Plaster doesn't heal.

He stands on the landing with the sword held low. His heart is going faster than it needs to. His hands are fine. His hands are always fine. It's the rest of him that takes a minute to catch up.

He breathes in. Damp and oil, and nothing sweet any more.

He checks the fifth floor anyway. Empty rooms. A child's sticker on a door frame, a cartoon bear, faded almost white. A calendar on a kitchen wall, still on March of a year that ended a long time ago.

*Someone lived here,* he thinks. *Someone knocked on these doors and got let in.*

He doesn't stay with that thought. He wipes the blade, sheathes it, and goes back down the stairs, three dents at a time.

---

Ijichi is exactly where he left him, by the gate, hands together. He lifts the curtain as Kusakabe comes out. The sky comes back. The geranium is red again.

Ijichi looks at his watch. Then at Kusakabe.

"That was eleven minutes."

"Yeah."

"Is it..."

"Done."

"Oh." Ijichi seems almost disappointed, the way people are when a film they braced for turns out to be short. "Good. That's good. I'll call it in."

He steps away to make the call. Kusakabe zips the sword into its bag and stands in the weeds with his hands in his pockets.

A curtain moves on the ground floor of the east block. The window with the geranium. An old woman in a cardigan looks out at him. She has the face of someone who hasn't slept properly in a month. She looks at him the way you look at a man from the gas company, which, as far as she knows, is what he is.

She slides the window open a few centimetres.

"Are you the inspection?"

"Yes."

"Did you find it? The noise."

He thinks about what to say. The truth would take an hour and she wouldn't believe any of it.

"It was the pipes," he says. "It'll stop now."

She looks at him for a moment longer. She doesn't thank him. She doesn't seem to think it's the kind of thing you thank anyone for. She just nods, once, and slides the window shut, and the curtain falls back.

He prefers it, honestly. Thanks would mean standing there while someone was grateful at him.

*She's going to sleep in her bed tonight,* he thinks. *That's the whole report, really.*

It won't be the whole report. The report has eleven sections and a box for *Additional Observations.*

On the drive back, Ijichi says, "Well done, Kusakabe-san," into the rear-view mirror, quietly, the way you'd say it to someone who might not want to hear it.

Kusakabe grunts.

It's as close to *thanks* as he gets before lunch.

---

Back at the school, the corridor outside the administrative office smells of floor polish and the radiator that clanks. He's on his way to nowhere in particular, maybe the staff room, maybe the vending machine, when a voice says his name the way people say the name of a parcel they've been waiting for.

"Kusakabe-san. A moment."

Okabe.

Okabe works in administration. Mid-level. Not a sorcerer, or not much of one; Kusakabe has never asked and never will. He's in his forties, with a neat side parting and a cardigan the colour of weak tea. He likes forms. Not in an ironic way. He likes it when the boxes are filled in on time, and he takes it personally when they aren't.

He has a clipboard with a printout on it. He holds it up.

"The Saitama assignment. Tuesday last week. The warehouse."

"Yeah."

"The report was due on Friday at five."

Kusakabe looks at the printout. It's a list of outstanding reports. His name is on it, alone, highlighted in yellow. Someone has gone to the trouble of highlighting it in yellow.

"Is there a reason it's late?" Okabe asks.

The true answer arrives in his head, whole and simple. The warehouse was fine. The curse was fine. What wasn't fine was the security guard who'd been living in the back office for a month because he had nowhere else to go, and who had been sitting on the floor in the middle of it all when Kusakabe walked in, with his knees pulled up, not screaming, just very quiet. Writing the report means writing that down in the box marked *Civilian Involvement.* Writing it down means remembering it properly. And on Friday at five he'd been sitting in his flat with the laptop open and the box empty, and then it was dark, and then it was Saturday.

That's the reason. It's not a reason Okabe has a box for.

"No," Kusakabe says.

Okabe waits. Kusakabe doesn't fill the gap. The radiator clanks.

"No?" Okabe says.

"No."

Okabe's mouth tightens, very slightly, at one corner. "Kusakabe-san, the reports aren't a formality. They go into the assessment records. If one report is missing, the whole picture is..." He searches for the word. "Incomplete."

"Okay."

"I'm not trying to be difficult."

*You're not trying,* Kusakabe thinks. *It comes naturally. I respect that.*

"I know," he says.

"So when can I expect it?"

"Tonight."

Okabe writes something on the clipboard. Kusakabe can see the pen moving, small and precise. It's not the word *tonight.* It's longer.

"I'll have to make a note of the delay," Okabe says. "On your record. It's procedure."

"Fine."

Okabe looks at him, waiting. An apology, maybe. A sign that Kusakabe understands the seriousness of the yellow highlighter. Some people need the other person to be sorry before they can stop being annoyed. Okabe is one of those people, and Kusakabe can see it, the annoyance sitting there in the tight corner of his mouth with nowhere to go.

He lets it sit there.

It isn't his job to make Okabe feel better about Okabe's feelings. Explaining never works with people like this. It only gives them more to hold onto.

"Well," Okabe says, after a moment. "Tonight, then."

"Tonight."

Okabe turns and walks back into the office. The door closes behind him a little harder than it needs to. Not slammed. Just firm. A door with opinions.

Kusakabe stands in the corridor.

*There it is,* he thinks. *A line on a file somewhere. Kusakabe Atsuya. Late. No reason given.*

It's small. It's nothing. Nobody will read it for years, probably. But he knows how files work in this place. Nobody reads them, until somebody does, and then everything that's ever been written down gets read at once.

He goes to the vending machine and buys a can of coffee. It's warm and too sweet and tastes faintly of the can.

It's better than the stuff at home. He doesn't like what that says about the stuff at home.

---

He spends the afternoon doing what most of the job actually is: nothing that looks like anything. He sharpens the sword in the equipment room. He sits in the staff room for forty minutes with a newspaper open at the same page, while two other teachers argue about a curse in Chiba and whether it was really a Grade 1 or whether someone in assessment panicked.

Nobody asks his opinion. He doesn't offer one.

At one point Yaga walks past the open door, big and square in his dark glasses, talking low on his phone. He doesn't look in. Kusakabe doesn't look up. He hears the footsteps go down the corridor, steady and heavy, until they're gone.

At half past five, he leaves.

He goes out the side way, past the bins and the storehouse, past the car yard where Ijichi is bent over the open boot of the sedan, sorting folders into other folders. Ijichi straightens up and raises a hand. Kusakabe raises one back.

The sun is already down behind the trees. Down below, the city is turning its lights on, a window at a time.

---

On the way home, he stops at the convenience store by the crossing.

It's the bright one with the music that never changes. He picks up a karaage bento without looking at the others, because looking at the others would mean choosing, and he's done enough choosing for one day. Then a bottle of tea. Then, at the till, without really meaning to, a small packet of chocolate biscuits.

The boy at the till has dyed hair and a name badge that says *Trainee.* He scans the bento, the tea, the biscuits.

"Would you like it warmed?"

"Yes."

"Chopsticks?"

"One pair."

"Bag?"

"No."

The boy puts the bento in the microwave and watches it turn, with the expression of someone watching the sea. Kusakabe watches it too. For forty seconds they are united in a total lack of interest in each other.

The microwave pings.

"Thank you very much," the boy says, to the till.

"Thanks," Kusakabe says, to the door.

*Best conversation I've had all day,* he thinks. *No notes.*

---

The flat is exactly as he left it. The mug in the sink. The bed unmade. The water stain in the corner, watching.

He takes off his shoes. He puts the sword bag in its place against the wall, where the light from the window falls across it in the mornings. He sits on the sofa with the bento on his knees and turns on the television, because that's what you do when you get home.

A variety show. Six celebrities in a studio built to look like a school classroom. They're answering quiz questions about vegetables, and the audience laughs in big rehearsed waves.

He eats the bento. The karaage is fine. The rice is a little dry at the edges where the microwave missed.

He isn't watching the show. He's facing it. There's a difference, and he's very good at the difference. The sound is just there, filling the room, so the room isn't empty. The colours move across his face. Someone on the screen wins a giant radish, and there's applause.

He thinks about nothing.

Not quite. He thinks about the grey thing in the wall, knocking three times, wanting to be let in. He thinks about the dents on the stairs. The old woman in the cardigan sliding the window shut.

Then he stops thinking about them. Or he puts them somewhere he doesn't have to look at them, which is the same thing, more or less, if you don't look too closely.

His phone buzzes on the arm of the sofa.

A message from Mrs. Fujita, his sister's carer. *Sunday as usual? She had a good day today. Ate all her lunch.*

He reads it twice. The second time, he reads only the last line.

*Yes,* he types. *Thanks.*

He puts the phone face down.

On the screen, the celebrities have moved on to fruit.

---

At ten to eleven, he opens the laptop.

The Saitama report is still there in a window he never closed. Eleven sections. Most of them are done. The box marked *Civilian Involvement* is empty, the cursor blinking in it with great patience, the way it's been blinking since Friday.

He looks at it for a while.

Then he types: *One civilian present (security guard, male, 50s). Unharmed. Referred to local services.*

He reads it back. It's all true. It doesn't say anything about the man's knees, or how quiet he'd been, or how he'd said *sorry* to Kusakabe, for some reason, three or four times, as he was led out to the car.

The box doesn't need that. The box has what it needs.

He fills in today's report too, while he's there. *Grade 2. Exorcised. No civilian injuries.* Under *Additional Observations,* he thinks for a moment and writes: *Tenant has a geranium.* Then he deletes it.

He sends both reports at 11:04.

Somewhere in the administration building, he imagines, there's a clipboard with a note on it that says *late* in small, precise handwriting. Sending the report won't remove the note. It'll just sit next to it.

*Fine,* he thinks. *Let it sit.*

---

He turns off the television. The room goes quiet all at once, the way a room does when you remove the thing that was keeping the quiet out.

He stands up. Something in his lower back clicks.

The mug is still in the sink from this morning, a brown ring dried in the bottom.

He runs the tap until it's warm. He rinses the mug, rubbing the ring out with his thumb. The water runs down his wrist and he lets it. Outside, a car goes past, slowly, its headlights moving across the ceiling and over the stain in the corner, and then away.

He turns off the tap. He shakes the mug once.

He puts it upside down on the rack.

*Another day of pretending I'm not tired.*
