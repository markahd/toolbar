# Chapter 9. The Clan Problem

June comes in wet and stays wet.

The rainy season sits on the school like a lid on a pan. Everything is damp. The steps, the grass, the paper in the staff room, which curls at the corners if you leave it out overnight. The hydrangeas along the storehouse wall have gone a blue so deep it looks deliberate. The practice swords in the rack have swollen in their grips, and Maki has started wrapping hers in white tape.

He sits on the third step anyway. The storehouse roof keeps off most of it.

On the second Tuesday of the month, Okabe comes down the path with an umbrella and stops at the top of the steps. That's as far as Okabe ever comes. The yard is not a place for cardigans.

"A visitor on Thursday," Okabe says. "From the Zen'in family."

In the middle of the yard, Maki doesn't stop. But the rhythm of the staff changes. Half a beat. Kusakabe hears it the way you'd hear a clock miss a tick.

"For what," he says.

"The family's annual review of its member." Okabe looks at the clipboard under the umbrella. "Zen'in Masaru. Branch house. He'll observe training, then meet with the homeroom teacher."

"Which homeroom teacher."

Okabe gives him the look he keeps for late reports. "You."

*I knew that. I just wanted to hear it out loud. It sounds worse out loud.*

"I've got a job Thursday."

"You haven't," Okabe says. "I checked the board."

Kusakabe has nothing to add to that. It's rare for Okabe to get there first. He doesn't seem to enjoy it, which is almost decent of him.

"The family asked for the teacher. In writing." Okabe pauses. He seems to be searching for a word, the way a man pats his pockets for keys. "Please be," he says, and finds it, "present."

Then he goes back up the path, the umbrella bobbing between the trees until it's gone.

Kusakabe looks at the rain.

*Present. That's the bar. I can probably get over that.*

---

Panda tells him the rest, because Panda tells people things.

He comes and sits on the step below, which creaks, or would if it weren't stone. He smells of wet fabric. Most things do, this month.

"Masaru," Panda says. "He came last year. And the year before."

"You know him."

"He brings his own tea. In a flask. He doesn't drink ours." Panda thinks about it. "I don't think he drinks anyone's."

"What does he want."

"To make sure she's still Grade 4."

Kusakabe looks over at Maki. She's working through a sequence with the staff, low and then high and then low again, every strike landing on the same spot in the air as if there's a nail in it.

Grade 4. He's watched her take apart a curse in the woods behind the school that a Grade 2 would have been glad to walk away from, and then complain about her glasses steaming up.

"She's not Grade 4," he says.

"No," Panda agrees. "But she is. She's been Grade 4 since she got here. She'll be Grade 4 when she's ninety."

"Why."

"The family writes letters."

That's enough. Kusakabe knows how it works. A letter on good paper goes to the right desk, and the right desk is grateful to be written to, and a recommendation that was going to go through doesn't. Nobody has to do anything.

"Sensei," Maki calls, from the middle of the yard.

He looks up. She hasn't turned round. She's facing the cherry trees, the staff level across her shoulders.

"Thursday," she says. "Don't do anything."

"Wasn't going to."

"Good."

She goes back to the nail in the air.

Panda, below him, says nothing at all. Which, for Panda, is a lot.

---

He knows the look. That's the trouble.

He thinks about it on Wednesday night, on the sofa, with the light off and the rain going steadily on the window. Not in a big way. In the small way you think about a tooth that's started to ache.

He knows the look a clan man gives a person with no technique. It's not hatred. Hatred would at least be interest. It's the look you'd give a chair in a waiting room. You'd sit on it if you had to. You'd never ask where it came from.

He's had it from old men at headquarters, reading his file with one finger. He had it in November from a nineteen-year-old Kamo with a strap of blood packs across his chest. He's had it from people he's saved.

Nobody with power has ever wanted him for anything. Not as an heir. Not as an ally worth a letter on good paper. He built what he has out of a style nobody fashionable learns any more, and a lot of hours, and nobody ever sent a man in a crested coat to check he was still where they'd left him.

*Lucky, really,* he thinks. *Being nobody's means nobody comes to inspect you.*

Maki is somebody's. That's her problem. Born into the biggest house there is, with almost no cursed energy, needing glasses to see what everyone around her could see from birth. A family like that doesn't let go of something it's ashamed of. It keeps it close, so it can keep it small.

It's not his business.

That's the other thing he knows. Clan business is a swamp. Better-connected sorcerers than him have walked into it and come out with their careers missing a leg. You stand on the bank and say yes and no, and the man in the crested coat goes home, and nothing changes, and nothing gets worse either.

She asked him not to do anything. That's the easiest instruction he's ever been given.

*Sit on the step. Answer in single words. Let him go past like a train you're not catching.*

He goes to bed at one. The rain goes on without him.

---

Thursday, Masaru comes in through the gate at ten past two.

Kusakabe sees him first. He always sees people first. That's what the gate's for.

A man in his late fifties, short and well-fed, in a dark grey haori with the family crest small and white on the chest. Hakama. White tabi, already spotted with mud from the path, which seems to offend him personally. A young attendant walks half a step behind and to the left, holding a large black umbrella over Masaru's head. The attendant's own right shoulder is soaked through. He doesn't seem to have noticed. Or he noticed a long time ago, and stopped.

They come down the slope. The attendant unfolds a small wooden stool at the top of the steps, under the overhang, and Masaru sits on it as if it had been waiting for him all his life. A steel flask appears. Tea goes into its lid. The umbrella stays up, even under the roof. Nobody tells the attendant to stop.

"Kusakabe-kun," Masaru says pleasantly.

*Kun.* Kusakabe is thirty-something and a Grade 1. Masaru knows that. He has a voice like a man who has never once had to raise it, soft and rounded at the edges, and every word comes out of it exactly where he put it.

"Zen'in-san."

"Please. Don't get up."

Kusakabe hadn't been going to. He stays on the third step. Masaru is three steps above him and to the side, so that Kusakabe has to turn his head to see him, and doesn't.

Below them, the yard. Maki in the middle of it, working. Panda on the grass at the edge, not lying down for once, sitting up with his paws on his knees like a schoolboy outside an office. Inumaki on the bottom step, collar to his nose, hood up against the drizzle.

Masaru watches Maki for a while, sipping his tea.

"You're the swordsman," he says eventually. "The one without a technique."

"Yes."

"How suitable. They've given her someone she can look up to without straining her neck."

Kusakabe says nothing.

*There it is. Two minutes in. Not bad.*

---

It goes on like that for half an hour.

Masaru talks the way some men smoke, as something to do with his mouth while the afternoon passes. The weather, which is bad for his knees. The drive from Kyoto, and a service station on the way, which has declined. And in between, lightly, he talks about Maki.

"She still wears the spectacles."

"Yes."

"Remarkable. A Zen'in who has to put something on her face to see what she's for." He sips. "We had hopes for the pair of them, you know. Briefly. Twins are an unlucky thing in a house like ours, but there were people who said, well, perhaps this time. Perhaps two halves would make something." He smiles at the yard. "They didn't."

Maki comes in off a feint, low and fast. Her back heel stays down. The strike comes out of nowhere.

"The other one, at least, has the sense to be modest about it," Masaru says. "In Kyoto. She knows her place. This one has never known anything of the kind. She thinks if she hits things hard enough, the family will have to look at her."

He laughs, softly.

"We look at her once a year. That's quite enough."

Kusakabe sits.

The damp seeps through the seat of his trousers. The can of coffee goes warm in his hand. His face is holding, the way it always holds.

*Not your business. Not your family. She said don't.*

He has been the man on the end of this voice. Not this one exactly. Others like it, a hundred times, in corridors and briefings and the back seats of cars. He knows the exact weight of it. It doesn't hurt in the moment. It goes in sideways and finds somewhere to sit and stays there for years, paying no rent.

He'd quite like to be on the sofa.

Maki doesn't look up. Not once. She keeps working, and she keeps working well, and he understands that this is how she's always done it. Not by answering. By being something the voice can't do anything about.

He has to admire it. He doesn't want to. He does.

Inumaki shifts on the bottom step. His hand goes to the zip of his collar and stays there.

"Inumaki," Kusakabe says. Quietly. Not looking.

The hand stays a moment longer. Then it drops.

Masaru doesn't notice any of this. Masaru isn't a man who notices boys on steps.

---

"Now," Masaru says. "The forms."

The attendant produces a folder. Masaru opens it on his knee and turns pages with one finger.

"Mission eligibility. Your monthly assessment. You've written here," and he holds the page a little further away, to read it, "'Eligible for Grade 2 accompaniment.'"

"Yes."

"The family would prefer Grade 4 work only. For her safety." Masaru closes the folder. "You'll amend it."

It isn't a question. It's said the way you'd tell a waiter you'll have the fish.

He feels the shape of the answer in his mouth before he's decided anything. Masaru goes home. The form goes in a little different. Maki does Grade 4 work for another month, which she was doing anyway. Nothing changes and nothing gets worse.

"I'll look at it," he says.

"Good." Masaru hands the folder back over his shoulder without looking, and the attendant takes it without being looked at. "I knew you'd be practical. Men like you usually are. You haven't the luxury of being otherwise."

He finishes his tea. The attendant takes the cup and screws it back onto the flask.

Kusakabe thinks that's it. He thinks the train has gone past.

Then Masaru leans down a little, towards him, and lowers his voice. Not much. Just enough to make it private. Just enough to make it friendly.

"Between ourselves, Kusakabe-kun," he says. "You take these children out on jobs. Things happen on jobs. If something were to happen to her, on one of your little outings, nobody in the house would think the worse of her teacher. Some would think rather better of him. It would close a file that's been open for sixteen years." He smiles. "I'd bear that in mind. You're not paid enough to try very hard."

---

Kusakabe stands up.

He doesn't decide to. He'd like to know when it happened. One moment he's sitting on the third step with a warm can of coffee, and the next he's on his feet, turned round, and the can is on the step by his shoe, upright, and he didn't put it there.

He's below Masaru, still. Three steps down. It doesn't seem to matter.

He doesn't touch his sword. It's on the step beside the can, in its bag, and his hand doesn't go near it. He doesn't draw the circle. He doesn't need it. Some things you don't need a technique for.

He looks at Masaru. Not hard. Just looks. The way he'd look at a corridor he was about to walk down.

"If anything happens to her," he says, "I'll come and ask you about it. Personally."

That's all.

It isn't loud. The rain is louder. Down in the yard, Maki's staff goes on swinging, the same steady rhythm, and he doesn't think she can have heard it from there.

Masaru's face does something it probably hasn't done in a long time. Nothing big. The pleasantness stays on it. But behind the pleasantness something goes very still, like a man on a stair who has felt it shift under his foot.

The attendant's umbrella dips. Just an inch. Then it comes back up.

"Was that a threat, Kusakabe-kun?"

Kusakabe doesn't answer. He picks up the can of coffee, and sits back down on the third step, and turns to face the yard again.

He can feel Masaru looking at the back of his head. He lets him. There's nothing there to see.

Then the creak of the stool being folded. Footsteps going back up the slope, two pairs, one of them muddy and offended and a little quicker than before.

The gate.

Gone. Twenty minutes early.

Kusakabe drinks his coffee. It's warm and bad. His fingers have gone stiff around the can, and he has to put it down and pick it up again before they'll let go of the shape.

*Well,* he thinks. *That was stupid.*

He waits to feel sorry about it. It doesn't come. Something else comes instead, quieter, and sits down next to him, and he doesn't look at it too closely.

On the grass, Panda has turned his whole head round to look at him. Panda's ears are good. Panda heard every word. He doesn't say anything. He looks at Kusakabe for a long time with his round black eyes, and then he turns back to the yard and lies down, slowly, in the wet grass, like a man settling into a bath.

On the bottom step, Inumaki takes his hand off his collar for good, and puts it in his pocket.

Maki doesn't stop.

Maybe she heard. Maybe she didn't. She finishes the sequence, and starts it again, and her back heel stays down the whole way through.

---

It takes four days. Which is quick, for paper.

On Monday, Yaga's door is open, and Yaga's voice comes out of it as Kusakabe walks past. "In."

He goes in. He stands just inside the door.

Yaga is at his desk with a letter in front of him, thick creamy paper with the crest at the top in grey. His hands are flat on either side of it, as if it might get up.

"The Zen'in have written," Yaga says.

"I'll bet."

"'Openly threatened a representative of the family in front of students.'" Yaga reads it flatly, without any expression at all, which is how Yaga reads everything. "'Conduct unbecoming. Wholly unacceptable from a teacher entrusted with a member of the house.' There's a page of this."

"They weren't in front. They were in the yard."

Yaga looks up.

"Did you threaten him?"

Kusakabe thinks about it. Honestly. It seems only fair.

"I told him I'd ask him a question."

The sunglasses stay on him for a long time. Somewhere behind the dark glass, Kusakabe suspects, Yaga is doing a very small sum, and arriving at an answer he doesn't intend to share.

"Headquarters wants a formal reprimand," Yaga says at last. "On your record. Twelve months."

"Fine."

"Your grade allowance review is deferred. A year."

This time he does the sum.

It does itself, quickly, the way it didn't in the autumn at his sister's flat. The review was due in August. Some part of him had already spent it, on Ono's hours and the thickening powder and the reed diffuser by the shoe cabinet.

Not small. Not ruinous. Just a hole, the size of something he'd been going to stand on.

"Fine," he says again. It comes out the same. That's what the face is for.

"They'd also like a written apology."

"No."

Yaga doesn't argue. He picks up a pen and writes something on the corner of the letter in small square characters.

"I'll tell them you're drafting it," he says. "Drafts take time."

"Thanks."

Yaga makes a sound that might, in a smaller man, have been a laugh. It isn't one, quite. He folds the letter back in three.

"Kusakabe."

"Mm."

"Don't make a habit of it."

"Won't need to."

In the corridor, his hand goes to his pocket and checks the thing that isn't there. A little more owed. With a debt that size, it's hard to tell whether a bit more makes any difference. He suspects Yaga knows exactly how much.

---

Okabe has the copy by the afternoon.

He brings it to the staff room in a clear plastic sleeve, like evidence, and lays it on Kusakabe's desk next to the radiator that ticks.

"Acknowledgement of formal reprimand," Okabe says. "Sign at the bottom. It goes in your file."

"Next to the other one."

"Next to the complaint from May, yes." Okabe watches him sign. "That's two this term."

"Is there a prize."

"There's a meeting," Okabe says. "In September."

Okabe checks the signature and tucks the sleeve under his clip. Then he hesitates, which isn't like him.

"For what it's worth," Okabe says, and stops.

Kusakabe waits. Okabe looks at the clipboard as if the rest of the sentence might be on it.

It isn't. Okabe nods once, to nobody, and goes.

*Huh,* Kusakabe thinks. And then, because there's nothing else to do with it, he gets on with the attendance.

---

Maki never mentions it.

Not on Tuesday, when the rain stops for a few hours and the yard steams in a sudden fierce sun. Not on Wednesday. She doesn't look at him any more than she did before, which was mostly when he was late.

He doesn't mention it either. It happened on a step, in the rain, and then was over.

On Thursday afternoon he comes down to the yard at the usual time and his step is occupied.

Not by a person. By a practice sword. One of the swollen ones from the rack, its grip freshly wrapped in white tape, the tape still bright. It's lying across the third step, exactly where he'd sit, so that to sit he'd have to move it.

Maki is in the middle of the yard with another one, her own, held loosely at her side. She isn't looking at him. She's looking at the cherry trees. Waiting, in the way that a person waits who's prepared to do it all day.

Panda is on the grass. Inumaki is on the bottom step. Both of them are looking very hard at something else.

Kusakabe stands at the top of the steps and looks at the sword.

All his reasons are still there. He counts them, like change. The circle doesn't know a student from a curse. There's no practice version of it. If he puts her inside it, it'll answer, and he doesn't want to find out how hard. And the other reason, the one he doesn't look at: that she'll see there's nothing else. No secret. Just a man and a lot of years.

All true. All still there.

He goes down the steps and picks up the sword.

The grip is damp from the air and the tape is rough under his fingers. It's lighter than his own. Everything is.

He walks out into the middle of the yard. She turns to face him. Neither of them says anything.

He keeps the circle folded away. Not drawn. Not even close. He keeps it the way you'd keep your hands in your pockets in a shop full of glass. What's left is just the old stuff, the stuff under the circle. Feet. Distance. Eyes. Breathing. The things he learned before he learned anything worth having.

She comes in fast.

She's very fast. It's different from here. The first strike is at his head and he isn't there, the second is at his ribs and his sword is there instead, and the shock of it goes up his arm into his shoulder and his teeth. The humid air is like breathing through a wet towel. His knees have opinions about the ground. He is, he realises, about twice her age, and every one of those years is in his calves.

She hits his forearm on the fourth exchange. Properly. A crack, and then a hot line of pain, and the bruise already forming underneath like a word on the tip of the tongue.

He doesn't say anything. He resets his feet.

On the seventh exchange he sees it. Not her heel. That's fixed. Her eyes. Just before she strikes, she looks at where she's going to hit. Only for an instant. A flick. But it's there.

He lets her come in again, and when her eyes go to his left shoulder he's already somewhere else, and the tip of his practice sword touches her side, just below the ribs, light as a finger.

She stops.

"You look at where you're going to hit," he says.

She stands there breathing hard, sweat running down from under her glasses. She looks at him for a long moment. He can't tell what she's thinking. He's not sure he'd want to.

Then she grunts, and goes back to her mark, and lifts her sword.

They do ten more minutes. Then he goes back to his step, and sits on it, and rubs his forearm, and doesn't look at anyone.

Panda, on the grass, says, "Huh."

Inumaki says, "Salmon." Not to nobody, this time.

---

It becomes a thing. Nobody decides that it will.

Every afternoon, after the drills, Maki leaves a practice sword on the third step. Every afternoon he looks at it for a moment, as if it might be somebody else's, and picks it up. Ten minutes becomes fifteen. Fifteen becomes twenty, on a day when the rain holds off and his knees forget to complain.

He never draws the circle. She never asks him to. She takes what she can get from him, which is feet and distance and the small cruel economy of a man too tired to move more than he has to, and she takes it apart in front of him and puts it back together inside herself, piece by piece.

She stops looking at where she's going to hit. It takes her four days.

He has bruises up both forearms by the end of the month. On Sunday his sister asks if he's been fighting. He says yes. She seems pleased, in a vague way, as if she always suspected.

Maki doesn't say thank you. She doesn't say anything about the man in the grey coat, or the letter, or the twelve months on his file. She doesn't need to.

He has a feeling she's saying it anyway. Every afternoon, with a practice sword, about twenty times.

He doesn't say *you're welcome.* He gets hit instead. It seems to cover it.

---

The last Friday of June, the rain comes back in the late afternoon. Not hard. A fine grey drift that makes everything look further away than it is.

They've gone longer than usual. His shirt is soaked through, and there's a fresh bruise on his left wrist that he'll feel tonight when he turns the tap. She caught him twice today. He caught her more, but not by as much as last week.

*She's going to be very good,* he thinks. *Whatever grade they write on her.*

He lowers his sword. He's done. He turns, and walks back to the steps, and sits on the third one in the shade of the storehouse roof. He lets the practice sword drop on the stone beside him. It slides off and lands in the wet grass at the bottom with a soft clack.

He leans back on his elbows and closes his eyes and listens to the rain.

*That's enough for one week. That's enough for one month. That's enough for a year.*

Footsteps on the grass. He opens his eyes.

Maki is standing at the bottom of the steps. She has his practice sword in her hand. She's picked it up from the grass. Her glasses are fogged at the edges and there's a smear of mud on her cheek and she doesn't look tired at all, which seems, in the circumstances, deeply unfair.

She looks at him. Then she tosses the sword to him, underhand, an easy arc through the rain.

His hand comes up and catches it by the grip without his asking it to.

"Again," she says.

He looks at the sword. At her. At the rain.

He sighs.

"Fine."
