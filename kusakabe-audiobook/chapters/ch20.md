# Chapter 20. Gakuganji

It's Ogawa who tells him. It's always Ogawa.

A Tuesday in November, a little after two. The staff room has been half empty since Shibuya. Desks with nobody at them. A jacket on the back of a chair that nobody has come back for, and that nobody moves, because moving it would mean deciding something about it.

Kusakabe is at his desk in the corner, by the radiator that ticks and doesn't work. He's filling in a form about stretchers. Somebody at headquarters wants to know how many were used on the night of the thirty-first, and by whom, and whether they were returned. He has written *Some* in the first box. He's looking at the second.

The door opens. Ogawa comes in, shuts it behind him, and stands by the kettle without filling it.

The toothpick goes from one side of his mouth to the other. Then back.

*Twice,* Kusakabe thinks. *He's got something.*

There are two other people in the room. A woman from Records, asleep with her chin on her hand. A young Grade 3 with headphones in, eating crisps one at a time, very slowly, as if he's been told they're the last crisps. Ogawa looks at both of them. He looks at the door. Then he comes over and sits on the edge of the next desk, which isn't his.

"You heard," he says.

"Heard what."

"This morning. Early." Ogawa takes the toothpick out and turns it in his fingers. "It's done."

Kusakabe doesn't ask what. He doesn't need to. There's been only one thing in this building for two weeks. It's in every room. People make tea round it. They pass each other in corridors and nod round it, and nobody says it, and it gets bigger every time nobody says it.

He puts the pen down.

"Who," he says.

"Kyoto's old man. Gakuganji." Ogawa puts the toothpick back. "They brought him up specially. Wanted someone with a title. Someone old enough that nobody could say it was personal."

"Was it?"

Ogawa thinks about that, which is unusual for Ogawa. Usually he's already thought about everything before you've asked.

"I don't know," he says. "I wasn't there. Nobody was there that I'd ask." He looks at the door again. "It's done, anyway. That's all I've got."

He gets up off the desk. He goes back to the kettle and fills it this time, and switches it on, and stands with his back to the room while it boils.

Kusakabe looks at the stretcher form.

The second box says *Returned? Yes or no.*

He sits there for a while with the pen in his hand. Then he writes *No*, because most of them weren't, and puts the form in his tray, and goes out.

---

Okabe catches him in the corridor.

Not on purpose. Okabe is coming the other way with a stack of paper against his chest, one sheet per person, going desk to desk. He's wearing a black cardigan. Kusakabe has seen grey, brown, green, and the one the colour of weak tea. He has never seen black. He has a feeling it was bought for this. Possibly yesterday.

Okabe stops. He takes the top sheet off the pile and holds it out.

Kusakabe takes it.

Three lines, typed, centred, on headquarters paper.

*The sentence passed on Yaga Masamichi, formerly Principal of Tokyo Jujutsu High, was carried out this morning. Staff are reminded that matters relating to the case are not to be discussed. Further guidance will follow.*

*Formerly,* he thinks. *They had that ready.*

He reads it twice. It doesn't get longer.

Okabe is still standing there. He hasn't moved on to the next desk. He's holding the pile very square against his cardigan, with both hands, and looking at a point on the wall just past Kusakabe's ear.

"He always signed in the right box," Okabe says.

Kusakabe looks at him.

"Leave forms. Requisitions. Fourteen years. He never once signed in the box for the countersignatory." Okabe's thumb moves along the edge of the paper, squaring it again. It's already square. "You'd be surprised. Most people don't read the boxes."

"No," Kusakabe says. "I wouldn't."

Something happens at the corner of Okabe's mouth. It isn't a smile. It's more like a man pressing on a bruise to see if it's still there.

Then Okabe nods, once, as if they've agreed something in a meeting, and goes on down the corridor with his pile, and Kusakabe hears him knock on the next door, softly, though it's open.

---

He doesn't go back to his desk.

He walks. Down the stairs, out the side door, along the path behind the old building. His feet take him the way they always take him when nobody's told them otherwise, and he ends up where they always end up.

The training yard.

It's empty. It's been empty since Shibuya. Maki is in a hospital bed with her burns dressed. Inumaki is in another one, somewhere else, learning to do things with one hand. The packed earth has a thin skin of leaves on it that nobody's raked. The storehouse doors are shut.

He sits down on the third step from the bottom. Gate in front. Wall behind.

It's cold. The step is colder. He sits on it anyway.

*He couldn't stop it.*

He thinks that first, and then he checks it, because he doesn't trust a thought that comes that quickly and makes him feel that much better.

So he goes through it properly. The way you'd go through a building, room by room, making sure.

He could have gone to headquarters. Walked in. Asked for a meeting. Sat in the empty chair in Conference Room B with his hands where everyone could see them and said *don't*. They'd have written it down. They'd have thanked him for his time. Then they'd have done it anyway, a day later perhaps, and added his name to a list.

He could have gone looking for the old man. Gakuganji. Stood in his way. A Grade 1 without a technique, standing in front of the principal of Kyoto and the whole of the higher-ups behind him, with a sword and a small circle. He tries to picture it and it doesn't even get as far as a picture. It's just a man in a corridor, and then it isn't.

Gojo could have stopped it. Gojo could have walked into the room where they decided it and said *no*, in that bored voice, and the room would have had to find some other way to be. But Gojo is sealed in something nobody can open. That's the whole reason any of this could happen. That's why the room got to decide.

Nobody else. He runs down the list of people who could have said no and had it count, and the list is one name long, and the name is in a box.

*Nobody could,* he thinks.

He waits to see if it's an excuse.

It isn't. He'd know. He's built enough excuses in his life to recognise the shape of one from across a room. An excuse has a door in it somewhere, a way out, a *technically*. This hasn't. This is just a wall. He's walked along it from one end to the other and there's no gap.

It doesn't make him feel better. That's how he knows it's true.

---

He thinks about Gakuganji.

He's seen him once. At headquarters, a year or two ago, at the far end of a corridor. An old man, very old, in formal dress, with a long face and white hair and the slow, upright walk of someone who has never in his life been in a hurry, because things wait for him. Two younger men behind him. And on his back, in a black case, an electric guitar.

Nobody mentioned it. Kusakabe didn't either. He remembers thinking it was the most interesting thing he'd seen at headquarters in years, and that he'd never ask.

He knows the type, though. Not the guitar. The rest. The ones who believe in the rules the way an old wall believes in standing up. They don't like the rules, particularly. They don't need to. The rules are what's holding up the roof, and they've lived under that roof so long they've forgotten it was ever built. Somebody tells them a thing has to be done in a certain way, by a certain kind of person, and they put on their good clothes and come up on the train and do it.

The same man told his students to kill a boy, in the summer. Ogawa said so. Kusakabe remembers being not much bothered by that, at the time.

He'd like to hate him. It would be simpler.

He tries it, sitting on the step. He finds he can't get it to take. It's like trying to light a damp match. He's only seen the man once. Hate needs feeding, and he hasn't got anything to feed it with: one corridor, a guitar case, a white head going round a corner. You can't keep a fire going on that.

And under that, the thing he doesn't want to look at. That an old man who does what the rules say, because the rules say it, isn't so very different from a man who signs under someone else's version because it's quicker. Just older. Just further along.

*Fine,* he thinks. *Don't hate him, then. You don't have to do anything about him at all. That's always been your favourite.*

---

His hand goes to his pocket.

He doesn't tell it to. It goes the way it always goes, on trains and in meetings and in corridors, to check.

The coin's there.

He's been half expecting it not to be. He doesn't know why. As if the debt might go with the man. As if a debt were the kind of thing you could bury.

It's there. But it's lighter.

He sits with his hand on it and tries to work out how he knows that. It isn't anything he can point to. It's the way a bag is lighter after you've taken one thing out of it. Still the same bag. Still heavy. Still on your shoulder. But you know.

He knows what came out.

He doesn't think about it in words. He doesn't think about a night, or a door, or what he did with his hands. He's not going to think about that, not here, not anywhere, not ever, because a thing you don't think about can't come out of your mouth by accident. He only thinks: there's a panda, somewhere on these grounds, who is sitting in the open air and not in a room. That's all.

That's what came out of the bag.

It isn't nothing. It isn't the whole thing, either. Not close. A boy on his mother's lap, years ago, in a chair by a window. Against that, one night's work and one risk taken, which might still come back for him and might not. He can do the sum this time. He doesn't need a calculator for it. It doesn't come out even.

*We're closer to even, old man,* he thinks.

Not even. Closer.

He'd never have said it to his face. Yaga would have hated it. He'd have looked at Kusakabe over the top of those glasses, the way he looked at a doll with a crooked seam, and gone back to his sewing, and said nothing, and the nothing would have been an opinion.

Kusakabe finds he's almost smiling. Not quite.

He didn't like him much. He's told himself that for years, on trains, and it was true, and it's still true. Yaga was stubborn and proud and difficult to talk to, and he made things in meetings, and he never asked for anything back. Not once. Not one look in a corridor. Not even at the end, when asking would have made sense.

You don't have to like a man to respect that. You just have to be tired enough to see it.

He's tired enough.

---

Panda comes at four.

Kusakabe feels him before he sees him. Something large crossing the edge of the circle, from the path, coming down the steps. He doesn't turn round. He knows the weight of the walk.

Panda doesn't stop by him. He goes on down past the third step, past the second, and sits on the bottom one, a little to the side. Where Inumaki sits. He sits the way he never sits, all the way down, with his knees up and his arms round them.

They look at the yard.

A crow lands on the storehouse roof, looks at them both, and decides against it.

"Ogawa?" Panda says, after a while.

"Yeah."

"Me too." Panda's voice is quite normal. That's the worst thing about it. "He told me on the stairs. He said he thought I'd want to hear it from a person."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything. He doesn't have anything that would sit right next to that.

The light goes down a little. The cold comes up out of the step.

"He was going to teach me to sew," Panda says. "Properly. Not just stuffing. The faces. He said when I was older."

A pause.

"I don't get older. That's sort of the thing about me." He tilts his head. "I think he knew that. I think it was a joke. He didn't make many."

*He never made one I laughed at,* Kusakabe thinks.

*That one, though.*

He doesn't say that either.

He doesn't put a hand on Panda's shoulder. He doesn't say *I'm sorry*, or *he was a good man*, or *at least*. He doesn't know what any of those would be for. Panda had a father. Kusakabe had a debt. One of those is a great deal bigger than the other, and it isn't his, and there's nothing he can put into it from three steps up that would make it any smaller.

So he sits.

That's the one thing he's always been good at. Not doing anything, very steadily, for a long time. For once it's the right tool.

The lights come on along the path. The crow leaves. Somewhere up at the main building a door bangs, and then another, people going home.

At some point Kusakabe realises that it's past five, and that he'd normally be gone by now, and that he isn't going to go until Panda does. He doesn't decide it. It's just what's happening.

At ten to six Panda gets up. He brushes leaves off the back of himself, carefully, though there aren't any.

"Night, sensei."

"Night."

Panda goes up the steps, past him, close enough that his fur nearly brushes Kusakabe's shoulder. He doesn't look down. Kusakabe doesn't look up.

He waits until the steps are quiet. Then a bit longer.

Then he goes home.

---

The rest of the week goes on as if it hasn't noticed.

It hasn't, mostly. That's the thing about a building full of people who've been told not to discuss something. They don't. They discuss the weather, and stretchers, and who's covering what, and whether the kettle in the second-floor kitchen is broken or just slow. The thing sits in the middle of every conversation like a table nobody's allowed to put anything on.

Nobody asks Kusakabe anything.

He keeps expecting them to. Every time a door opens behind him, some part of him waits to hear his name. Kusakabe-san, a moment. Kusakabe-san, there's someone from headquarters. Okabe in the corridor with a sheet that has only one line on it. It doesn't come on Wednesday. It doesn't come on Thursday. He doesn't take that to mean it won't.

*It's not over because nobody's said anything,* he thinks. *It's not over until the file's closed. And nobody ever tells you when the file's closed.*

He does his jobs. A Grade 3 in a multistorey in Nerima, small and stupid and fast, two cuts. A residual check on a shrine. A morning of paperwork about the night of the thirty-first, which is still producing paperwork, and will be, he suspects, for years.

On Thursday he goes to the staff pigeonholes for his post, and there's something in the one next to his.

The pigeonholes run in names, in order. His is near the bottom. The one next to it, for reasons of the alphabet, says YAGA on a strip of tape that has gone yellow at the ends.

There's a catalogue in it. Thin, glossy, a mail-order company. On the front, stacks of felt in squares of colour, every colour, like a paintbox. Underneath, smaller: *Wool felt, stuffing, safety eyes. Trade prices.* It's addressed to Yaga by name, on a printed label, with the word *Principal* after it.

It must have been posted a week ago. Ordered by the man months before that, probably. Just a company, somewhere, sending a catalogue to a good customer, the way it always does.

Kusakabe looks at it.

He doesn't take it out. It isn't his. He doesn't know whose it is now. He doesn't think it's anybody's.

On Friday it's gone. So is the tape. There's a clean pale strip on the wood where it was, and the pigeonhole is empty, and the one below has already started to use a bit of it for overflow.

He takes his own post and goes.

---

Sunday.

The train is the same train. The six stops are the same six stops. He gets up at the right one without counting.

He has a carrier bag between his feet. Soft rice crackers. A new lavender stick, because it's been two months. A tin of the thickening powder, because it was on the list Ono texted him on Friday: *Powder. One tin. Not the big one, she doesn't like the big one.* He doesn't know how anyone can tell the difference. He bought the small one.

He rings the bell. Then the key.

The lavender reaches him first. Faint now, the old stick nearly done. He takes it out of the little bottle on the shoe cabinet and puts the new one in, before he's even got his shoes off. First thing in.

"You're early," Ono says, from the kitchen.

"Train."

She doesn't ask. She never asks. She's at the counter in her black puffer jacket, which she's never once taken off in this flat as far as he knows, writing the last line in the rabbit notebook. She finishes it. She turns it round on the counter so it's facing him, and puts the pen down beside it, square.

"She's had a good week," she says. "Mostly."

He reads it standing up.

Ono's handwriting is small and upright and wastes nothing. No full stops if she can help it. *Tue: ate all. Wed: physio Thurs moved, physio sick. Thu: confused PM, thought it was the hospital. Fine by dinner. Fri: good.* And then, under Thursday, squeezed in afterwards in the margin:

*Asked twice about "the principal." Big man, glasses. Said I don't know him. (True.)*

He reads that one a second time.

Ono is watching him. Not obviously. She's putting her bag on her shoulder. But she's watching.

"She means a man who visited," he says. "Years ago. Before your time."

"Okay."

That's all. She doesn't want the rest. He's grateful in a way that sits in his chest like something warm swallowed too fast.

"The TV was on," Ono says, at the door. "Night man had it on. News." She zips the puffer jacket up another centimetre. "I turned it off. She didn't see much."

"Thanks."

"Mm." She opens the door. She doesn't say goodbye to his sister, because she already has; Ono says goodbye once, in the room, properly, and doesn't shout it down the hall afterwards. "Wednesday," she says to him.

"Wednesday."

The door shuts.

He stands in the hallway a moment in the new lavender. Then he goes through.

---

She's in the window chair.

The light is November light, low and clean and a bit thin, as if it's been rationed. It lies across her blanket and her two hands, the one that works and the one that doesn't, and the side of her face. Outside, the car park. A man washing a white car in a sweater, very thoroughly, as if somebody important is going to inspect it.

"Hey," he says.

She turns her head. Her eyes go over him slowly, deciding.

"Oh," she says. "It's you."

He waits to find out who *you* is.

"Atsuya. You've got leaves on your coat."

He looks. He has. A wet yellow one, stuck flat to his sleeve. From the yard, maybe. Or the street. He peels it off and doesn't know what to do with it and puts it in his pocket.

"Ginkgo," she says. "That's a ginkgo. It'll smell."

"It's fine."

"It'll smell in your pocket."

"I'll take it out later."

She looks satisfied. She looks back at the man and his car.

He sits down on the stool beside her. The TV is off, in the corner, its screen dark and grey and showing a small bent version of the room. He looks at it a second longer than he needs to. He gets up and turns it so it faces the wall a little more. He doesn't know why. It's off. It can't show anything. He does it anyway, and sits down again.

*That's paranoid,* he thinks. *Fine. That's allowed.*

---

He makes the tea.

The cheap green tea, in a bag. The water in. The bag dipped. One. Two. Three. Out.

The colour of almost nothing.

Then the powder, the small tin, one level scoop, stirred in until the tea goes slow and thick. He's got good at this. It took a few weeks to stop the lumps. Now he can do it without looking, and he watches the window while his hand stirs.

He makes his own with the same bag. No powder. He drinks some standing at the counter. It tastes, as it always tastes, of hot water that once heard about tea.

He carries hers through and holds the cup for her. She drinks. Slow sips. Some of it goes down her chin and he wipes it, and neither of them mentions it, which is the arrangement.

She doesn't say it's too strong.

She doesn't say anything at all about it. She drinks half the cup, and then turns her head away, which means enough, and goes back to the window.

He takes that as a review. It's the best one he's had.

---

The afternoon goes.

He does the small things. The pills, from the plastic organiser, one at a time: Sunday's door, three white, the yellow one she frowns at, the half. He crushes them now, mostly, and folds them into a spoon of something soft, and she eats them without knowing, which is the doctor's idea and which he hates a bit, and does anyway. He checks the toilet handle, which sticks again. He fixes it with the handle of a teaspoon, which is the right tool, it turns out, and always was. He rubs hand cream into her fingers, the unscented one, and she lets him.

She talks to the room. The dog upstairs. Something about a bus. The sea, again, the red roof and the watermelon, in the same order, and he listens to it in the same order, the way you'd walk a path you know.

At about three, she stops talking for a while. He thinks she might be asleep. Then she says, not turning her head:

"How's the nice principal?"

He's holding the hand-cream tube. He keeps holding it.

"The big one," she says. "With the glasses. He came here. Didn't he come here?"

"Yeah," he says. "A long time ago."

"He took his glasses off to talk to me." She frowns a little, getting it right. "Indoors he kept them on. Then he sat down, there, and he took them off. Nobody does that. People talk to the chair." She lifts her right hand an inch off the blanket, at the room, at everyone who's ever come in it and talked to the chair. "He talked to me."

Kusakabe looks at the spot she means. The floor by the low table. There's nothing there. A bit of carpet, worn flatter than the rest, from the stool and the years.

He could tell her.

It's right there. One sentence. He's very good at one sentence. He could say it plainly, the way you'd say the bus is late. And she'd hear it. She'd hear it today, with her eyes this clear, she'd hear every word. And then she'd forget it in an hour, or in a minute, and ask again next week. And he'd have to decide again. And again. Every week, a new first time, for her. For him the same one, over and over, worn a bit flatter each time, like the carpet.

Or she wouldn't forget. That would be worse.

*No,* he thinks.

Not because it'd be hard for him. It would be. That's not why.

Because this room is about the size she can manage. A chair. A window. A car park. Some futons, when it's dry. A man on Sundays who makes the tea wrong. That's her whole world now, and it holds. It's been holding for years. And there's no news from outside it that would make it bigger. Only heavier.

He's let in enough. He knows exactly what's outside these walls this month. He's not bringing any of it through the door.

"Is he well?" she says.

"Haven't seen him for a bit," Kusakabe says.

It's true. It's the kind of true that he's been using all his life, the kind that has a fact in it and nothing else. *Traffic.* *Battery.* He knows how it works. He hates a little that it works so well, here, on her.

She nods.

"Busy man," she says.

"Yeah."

"Tell him I said hello. When you see him."

The hand cream is warm in his fist now. He puts the cap back on it. He puts it on the little table, very neatly, next to the cup.

"Okay," he says.

And that's another one. Another thing in the pocket. Small, this one. He's carrying worse.

She seems pleased. She turns back to the window. The man with the car has finished, and is standing back from it, looking at it, the way you'd look at something you made.

---

At four she asks for the radio. He finds a station with music on it, old songs, no talking, and keeps his thumb near the dial in case the news comes on at the hour. It does. He turns it off before the second sentence. She doesn't notice. He turns it on again at five past.

"There was a fire," she says, a little later. Out of nowhere. "The lady said. The night lady. On the television. A big one."

The night carer is a man. He doesn't correct her.

"Somewhere else," he says. "A long way off."

"Was anyone hurt?"

"People are seeing to it."

*Not much of an answer,* he thinks. *It's the one there is.*

She accepts it. She has no reason not to. He's her brother, and he's sitting on the stool, and the tea was right, and the lavender's new, and there is nothing in this room that is on fire.

That's the size of it. He can hold that size. Arm's reach, more or less. The chair, the window, the cup. Anything bigger, he can't promise. But this he can keep. He's been keeping it for years. He'll keep it a bit longer.

---

The light goes.

It goes the November way, early and all at once, the sky over the car park turning the grey of a pigeon's back and then darker. Windows come on across the way. Somebody on a third-floor balcony takes in a futon, finally, in a hurry, as if they've only just remembered it's there.

He doesn't turn the lamp on yet. She likes the dusk. She's always liked it.

He sits on the stool with his hands between his knees. In his coat pocket, on the hook by the door, there's a ginkgo leaf going soft, and a coin that weighs a little less than it did. He can feel both from here, which isn't possible, and he feels them anyway.

His sister starts to hum.

Quietly. Not to him. To the window, the way she talks to the room. A tune that goes up and comes down again and goes up a little further the second time, like someone climbing stairs and stopping on each landing to see if anyone's following.

He doesn't know it.

He listens for it to turn into something. A song from the radio. Something from the guest house with the red roof, or the bus, or the hospital, or none of those. It doesn't turn into anything. It just goes on being itself. Up, and down, and up a bit further.

He could ask her what it is. She might even tell him.

He doesn't ask.

Outside, the last of the grey goes out of the sky. The room gets darker round them, small and warm, the right size.

She hums it through once, and then again from the beginning.

He doesn't know the song.

He listens anyway.
