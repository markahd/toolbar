# Chapter 22. Culling Season

The dispatch board has run out of room.

It's the long whiteboard on the wall of the office by the car yard. For six years it has held one week of jobs in the coordinator's small square handwriting, with a strip at the bottom for notes nobody reads. Now the jobs go all the way down to the frame. Someone has taped sheets of printer paper underneath and carried on, and the sheets nearly reach the floor.

Kusakabe stands in front of it with his coat on.

His name is up there seven times this week. Nerima. Itabashi. A hospital in Adachi that's been half emptied and wants to be fully emptied. An evacuation centre in a school gym in Kita. A residual check on something in Ota with a question mark after it and no grade.

Other names have lines through them. Some have nothing next to them at all, which is worse, because a line means somebody decided something.

The coordinator sits under the board with her headset round her neck and a phone against each ear. She hasn't had a rice cracker in a fortnight. He knows because he's started noticing what isn't there.

She covers one phone with her hand.

"Adachi first," she says. "The car's outside. It's not Ijichi."

"Fine."

"She drives fast."

"Fine."

She takes her hand off the phone and goes back to saying no to someone, politely, in four different ways.

---

The driver is called Sekine. She's twenty-two, has been an auxiliary for nine months, and drives as if the road has personally let her down.

"Sorry," she says, at the first corner. She doesn't slow down.

"It's fine."

"Ijichi-san said you don't like talking."

"Ijichi-san's right."

She nods, satisfied, and doesn't say another word until Adachi. He likes her already. He won't tell her.

The city goes past the window, and it's the wrong city.

Most of it is the city he knows. But it's thinner. Whole streets have a gap in them, as if someone took one building out of the row to see if the others would notice. Some of the gaps are Shibuya. Some are newer.

At a crossing in Kita, a whole school class walks past in a line, holding a rope, with a teacher at each end. Their bags have name tags and a second tag in a different colour. He doesn't know what the colour means. He has a feeling it means where to send them.

Two streets on, there's a tape across the road and a policeman in front of it who doesn't look at the car. Beyond the tape the road goes on, perfectly ordinary, into nothing in particular. The light past a certain point is a shade wrong. A shade heavier. As if the air on that side is paying more rent.

"Is that one of them?" he says.

Sekine looks.

"No. That's just closed. The real ones you can't miss."

She says it like a commuter. Nine months in, and she's already got the voice.

---

He gets the Game the way he gets everything now. In pieces, from people who are in a hurry.

From Okabe first, on a single sheet, typed, under the heading *Interim Guidance*. The sheet says there are barriers. It says they're called colonies. It says there are ten of them across the country, and two in Tokyo, and staff who are not players are not, under any circumstances, to cross into one.

*Staff who are not players.*

He reads the word twice. Players.

The rest he gets from wherever it falls. From Sekine, at a red light. From two Grade 3s at the vending machines, talking low the way people talk about money. From the TV in a noodle shop, where a newsreader says *unexplained* eleven times in a minute and a half, and the man behind the counter switches over to baseball.

It goes something like this.

The man in the robes, the dead one, the one with the stitches across his forehead, did something at the end of Shibuya. Something long prepared. All across the country, people woke up different. Ordinary people. Office workers, students, a woman who runs a florist's. One morning there's a technique in them that wasn't there the night before, and nobody asked them. And some of the people who woke up weren't people who'd gone to sleep. Old ones. Very old ones, from centuries back, who'd made some arrangement with him long ago, and were waiting, and now have somebody's body to wear.

Then the barriers came down round them, and the rules came with them.

The rules are a game. Points for killing. Points for killing sorcerers. Join, or the technique kills you. Stay in, or the rules do. Ogawa has seen them written out on a sheet, and says they read like the instructions for something you'd be given at a children's party, if the party were in hell.

"Who's in," Kusakabe says.

Ogawa moves his toothpick. "Some of ours. Not many. The young ones mostly."

"Ours."

"The first-years. The Okkotsu boy's back." He looks at the door. "Nobody's saying who else."

And the boy. The one they sent Okkotsu for. Kusakabe hears he's dead, on a Thursday, from Ogawa. On the Monday after, from Ogawa, he hears he isn't, and that he's gone into one of the barriers. Ogawa tells him both with the same face.

Kusakabe doesn't know which one he was hoping for. He's careful not to find out.

He doesn't ask who else. He finds he can do that, these days. Not ask. Stand in a corridor with a question sitting right there in his mouth, and close it, and walk on.

*Not a player,* he thinks, on the way out. *First game in my life I wasn't picked for. And I'm glad.*

It isn't quite a joke. It isn't quite not.

---

Adachi is a hospital with the patients going out of it in ones and twos, on trolleys and in wheelchairs, into ambulances that come from three other wards and go back full.

Nobody has told the staff what's in the basement. They've been told *structural survey*.

What's in the basement is small and has been feeding for a week. It's grown fat on the leaving. On every worried family in the car park, every *where are they taking her*, every phone call that ends with *I don't know, they won't say*.

He goes down alone. Sekine waits at the top of the stairs with her radio, because he asks her to, and because she's nine months in and knows the difference between brave and useful.

The boiler room hums.

He lets the circle out. It goes out to its usual edge and stops there, and the curse is just outside it, wedged between two pipes, watching him. Too many joints. A face like a bruise that hasn't decided on a colour yet.

It comes in.

His hands answer. One cut, low. The thing comes apart on the blade like a fistful of lint, and then it's ash on the warm floor, and the boiler keeps humming. As far as the boiler's concerned, nothing has happened.

His palms are cold. They always are. He stands for a while until they come back.

*Grade 1,* he thinks. *Sword. Small circle. Basements, mostly.*

When he comes up the stairs, Sekine looks at him, then at her watch.

"That was quick."

"It was small."

"They're not all small," she says, and starts the car.

---

The third week of November, he goes to his sister's on a Wednesday.

There's no reason. There's no job near her. It's simply that after the hospital in Adachi he gets back to the school at half past four, with nothing more on the board for him until morning. He stands in the car yard, and his feet go to the station.

He has done this twice before in the last ten days. A Friday. A Monday.

Ono is in the kitchen when he lets himself in, writing up the day in the rabbit notebook. She looks at him, then at the calendar on her phone.

"It's Wednesday," she says.

"Yeah."

"You came Monday."

"I was passing."

Ono knows exactly where the school is. She doesn't say so. She holds out the notebook, and he reads it.

*Wed: ate most. Physio OK. Asked about the TV. Told her it's broken. (Unplugged it.)*

He looks at her.

"She was getting upset," Ono says. "The news is all the same thing. She doesn't know what it is but she can tell it's the same thing."

"Right."

"I'll plug it back in. When it's different."

"Thanks."

She puts her bag on her shoulder and doesn't go. That's new. Ono always goes.

"My cousin's in Nakano," she says. "They moved her whole street on Saturday. Buses. Two hours' notice." She hitches the bag strap higher. "If they say move, here. Where do I take her?"

It's the most she's ever said to him in one go.

He opens his mouth to say *they won't*, and closes it, because he doesn't know that, and she'd know he didn't.

"Call me," he says.

"And if you don't answer?"

There it is. He's been waiting a year for someone to say it back to him.

"I'll answer."

Ono considers this for about as long as she considers anything, which isn't long. Then she nods, once, and writes something in the margin of the notebook. He reads it after she's gone.

*If move: call brother. He answers.*

Underlined. He stands in the kitchen and looks at it longer than it needs.

---

His sister is awake. The lamp's on already. The days are short now, and getting shorter.

"You were here," she says, when he sits down.

"When?"

"Before. Yesterday."

"Monday."

"Yesterday," she says firmly, and he lets it be yesterday.

She looks at him a while. Her eyes are clear tonight. Not always. Tonight.

"You're checking on me," she says.

He doesn't answer that.

"You've got a face," she says. "Like you're counting the windows."

*I am,* he thinks. *Two. One locks properly.*

He gets up and checks the second one. It locks properly now as well. He fixed it on Friday with a screwdriver and a piece of folded card. She watches him do it, and doesn't say anything, and when he sits down again she puts her good hand on his sleeve and leaves it there.

It stays there for the rest of the evening. He doesn't move his arm.

---

Panda is in the corridor outside the dispatch office on the last Monday of November.

Kusakabe sees him from the far end. There's no missing him. He's standing by the noticeboard with a bag over one shoulder, which is strange, because Panda doesn't carry bags. Panda has never needed anything he couldn't hold in his paws.

He's talking to the coordinator. Then he isn't. He turns and comes up the corridor towards the stairs, which means towards Kusakabe.

Neither of them slows down.

It isn't far. A corridor is never far, whatever it feels like. Grey floor. The radiators going. Somebody's umbrella drying against the wall in a small brown puddle.

"Sensei," Panda says, level, as they pass.

"Panda."

That's all. Panda goes on to the stairs. He doesn't look back. He goes down them steadily, the bag swinging, and the door to the yard bangs at the bottom.

Kusakabe stands outside the dispatch office.

The coordinator looks up at him through the glass, then back down at her phones. She doesn't tell him. She has no reason to. He reads it off the board on the way past, under a sheet of printer paper headed *Colonies. Personnel. Do not discuss.*

Panda's name is there. A little further down, Maki's.

He'd known they would. Since the first sheet from Okabe, he'd known. You don't spend a year teaching a girl like Maki to fight and expect her to stay in a yard. Panda neither. He just thought someone might say so.

*Someone did,* he thinks. *The board did. That's what boards are for.*

He goes out to the car.

He thinks about the third step on the way to Itabashi. He hasn't sat on it since the afternoon he sat on it alone. He doesn't think about why. He thinks about the road, and the car in front, and the way Sekine taps the wheel at red lights. He thinks about Nerima tomorrow. It works for most of the drive.

---

The rest rule comes back on the second of December.

Somebody upstairs has remembered it exists. Twelve days on, one day off, compulsory, for anyone who isn't currently bleeding. A sheet goes up on the board with the days blocked out in yellow highlighter. It's Okabe's yellow. Kusakabe would know it anywhere.

His day is a Tuesday.

He wakes at ten past six out of habit. He lies there. He gets up, eventually, and makes the coffee, and drinks it, and then there's nothing.

He sits down on the sofa.

He knows this sofa. He knows the way the cushion gives, and the angle you can lean back to without quite lying down. He knows which spring you can feel through the cover if you sit too far to the right.

He doesn't sit too far to the right.

At noon the ward loudspeaker plays its chime, the one that's meant to test the emergency system and has never once been used for an emergency. Twelve notes, thin and cheerful, over the rooftops. He hears it. That's all he does with it.

The phone is on the arm of the sofa, face up. It doesn't ring. It doesn't need to. Nobody's going to need him today. That's the whole idea of a rest day. The board has his name blocked out in yellow, and somewhere else a car is taking someone else to a basement.

He sits.

It used to be easy. That's what he notices first. It used to be the easiest thing in the world. He'd sit down and the day would come apart quietly in his hands, the way wet bread comes apart, and there'd be nothing left by evening, and he wouldn't miss it. That was the point.

It isn't easy now.

He doesn't know when it changed. He sits there and tries to find the old shape of it, the soft grey nothing, and it won't come. Something else comes instead.

His chest is heavy.

Not sad. Not tired, though he is. Heavy, like something's resting on it. Flat across the ribs. A weight he has to breathe round. He can taste something. Not dust. Not quite. The memory of dust, at the back of the throat.

The room is light. It's the middle of the day. But the edges of it are going dark, the way they go dark when you've been lying still too long in a small space, and your eyes have stopped bothering.

He doesn't move. Not moving is the whole idea.

And he understands, quite slowly, sitting there in the middle of a Tuesday with his hands on his knees, what this is.

It's the slab.

It's the same. It's exactly the same. Lying still and letting it all go on somewhere else, without him, and calling that safe. He's done it on this sofa a thousand times. He did it once under a building. He hadn't noticed, until now, that they were the same thing. The sofa just had better lighting.

*Maybe if I sit here long enough,* something in him starts to say, *someone else will—*

He's on his feet.

He didn't decide it. There's no resolve in it. He'd laugh at anyone who called it that. It's the thing his body does when something crosses into the circle without asking. His legs simply stop agreeing to it. That's all. Sitting still has started to feel like being under the rock, and his body, which has only ever been sensible about one thing, has decided it isn't going under there again.

He stands in the middle of the room, breathing, a little stupidly, like a man who's got up to answer a door and found nobody there.

The coffee cup is on the floor by the sofa. He picks it up and puts it in the sink.

Then he puts his shoes on.

---

He doesn't go anywhere in particular. He walks.

Down the hill, past the shrine, where the ginkgos are bare now. Along the river, where one man is fishing and nobody looks at anybody.

He walks for two hours.

His chest gets lighter as he goes. He doesn't make anything of it. It's a fact, like the cold.

Somewhere around three he finds he's at the school gate.

*Of course I am,* he thinks. *Nowhere else to go on a day off. Very healthy.*

The coordinator looks up when he comes into the office. She looks at the yellow block with his name in it. She looks at him.

"You're off," she says.

"I know."

She looks at him a while longer. Then she reaches under the board, where the printer paper goes nearly to the floor, and tears off a strip at the bottom.

"Nobody's taken this," she says. "Since Thursday. Nobody wants it."

He reads it. *Evacuation centre, Kita. School gym. Reports: people waking at night, can't breathe, something on the chest. Grade unknown. Night only.*

He reads it twice.

*Something on the chest.*

"Fine," he says.

---

The gym is full of people sleeping.

Three hundred, the warden says. Families on blue mats in rows, with bags and coats in the gaps. A whole street, and part of the next one. A radio playing quietly in a corner. A child's drawing taped to the climbing bars: a house, a tree, a sun with a face, all of it in very confident orange.

They're sleeping badly. You can hear it. Three hundred people turning over and turning back.

He sits on the stage at the end with his back to the wall and the doors in front of him. Sekine sits on the steps below. She's brought a flask. It's terrible, and it's better than anything he'd have made.

At ten past two a woman in the third row sits up and makes a sound like she's been pushed under water.

He's already moving.

It's low and flat and grey, lying across her the way a fog lies on a field, and it's been feeding on exactly what you'd expect. On three hundred people lying in a strange room, being told nothing, waiting to be told when they can go home. It doesn't care about any one of them. It just likes the weight.

It feels him coming and lifts off her, and turns, and it's in the circle.

Two cuts. That's all. One to make it let go, one to finish it. It comes apart without a sound, which is the only mercy, because nobody wakes. Not even the woman. She's already lying back down, breathing easier, frowning in her sleep at something she won't remember.

He stands in the aisle between the mats with the sword still out, and his palms cold, and three hundred people sleeping round him, more easily now, one row at a time.

He doesn't feel brave. He feels like a man who's come in to fix a draught.

*Something on the chest,* he thinks. *Yeah. I know that one.*

He goes back to the stage and sits down. Sekine passes him the flask without a word.

He drinks it. It's still terrible.

---

December goes past in pieces.

A job in Nerima. A job in Ota that turns out to be two. Paperwork he fills in at three in the morning in the back of Sekine's car with a torch in his teeth, because the forms don't stop just because the world has.

He goes to his sister's on Sundays, and on Wednesdays, and twice on a Friday. Ono stops remarking on it. The rabbit notebook gets a new line at the bottom of each page. *Brother came.* As if he were weather. As if he were the physio.

News comes the way it's been coming. From other people's mouths, in corridors, without the beginnings or the ends.

The Game goes on. The colonies hold. Some of the players are sorcerers from a thousand years ago, and some are office workers, and the points go up.

Names go on lists. Some of them are names he knows. He doesn't ask about Panda. He doesn't ask about Maki. He reads the board every morning, though, top to bottom, all the way to the floor. Their names are still on it, without a line through them, and he goes out to the car.

---

On the nineteenth, a Wednesday, the radio in the dispatch office says Gojo's out.

It says it the way it says everything now. Broken up. *Prison Realm. Confirmed. Repeat, Gojo-san is.* And then a lot of people talking at once, and then the coordinator turns it down, because everybody in the room has stopped what they're doing and she needs someone to answer a phone.

Kusakabe is by the door.

He feels it go through the building. Not the radio. The people. A whole school letting its shoulders down at once, like a room after the exam papers have been collected. Ogawa takes the toothpick out of his mouth and doesn't put it back.

*Out,* Kusakabe thinks.

He waits to feel what everyone else is feeling.

Some of it comes. Relief, real and warm. He'd be lying if he said it didn't. There's a kind of tiredness that only goes when the strongest man in the country is back on the map.

But under it, colder, there's the other thing. The thing he knows because he's spent his whole life working out where the trouble goes.

If Gojo's out, someone's going to fight him.

There's only one someone.

He finds out the rest by evening. In pieces, the way he finds out everything. The night Gojo came out, something else came loose as well. Ogawa has three versions of what, and doesn't like any of them. Something with the King of Curses. A cost. There's always a cost. Nobody in the staff room says it the same way twice, and nobody says it all the way to the end.

Kusakabe doesn't try to finish the sentences for them.

He goes home. He sleeps, which surprises him.

---

The date goes up on the board on the twenty-first.

He comes in at eight and there it is, at the top, above all the printer paper, in red. The coordinator's small square writing, gone a little larger than usual, as if her hand didn't quite trust itself.

*24 Dec. Shinjuku.*

Nothing else. No grade. No team. No *All available*, not yet. Just the place and the date. Nothing to say who it's for.

He stands in front of it with his coat on.

"They agreed it," the coordinator says, from under the board. She isn't on the phone, for once. "Gojo-san and the other one. Like a dentist's appointment." She looks up at it with him. "The twenty-fourth."

"Yeah."

"One year," she says. "Since the Parade. To the day. Did you notice?"

He'd noticed. He'd noticed before she finished the sentence. A year ago he was in Shinjuku in the dark, under a sky full of Christmas lights, and his arm was bleeding into his sleeve, and he didn't know any of their names yet. Not Panda's, not properly. Not Maki's. Not the pink-haired boy's.

*Someone's got a sense of occasion,* he thinks. *I hope it isn't the King of Curses. It's probably the King of Curses.*

"Yeah," he says. "I noticed."

He goes out to the car. It's Nerima again. Something in a laundrette. He's quite glad of it.

---

That night he's in the kitchen, waiting for the kettle.

The kitchen is narrow. He uses it for two things: the kettle, and standing in. He's standing in it now, with his back to the counter, looking at the wall by the fridge.

There's a calendar on it.

A paper one, from Midori Home Care. It came in the post with an invoice last December. A brown envelope, with a card saying *Season's Greetings from all the team*, and he hung it on the nail by the fridge because the nail was there. A photograph for each month. Mountains, mostly.

It's still on January.

He's never turned it over. Not once in a year. He's looked at it every day, standing here, waiting for the kettle, and it's been January the whole time. A mountain with a little red shrine at the bottom. The same mountain, every morning, for three hundred and fifty-something mornings.

He lifts the pages.

February. March. A lake in April with blossom on the water. He goes through the whole year in one handful. Every square empty. Every Sunday empty, though there wasn't a Sunday he missed. Every Monday empty, though he paid the bills on every one. Nobody would know from this calendar that anything happened to anyone in 2018.

December is a bridge in the snow. Two people crossing it, small, with an umbrella.

He finds a pen in the drawer. It's a cheap biro from a bank he's never used. He shakes it until it works.

He finds the twenty-fourth. A Monday.

He holds the pen over it for a moment. Not long. He's not a man who needs long.

Then he draws a circle round it. Small. Neat. Just the square, and the number inside.

He looks at it.

It's the first thing he's written on the calendar in a year. A single small circle, in blue, on a page with a bridge on it.

The kettle clicks off behind him. He doesn't turn round yet.

He leaves the pen on the counter, next to it. In case.
