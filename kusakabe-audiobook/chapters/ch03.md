# Chapter 3. The Hurricane with Sunglasses

The corridor outside the old archive room is the quietest place in the school after four o'clock.

Kusakabe knows this because he has tested it. Nobody comes down here except to look for old files, and nobody looks for old files. There's a bench under the window. There's a radiator that works on one side. If you sit at the warm end with your back to the wall and a folder open on your knees, you look like a man reviewing something important.

He has been reviewing the same page for twenty minutes. It's a list of approved suppliers of paper towels.

*Critical work,* he thinks. *The country depends on it.*

He feels it before he hears it. Not through the circle. The circle is folded away, the way it always is when he's sitting down with nothing to do. He feels it the way you feel the air change before a storm. A sort of brightness at the far end of the corridor, coming closer. Cheerful. Enormous.

Then footsteps. Long ones, unhurried, with a little bounce in them.

He doesn't look up.

*If I don't look up,* he thinks, *it might be someone else.*

"Ku-sa-ka-be."

It isn't someone else.

Satoru Gojo stops in front of the bench. He's tall enough that Kusakabe would have to tip his head right back to see his face, so he doesn't. He looks at the knees instead. Black uniform trousers. A paper bag from the bakery by the station, held by the top. The edge of the blindfold at the top of his vision. Gojo wears it at school the way other people wear a lanyard, and it has never once stopped him seeing exactly what you'd rather he didn't.

There's powdered sugar on the bag. There's powdered sugar on Gojo.

"Found you," Gojo says.

"I wasn't lost."

"No, you were hiding. Different thing. Lost people want to be found." He sits down on the bench, which is not long enough for two grown men when one of them is Gojo. Kusakabe moves his folder. Gojo leans over and reads it upside down, blindfold and all. "Paper towels. Wow. Gripping stuff."

"It's for a report."

"It's from three years ago. The supplier went bust. I know because I put a hole in their warehouse." He holds out the bag. "Want one?"

"No."

"They're really good."

"No, thanks."

"Your loss." He takes one out and eats half of it in a single bite, and talks through it. It should be disgusting. It somehow isn't. The normal rules seem to have come to some private arrangement with him. "So. I need a favour."

*Here it comes.*

Kusakabe has seen this happen to other people. They walk into a conversation with Gojo holding a plan for their evening, and they walk out holding Gojo's.

"There's a hospital in the east of the city," Gojo says. "Children's ward. Something's been growing in it. Started small. Bad dreams, kids waking up crying at the same time every night, that sort of thing. Now it's fevers nobody can explain, and a night nurse who says the corridor lights keep going out." He licks sugar off his thumb. "Window people called it Grade 2 last week. Grade 1 this week. It's eating well."

"Eating what."

"Fear. Obviously. Kids in hospital at night? They're terrified. It's a buffet." He says it lightly. He always says things lightly. Kusakabe has never worked out whether that's because he doesn't feel them or because he feels them and can't be bothered to let anyone see. "Anyway, it was mine. And I can't. I'm in Kyoto tonight. Meeting. Old men. You know." He pulls a face. Even round the blindfold, the face is very clear. "So I thought of you."

"Why."

"Because you're good. And because you're here."

Kusakabe looks at the paper towels.

There are three ways out of this. He's sure of it. He's a Grade 1 sorcerer. He has been getting out of things for most of his adult life.

"I'm busy," he says.

"You're not. You're reading about paper towels in a corridor nobody uses. That's the least busy a person can be without lying down."

Fair. He tries the second way.

"There are other Grade 1s on the board. Send one of them."

"There are two this week. One's in Hokkaido with a broken collarbone. The other one I don't like."

"That's not a reason."

"It's a great reason. Also, he'd go in loud. You can't go in loud on a children's ward. You need somebody quiet. Somebody boring." Gojo points at him with the other half of the pastry. "You are the most boring man I know. That's a compliment. I don't give many."

Kusakabe considers the third way. It's the last one he has, and it's the honest one, which is why he keeps it for last.

"I don't want to."

Gojo tilts his head. For a moment he's genuinely still, which with Gojo is like a fountain stopping.

"Oh, I know," he says. "Nobody wants to. Ijichi's picking you up at ten, by the way. I already told him it was you. The file's on your desk."

"You went to my desk?"

"I went to everybody's desk. Yours was the tidiest. Very suspicious." He stands, and brushes sugar off his trousers onto the floor, and onto Kusakabe. "The night nurse is called Mori. She's apparently a nightmare. You'll love her."

And somewhere in there, between the second way and the third, Kusakabe hears his own voice say:

"What time does it start?"

He doesn't know where it came from. He was there the whole time. He didn't see it happen.

"Around three, the nurse says." Gojo is already walking away, the bag swinging. Without turning round, he lifts a hand. "You're the best, Kusakabe!"

Then he's round the corner, and the brightness goes with him, and the corridor is the quietest place in the school again.

Kusakabe sits.

There's powdered sugar on the bench where Gojo was. A little on his own sleeve. He doesn't brush it off. It feels like evidence.

*Three ways to say no,* he thinks. *Used all three. Nobody told me you needed a fourth.*

Some of the staff call Gojo a hurricane, when he isn't in earshot. Not because of the damage. Because there's no point arguing with one. You go indoors, and you wait, and afterwards you go outside and find out where your things ended up.

His things, it turns out, have ended up in a children's ward in the east of the city, at three in the morning.

He closes the folder and goes to find his desk.

---

Ijichi is in the car yard at ten exactly, with the engine running and the heater on.

"Kusakabe-san. Thank you for doing this." He hesitates, his hand on the gearstick. "Gojo-san said you volunteered."

"Did he."

Ijichi looks at him in the mirror. Then he looks at the road, which is empty, and keeps looking at it.

"I'm sorry," he says.

"Not your fault."

"No. It's quite often not my fault." Ijichi pulls out of the gate. "I find I'm still quite often sorry."

Kusakabe reads the file under the little light in the back. Eleven children on the ward. Ages two to nine. A night nurse and one agency nurse. Lights failing in the main corridor around three, never at the nurses' station. Four children with fevers of no known cause, all in the rooms nearest the end of the corridor. A playroom at that end. The hospital's facilities office has replaced the corridor bulbs twice.

At the bottom, in someone's careful handwriting, copied from the nurse's own notes: *They wake up and say something was under the bed. All of them say the same thing. Same time. They don't know each other.*

He reads that line twice and closes the file.

The city goes past. Wet roads. Konbini light. A man sleeping upright at a bus stop with his umbrella still open over him.

*Kids,* he thinks. *Of course it's kids.*

He'd rather it were an empty warehouse. He'd rather it were anything else. Not because children are worse to save. Because if something goes wrong with an empty warehouse, it's just a warehouse.

---

The hospital is big and grey and mostly asleep. Ijichi parks by a side entrance under a sign about deliveries. A man from the night administration office meets them there, in a padded jacket, holding a lanyard out at arm's length as if it might bite. It says CONTRACTOR on it. Kusakabe puts it on.

The lift smells of floor polish. The fifth floor smells of everything else.

Disinfectant first, sharp, the kind that gets into the back of your throat. Then warm plastic. Then something sweet: fruit juice from the little cartons with the bent straws. And underneath all of it, the smell hospitals have at night, which isn't really a smell. It's the sound of people trying not to make any noise.

The lights are dimmed to half. The walls have animals painted on them, big and friendly, a little faded. An elephant. A row of ducks. A giraffe whose neck goes round the corner, so you can only see its body from the lift and you have to walk to the end to find its face.

The doors to the rooms are open a hand's width. Through each gap, a bed.

The beds are small.

He knew they would be. He's not stupid. Knowing doesn't help. The beds are small and the blankets are small and in one of them, a hand is lying outside the blanket, and it's about the size of the bottom of a teacup.

He keeps walking.

The nurses' station is a curved desk under the only full-strength light on the ward. Behind it sits a woman in her forties in blue scrubs with a grey cardigan over them, her hair in a clip that's slowly losing the argument. A mug of tea at her elbow with a skin on it. A chart in front of her. The name badge says MORI.

She doesn't look up.

"You're the one admin called."

"Yes."

"I don't know what you are. I don't want to know. I've got eleven children, me, and one agency nurse who's on her break." She turns a page. "Don't wake them. Don't go in the rooms unless you have to. If you have to, knock first. Quietly. They're not animals in a zoo."

"Fine."

"Don't touch the drip stands. Don't touch the trolleys. If you need the toilet, it's the staff one at the end, and you put the seat down."

"Okay."

Then she does look up. At his face, briefly, and then at the long bag on his shoulder. She looks at the bag a second longer than she looked at him. Something goes across her face and then she decides not to have it.

She looks back at the chart.

"Room four's night light is broken," she says. "If she wakes up and cries, that's why. It's not you. Facilities said Monday." She writes something. "It's been Monday for two weeks."

He waits to see if there's more. There isn't.

He doesn't take it personally. She's been on her feet since before he got out of bed this morning, probably. Eleven children and a cold cup of tea and a man with a long bag, turning up on her shift without asking, the latest in a long line of things that turned up without asking. He'd be curt too. He usually is.

"The lights," he says. "Around three?"

The pen stops.

"Who told you that?"

"It's in the file."

"Nobody reads what I write."

"Someone did."

She looks at him again. Not warmer. Just a little more carefully, the way you'd look at a step you'd been told was loose.

"Around three," she says. "Ten to, sometimes. Never here. Only down there."

She points with the pen. Down the corridor, past the giraffe's body, to where the light is thinnest.

"Thanks."

She's already writing again.

---

He walks the ward once with the circle open.

He doesn't draw it fully. Just lets it out a little, the way you'd crack a window. The reach of an arm and a sword, wrapped around him as he walks, close and quiet. Anything that enters it, he'll know.

What he feels is residue. Not the curse. The leftovers of it. A faint chill at the threshold of each door, like the draught that comes off a fridge. It's thickest at the far end. Room four. Room five. The playroom, with its door shut and a paper sign on it with a smiling sun.

Room four's door has a name card in a little plastic sleeve. *Yui.* There's a strawberry sticker on it, slightly peeling.

He doesn't go in. Through the gap he can see the end of a bed, a small hump under a blanket, and a plug-in night light in the wall socket by the floor. A plastic rabbit, sitting up. It isn't lit. Room four is darker than every other room on the ward.

The hump under the blanket breathes fast and shallow. Not awake. Somewhere near it.

He goes back to the middle of the corridor. There's a plastic chair by the drinking fountain, the kind made for a parent to sleep badly in. He sits on it with the bag across his knees.

And he waits.

The ward at night has its own noises. A pump somewhere, beeping every few minutes, until somebody's shoes squeak over and it stops. A child talking in their sleep, one clear word, *no*, and then nothing. The agency nurse coming back from her break, young, with a ponytail, who sees him and stops dead, and is told something in a low voice by Mori, and then very carefully doesn't look at him for the rest of the night.

One forty. Two. Two thirty.

He thinks about nothing. He's good at it. It's the main thing the job has taught him. Not the sword. The waiting.

At two fifty-two the hair on his arms stands up.

---

The light at the far end of the corridor goes out.

Not a flicker. Just gone, like a mouth closing. Then the next panel. Then the next. The dark comes up the corridor towards him one ceiling panel at a time, over the giraffe, over the ducks.

Behind him, at the nurses' station, the light stays on.

Kusakabe stands. He doesn't remember deciding to. The bag is open. He doesn't remember that either.

The circle draws itself closed around him, tight and certain. Everything inside it goes very clear and very slow.

Under the playroom door, something pours out.

It's ugly. He'll think afterwards that he expected it to be. It's made of what children see at three in the morning. The shape of a coat on a hook. The gap under a bed. A face in the pattern of a curtain. All of it stitched together badly, too many elbows, legs that bend the wrong way at the wrong time. Where its middle should be there's a mouth, and in the mouth, very faintly, a monitor beeping out of time.

It doesn't come for him.

It goes for room four. The dark one. Where it's easiest.

It's fast. Faster than anything that shape should be. It moves along the floor like spilled ink finding the low side of a table.

It crosses the line of the circle.

His body is already there. That's all he knows about it. One moment the corridor, the next his shoulder against the doorframe of room four and the blade coming round, low, and the thing meeting it.

It makes a sound only he can hear, thin and high, like a kettle left on in another flat.

The second cut is the one that counts. He feels it go through. Something in the middle of it gives, the way a knot gives when you find the right loop.

Then it comes apart. Not dramatically. It just stops having a shape. A grey mist with a smell like rubber gloves, and then not even that.

Behind him, the corridor lights come back on, one panel at a time, from the near end.

He stands in the doorway of room four with a drawn sword, under a painted giraffe, at two fifty-four in the morning.

His heart takes longer to finish than the curse did.

*If anyone comes round that corner right now,* he thinks, *there's no story in the world.*

Nobody comes round the corner. He wipes the blade, sheathes it, and puts it back in the bag, and zips the bag, and stands there with his hand on the zip until it stops shaking. It doesn't take long. It never takes long. It's still the longest part.

At the far end of the corridor, Mori has stood up behind the station. She's looking at the lights. Then at him. A man in a doorway with a bag.

She looks at him for a while. Then she sits back down.

---

In room four the breathing has changed.

He can hear it from the doorway. Slower. Deeper. The fast shallow thing has gone out of it, like someone putting down something heavy.

He should go. That's the job. In, done, out. Nobody wakes. Nobody knows.

He looks at the rabbit.

He knocks on the door frame with one knuckle, very quietly, because she told him to. Then he goes in.

The night light is cheap, the kind you'd find in any hundred-yen shop. The plastic body is cracked down one side. Someone has tried to fix it with a strip of sticking plaster, which has come away. He eases it out of the socket. One of the metal prongs is loose in its housing and bent a little sideways, so it never quite meets the contact.

*Facilities,* he thinks. *Monday.*

There's a roll of white surgical tape on the trolley outside. Mori told him not to touch the trolleys. He touches the trolley.

He bends the prong straight with the tip of his door key. He presses the cracked body together and winds the tape round it, twice, tight. He tears the tape with his teeth, which he's fairly sure is against some rule. He pushes the rabbit back into the wall.

It lights.

Not much. A soft yellow, low down, the colour of the inside of a lemon. Enough to see the leg of the bed. A pair of small slippers with faces on them, side by side. The edge of the blanket.

The hump under the blanket turns over and sighs and doesn't wake.

He stands there for a moment, which he shouldn't.

Then he goes out, and puts the tape back on the trolley exactly where it was, label facing the same way.

---

"Done?" Mori says, when he passes the desk.

"Done."

"Will it come back?"

"Someone'll check. In a few days."

She nods. She doesn't say thank you. He doesn't wait for it. She has a chart to finish and nine hours of her shift gone and three to go, and none of it is about him.

"Right," she says.

He's at the lift when he hears her shoes. He turns. She's walked halfway down the corridor, to check on room four the way she must check on it every hour. She stops at the door.

She stands there a second. Looking in.

Then she goes on to room five, and he gets in the lift.

---

He writes the report in the car, on his phone, before they've left the car park.

*Grade 1 curse, fifth floor, paediatric ward. Exorcised at 02:54. No civilian injuries. Residual check recommended within one week.*

That's it. He reads it once. He sends it.

Ijichi glances at the mirror. "That's very fast, Kusakabe-san."

"Yeah."

"It's usually..." Ijichi stops himself, kindly.

"Late," Kusakabe says. "I know."

He doesn't say why. He isn't completely sure why. Something about the hand outside the blanket, the size of the bottom of a teacup. Something about not wanting that file to sit open on anybody's desk with his name on it for a week.

He puts his head back against the seat and closes his eyes and doesn't sleep.

---

Gojo comes back from Kyoto the next afternoon.

Kusakabe knows because he can hear him from the other side of the building.

He's in the staff room at the time, at the desk with the dying plant on it, which is his desk. Ijichi is at the next one along, eating rice crackers and filling in mileage forms. Two of the auxiliary staff are at the kettle. The dispatch coordinator is at the big table with her laptop open, a woman in her thirties who wears a headset round her neck and has never, as far as anyone knows, taken it off. By the photocopier, Okabe is feeding in a stack of something and watching each page come out as if it might try something.

The door opens and the weather comes in.

Gojo isn't wearing the blindfold. He's off the clock, or wants everyone to think so. Little round dark sunglasses, perched on his nose. White hair loose over them. A big box under one arm, wrapped in green paper with a shop name in gold. Kyoto sweets.

"Souvenirs!" he announces, to the room. "Don't fight."

He puts the box on the table in front of the dispatch coordinator, who moves her laptop out of the way without looking up, like someone who has done it before. Then he turns, and the sunglasses find Kusakabe, and Kusakabe knows, with complete calm, that something bad is about to happen to him.

"There he is!"

"Here I am," Kusakabe says. To his screen.

"Everybody. Everybody, listen." Gojo comes across the room and puts a hand on Kusakabe's shoulder. It's like having a bird land on you, a very large and very pleased bird. "Last night. Children's ward. Grade 1, getting bigger every night, sitting in a room full of sleeping kids. This man walks in. In and out. Not one kid woke up." He leans down to the room in general. "Do you know how hard that is? I wake children up just by walking past their house."

One of the auxiliaries laughs. Ijichi has stopped eating his rice cracker.

"It was one curse," Kusakabe says.

"See? Modest. I love it." Gojo straightens up and lifts his voice, so it carries all the way to the photocopier. "Best Grade 1 without a technique in the country. I'm serious. Somebody write that down."

By the photocopier, Okabe takes out a pen.

He doesn't write anything with it. Probably. He just holds it. Kusakabe can't see from here, and he isn't going to look.

*There's a word in that sentence,* he thinks, *doing a lot of work. And it isn't "best."*

But that isn't the part that sits on him. He's heard the other part his whole working life, in different words, from people who meant it a lot less kindly. He's used to it. It's like the weather.

The part that sits on him is the rest of it.

Because the room is looking at him now. Not long. Nobody looks at Kusakabe for long. But the auxiliaries at the kettle look, and Ijichi looks, and the dispatch coordinator lifts her eyes from her laptop for a second. She looks at him with the face of somebody moving a name a little further up a list.

And it's out now. It's been said, out loud, by the strongest sorcerer alive, in a room with a dispatch coordinator in it. It can't be unsaid. It's going to be in people's heads the next time something ugly turns up in a building, and they need a name.

Until today nobody at this school expected anything from him except that his reports would be late.

That was a manageable expectation. He was meeting it.

*Praise from Gojo,* he thinks, *is a loan. Nobody tells you the interest rate.*

Gojo has already moved on. He's opening the souvenir box himself, tearing the gold paper, offering the first one to Ijichi, who takes it with both hands like a certificate. He's telling the auxiliaries a story about the old men in Kyoto that is almost certainly not true and almost certainly will be by the end of the week.

On his way out, he comes past Kusakabe's desk again and puts a wrapped sweet down next to the dying plant.

"You were going to say yes anyway," he says. Not loudly, this time.

"I wasn't."

"Sure." The sunglasses slide down his nose. Above them, for half a second, the eyes. Very blue and very amused and not looking at Kusakabe so much as all the way through him and out the other side. Then he pushes the glasses back up with one finger. "Eat that. It's the good kind."

Then he's gone, and the room goes back to its normal size.

Kusakabe looks at the sweet. Pale green, soft, folded into a triangle, in a little cellophane wrapper.

He doesn't eat it. He puts it in his drawer.

---

On Monday, the dispatch sheet has his name on it four times.

Usually it's once. Sometimes twice, if somebody's off sick.

He stands in front of the board in the corridor and reads it. A Grade 1 in a closed-down school in Chiba, Wednesday. Something in a multistorey car park near the docks that has already sent one Grade 2 home with a broken wrist, Thursday. A shrine in the hills outside the city, Saturday, with a note next to it in the coordinator's small square handwriting: *Requested. Per G.*

And the fourth one, which was already his. *Residual check, paediatric ward. Thursday night.*

He reads it all twice. The words don't change.

Okabe comes past with his clipboard and stops, and looks at the board, and then at Kusakabe.

"Busy week," Okabe says.

"Seems so."

"Reports by Friday." Okabe taps his clipboard with his pen. "All of them."

"Mm."

Okabe goes. Kusakabe stays a little longer, looking at the line that says *Per G.*

*Best Grade 1 without a technique,* he thinks. *Also, it turns out, the most available.*

He takes a photo of the board with his phone, so he won't have to come back and look at it again. Then he goes to find somewhere quiet, and finds that the bench outside the archive room has a box of old files on it now, and somebody's coat.

---

Thursday night, he goes back.

It's late. Later than it should be. The car park by the docks took longer than it was supposed to, and he has a long graze on the back of one hand from a concrete pillar that came closer than it looked, and Ijichi drove him straight from one to the other with a plaster from the glove box and an apology for the plaster.

The fifth floor smells exactly the same. Disinfectant. Plastic. Juice cartons. The giraffe still hides its face round the corner.

Mori is at the station. Same cardigan. Different mug, same skin on the tea.

"You again," she says.

"Me again."

"Lights have been fine."

"Good."

She goes back to her chart. That's all. He didn't expect more and doesn't get it. Whatever she saw the other night, a man standing in a doorway with a bag, she's decided it isn't something she's going to carry around with her. He respects that. It's what he'd do.

He walks the ward with the circle open.

Nothing. No chill at the doors. No draught off a fridge. Just a ward at night, ordinary and tired. A pump beeping. Somebody's shoes squeaking over to stop it. The playroom door is propped open now, and inside, in the half-light, there's a table with small chairs round it and a box of crayons and a drawing taped to the wall of a very large green dog.

It's clean. It's done.

He comes back past room four.

The name card has gone from the door. Just the empty plastic sleeve, and a little tacky square where the strawberry sticker used to be.

He looks in.

The bed is stripped. The mattress is bare in its blue plastic cover, and the pillow is gone, and the slippers with faces on them are gone. The room has that particular emptiness of a hospital room that's waiting for the next person, clean and patient and not anyone's.

*Went home,* he thinks. He doesn't ask. He doesn't need to.

Down by the floor, in the socket by the bed, the rabbit is still glowing.

Nobody has unplugged it. Nobody has thought to. White tape round its middle, sitting up, giving off its small yellow light to a room with no one in it.

He doesn't unplug it either.

At the station, Mori doesn't look up when he says he's finished. She lifts a hand off the desk an inch, which might be goodbye, or might be her telling him to go away. Either way, he goes.

He walks back down the corridor towards the lift. The lights are at half. The animals are faded. Every door is open a hand's width, and behind each one somebody small is sleeping through the night.

At the lift he presses the button, and waits, and looks back.

The corridor is empty. Nobody at this end. Nobody at that end. Just the long pale floor, and the painted ducks, and halfway down, from the open door of room four, a little patch of yellow light lying on the tiles, the size of a folded handkerchief.

It doesn't need anybody to see it. It's just on.

The lift doors open behind him. He stays where he is a moment longer, looking at it.

Then he gets in, and the doors close, and the rabbit goes on glowing in the empty corridor without him.
