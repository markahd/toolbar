# Chapter 8. Rice Ball Words

The job comes in on a Thursday in the middle of May, on a sheet of paper with two names on it.

*Kusakabe A. (Grade 1, supervising). Inumaki T. (Semi-Grade 1, second year). Field experience.*

The dispatch coordinator hands it to him across her desk without looking up. Her headset is round her neck. She's eating a rice cracker very quietly, as if somebody might take it off her.

"Kita ward," she says. "Karaoke building by the station. Closed six weeks. Staff kept hearing singing from empty rooms. Grade 2, probably."

"Probably."

"It's always probably."

He reads the sheet again. The second name stays where it is.

"Why him?"

"Principal's list. Second-years need hours in the field with a supervisor. You're their supervisor." She takes another bite. "That's what homeroom means."

*Nobody told me homeroom meant anything. I thought it meant a room.*

"Car's at one," she says.

---

Inumaki is already in the car yard when he gets there, which is new. Kusakabe is used to arriving at things after they've started.

The boy is leaning on the black sedan with his hands in his pockets and his collar zipped up to the bridge of his nose. Uniform, school bag on one shoulder. He doesn't straighten up when he sees Kusakabe. He just lifts his chin a fraction.

"Kelp."

"Afternoon."

Ijichi gets out of the driver's side, bows to both of them, and opens the back door. Then he hesitates over whether to open the other back door as well, decides not to, half changes his mind, and gets back in.

They sit in the back, one each side, with the empty middle seat between them like a third passenger nobody wants to talk to.

The car pulls out. Ijichi drives through the trees and down the hill and into the city, and for the first ten minutes nobody says anything.

It's the best ten minutes Kusakabe has had all week.

He's spent a month now watching the second-years. He knows Maki's footwork better than his own. He knows Panda lies on the grass in a slightly different place each day, following the sun like a cat. He knows less about Inumaki than either of them, because there's less to know from the outside. The boy sits on the bottom step. He eats rice balls. He watches things. He says salmon, and sometimes kelp, and once in a while, if something's going wrong, bonito flakes, and that's the whole public record.

From the outside, Kusakabe thinks, they probably look much the same. Two people who don't say much, sitting as far apart as a car allows.

It isn't the same, though. He knows that much. Kusakabe doesn't talk because he doesn't want to. Inumaki doesn't talk because his words can make people do things. Tell someone to stop and they stop. Tell them to sleep and they sleep. Tell them something worse and it happens, all the way. So the boy has cut his language down to fillings. Words that can't hurt anyone. Kelp. Salmon. A whole personality, built out of a convenience-store fridge.

*One of us is being careful,* Kusakabe thinks. *The other one's just tired. Hard to tell which from the back seat.*

Ijichi clears his throat at a red light.

"The building's managed by a company," he says. "They've been told to stay out. The auxiliary on site has the keys. There's a cordon at the front." He pauses. "It's a small cordon."

"Fine."

Inumaki takes a small brown bottle out of his bag, unscrews the cap, and drinks a capful of something that smells like cherries and paint. He screws it shut and puts it back. Then he takes out a flat tin, opens it, and shows it to Kusakabe across the empty seat. Lozenges, wrapped in clear plastic, the colour of honey.

Kusakabe shakes his head.

Inumaki shrugs, takes one, tucks the tin away and pulls his collar down just far enough to put it in. The marks at the corners of his mouth show for a second, the two small curls. Then the collar goes back up.

"Throat," Kusakabe says. Not a question.

Inumaki nods.

"Every day?"

The boy thinks about it. He tilts one hand side to side.

"Tuna mayo."

*Depends.* Kusakabe nods. That's fair. Most things do.

---

The karaoke building is five storeys of tired orange tile, wedged between a shop that sells phone cases and a dentist. A sign on the roof spells out SING in letters as tall as a man. The G has been dead for a while. Below it, a banner says CLOSED FOR RENOVATION, and below that, a strip of yellow tape runs from a lamp post to a drainpipe, which is the cordon.

The auxiliary is a young man with a clipboard and a very new suit. He gives Kusakabe the keys and a floor plan.

"Singing," he says. "Mostly on four. Mostly at night. Sometimes now."

They stand on the pavement and listen.

At first there's just the street. A scooter. A woman calling a child. Then, very faint, from somewhere above, a voice comes down through the closed windows. Then another, under it. Then a lot of them. Not singing together. Each one singing something different, all of them slightly flat, all of them coming to the end of the same line and starting it again.

*Six weeks of that,* Kusakabe thinks. *I'd have closed it too.*

Inumaki looks up at the windows. Then he looks at Kusakabe.

"Stay in my reach," Kusakabe says. "Behind me, left. If I say go, go."

The boy nods once.

"Don't use it unless I tell you."

A slightly longer look over the collar. Then a nod.

Kusakabe unlocks the glass doors.

---

Inside, it smells of old smoke and carpet shampoo and the inside of a microphone. The reception counter is covered in a dust sheet. The lights are off. A rack of plastic tambourines stands by the lift with a sign saying PLEASE RETURN AFTER USE.

They take the stairs.

On the second floor, the corridor is narrow and very long, with doors down both sides. Each door has a small glass window at head height and a number. The rooms behind them are dark. In one, a television screen is on with nothing on it, a blue square glowing in the black.

The singing gets louder.

He lets the circle out on the third-floor landing, not far, just to the end of his sword and a little past. He feels the stairwell, the cool rail, the boy one step behind and to the left, breathing through his nose. He feels the building above them, not with the circle, which doesn't reach that far, but with the back of his neck. Something up there is big. Bigger than probably.

*It's always probably,* he thinks. *Until it isn't.*

Fourth floor.

The corridor is the same as the others, except every door is open.

The voices are coming out of the rooms. All of them. Twenty doors, twenty songs, none of them finished. A love song in a man's voice that cracks on the high note every time. A girl doing an idol song, a beat behind the music that isn't there. Somebody drunk, doing a ballad, very sincerely. It's like walking into the inside of a hangover.

He walks down the middle of the corridor with the sword still in its bag on his shoulder and his right hand resting on the zip.

At the far end, the corridor turns left. Past the corner, a door is shut. Light under it. Not blue. Yellow.

*That's not a television.*

Then the circle tells him two things at once.

The first is that something is coming down out of the ceiling behind them. Not fast yet. Heavy, and wet, and made of all the voices at once.

The second is that there's a man in the room with the light.

---

It goes the way these things go. Faster than he can tell it.

The ceiling tiles behind them sag and drop, and the curse comes down through the gap in a long grey pour, like dirty water through a broken gutter. It has no shape he can name. Mouths, mostly. Open ones. Some of them still singing. It hits the carpet and doesn't stop. It goes past them, low and quick, round the corner towards the yellow light.

Towards the man.

The door of that room opens. A man in a cardigan comes out with a tape measure in one hand and a phone in the other. He's frowning at the noise, which to him must be just a noise, or nothing at all. He can't see it. He's looking straight at it, and he can't see it.

The curse rears up in the corridor in front of him, all mouths.

It's seven metres away. The circle is less than two.

Kusakabe is already moving. He knows, the way you know a dropped glass is going to break, that he's not going to get there. Seven metres. Half a second too many. His feet know it. His hand on the zip knows it.

Beside him, the boy pulls his collar down.

"*Don't move.*"

It isn't loud. It's worse than loud. It goes down the corridor like a hand pressing down on everything, and everything stops.

The curse stops, reared up, all its mouths open and silent for the first time in six weeks. The man stops, mid-step, mid-frown, tape measure out.

Kusakabe doesn't stop. It wasn't meant for him, and he was already gone.

Four metres. The curse shudders. It's strong. It's pushing back against the words, and he can feel it pushing, like a tide coming in under a door.

"*Don't. Move.*"

The second time is rougher. Something tears in it.

Two metres. The circle touches the curse and the sword is out, and his hands do the rest. One cut, rising. One cut, across. The mouths go out one by one, like rooms going dark at closing time, and the corridor is suddenly, completely quiet.

The man falls over.

He was frozen mid-step, and when the words let go of him, his weight was in the wrong place. He goes down sideways onto the carpet with a short surprised sound and lands on his wrist. The tape measure snaps back into its case with a little *clack*.

Behind Kusakabe, Inumaki starts coughing.

---

He turns.

The boy is bent over with one hand on the wall, coughing into the other. It isn't a normal cough. It goes on too long and comes from too far down. When he straightens up and takes his hand away, there's blood on his palm. Not much. Enough. A dark smear across the lifeline. There's more on the inside of his collar.

Kusakabe's hands have stopped being useful. They want a curse to cut. There isn't one.

"Sit," he says.

Inumaki sits on the carpet against the wall. He gets the brown bottle out with a hand that's mostly steady and drinks straight from it this time, not bothering with the cap. He makes a face. Then he leans his head back on the wall and shuts his eyes.

Kusakabe crouches in front of him.

"Bad?"

Inumaki opens his eyes. Thinks about it. Lifts a hand and tilts it.

*Depends.*

"Liar," Kusakabe says.

Something happens at the top of the collar that might be a smile.

Down the corridor, the man in the cardigan is sitting up, holding his wrist, staring at both of them.

"What," he says. "What the hell was that. What did he *do* to me?"

---

The man's name is Kanda. He's the new manager. The company told him the building was being fumigated, and he believed them, mostly, and he needed measurements for the new carpet by Friday, and he had his own key. He came in through the back at twelve. He didn't see a cordon. He didn't hear anything strange except some sort of music, which he assumed was the fumigators.

He tells all of this to the auxiliary on the pavement, holding his wrist against his chest, very loudly. Then he tells it again, to Ijichi, who has come out of the car with a first-aid kit and is saying "I'm so sorry" at intervals, like a man bailing a boat.

"I couldn't *move*," Kanda says. "That kid shouted at me and I couldn't move. I couldn't move my *legs*. What is he, what kind of, is that a drug thing? Is this a cult?"

Nobody answers that.

Kusakabe stands a little way off, by the dead G's shadow on the pavement, with the sword back in its bag. He doesn't say anything. There's nothing to say that Kanda would believe, and the true thing would only make it worse. *A curse made of everybody's bad singing was about to eat you. You couldn't see it. A boy used up some of his throat so you'd keep your head.*

It's not the kind of sentence that helps.

The auxiliary is writing it all down. Kanda's wrist. Kanda's legs. Kanda's question about the cult.

Inumaki is sitting on the kerb by the car with his collar up and his hands between his knees. He doesn't look at Kanda. He looks at the gutter.

"Clinic," Kusakabe says to Ijichi.

"Yes. Yes, I've called them." Ijichi glances at the auxiliary's clipboard and lowers his voice. "Kusakabe-san. Because there's a civilian injury, and because it was a, a restricted technique, it'll go to headquarters. Someone will want to talk to you."

"When?"

Ijichi looks like he'd rather be driving.

"I think," he says, "today."

---

The clinic the school uses is a small grey building in a residential street, three floors, a car park the size of a tablecloth. No sign on the front that means anything. The doctor who sees to throats is an old man with very clean hands who doesn't ask how anything happened. He takes Inumaki into a room at the end of the corridor and shuts the door.

Kusakabe sits in the waiting area on a plastic chair. There's a fish tank with one fish in it. The fish is orange, and old, and appears to be thinking about something.

*Same,* he thinks, and then wishes he hadn't, because it's a fish.

He looks at the dried blood on his own cuff instead. Not his. The boy's, from when he crouched. He leaves it.

He keeps seeing the boy pull the collar down. Not the words. The half second before them, when Inumaki already knew what it would cost and did it anyway, without looking at him for permission. Kusakabe had said *don't use it unless I tell you*, and meant it, and the boy had heard it, and weighed it, and put it down.

*Good,* he thinks. *Somebody had to.*

He doesn't know yet what he's going to say about that, or to whom. He hopes nobody asks.

Inspector Sakaguchi arrives at ten past five.

He's younger than Kusakabe expected. Late thirties, maybe. Navy suit, no tie, rimless glasses, a slim leather folder under his arm and a small black recorder in his breast pocket, the kind journalists use. He walks like someone who's already decided where he's going to sit. He shows Kusakabe a card. Headquarters. Office of Field Conduct.

"Kusakabe-san. Thank you for waiting."

Kusakabe hadn't been waiting for him. He'd been waiting for the boy. He lets it go.

The receptionist finds them a side room with a desk and two chairs and a poster about hand-washing. Sakaguchi sits behind the desk. Kusakabe takes the other chair. A moment later the door opens and Inumaki comes in, collar up, a fresh white dressing taped under his jaw where the doctor has had a look at something. The doctor has found him a third chair from somewhere. The boy sits on it.

Sakaguchi turns on the recorder and sets it on the desk between them.

"I'd like to start with the student," he says.

He opens the folder. He has the auxiliary's notes already, typed. Kanda's name. Kanda's wrist. The word *restricted*.

"Inumaki-kun. At approximately two fifteen this afternoon, you used Cursed Speech in a building where a civilian was present. The civilian was affected. He's been injured. I need you to tell me, in your own words, what happened, and why you made that decision."

Inumaki looks at him.

He doesn't move. He doesn't reach for the collar. After a moment he looks at Kusakabe, just with his eyes.

"He can't," Kusakabe says.

Sakaguchi glances at the dressing. "Is he injured?"

"He's been talking."

"I'm sorry?"

"His throat. Using it costs him. He used it twice today." Kusakabe stops there. That's enough. It should be enough.

Sakaguchi takes his glasses off, looks at them, and puts them back on.

"Then he can write it down." He turns a blank sheet round on the desk and puts a pen on top of it. "In full, please. Times, distances. Who gave the instruction."

Inumaki looks at the paper.

Kusakabe looks at it too. It's a big sheet. It's very white. And he knows, the way he knows where the gate is when he sits down, what Sakaguchi wants on it. Not what happened. A version where a sixteen-year-old with a restricted technique got frightened, or excited, or careless, and opened his mouth. A version that fits in the folder.

The boy picks up the pen.

*Don't,* Kusakabe thinks. *This one's mine.*

He hates this. He wants that noted, somewhere, even if it's only in his own head. He hates explaining. Every sentence you give somebody is a sentence they get to hold up to the light and turn over and find the crack in. He's built most of his adult life around not handing people sentences.

But the boy is holding a pen over a sheet of paper, and there's blood on the inside of his collar, and Kusakabe is the one with *supervising* written next to his name.

"I'll do it," he says.

Sakaguchi looks at him. "You'll give a statement on his behalf."

"Yes."

"Go ahead."

Kusakabe puts his hands on his knees. He looks at the recorder, which is easier than looking at Sakaguchi, and he says it the way he'd cut something. Once, in the right place.

"The building was supposed to be clear. It wasn't. The man came in by the back with his own key. Nobody knew. The curse was stronger than the file said. It went for him. He couldn't see it. It was seven metres from him. I was too far. I'd told Inumaki not to use his technique unless I said so. I didn't get the chance to say so. He did the right thing anyway. Two words. The man has a sprained wrist. Without those two words, he doesn't have a wrist. Or an arm to put it on."

He stops.

The room is very quiet. Out in the waiting area, the fish tank pump is going.

"That's what happened," he says.

Sakaguchi writes for a moment. His handwriting is small and neat and doesn't slope at all.

"You told the student not to use his technique," he says, "and he used it."

"Yes."

"Without your instruction."

"There wasn't time for one."

"So, without your instruction." Sakaguchi looks up. "You were supervising, Kusakabe-san. You let a student with a restricted technique get into a position where he felt he had to use it on a civilian. That's either a failure of supervision or a failure of discipline. I'm trying to establish which."

*There's a third one,* Kusakabe thinks. *It's called a curse.*

He doesn't say it. He's already said more today than he usually says in a week, and he can feel it, the way you feel a long climb in your legs the next morning.

"He saved the man's life," he says instead. "Put that in."

"The man doesn't believe his life was in danger. The man believes a teenager drugged him. He's talking about a lawyer." Sakaguchi taps the folder. "And, frankly, I can only write down what I can verify. I can verify a civilian was affected by a restricted technique. I can verify he was injured. I can't verify a curse nobody but the two of you saw."

"Then don't write it. Write that I said it."

Sakaguchi looks at him for a long moment.

"I'll write that you said it," he says.

He switches off the recorder. He puts the pen and the blank sheet back in the folder, and the folder under his arm. He stands up.

"I'll be filing a formal complaint about the supervision on this mission," he says. "It'll go on your record, Kusakabe-san. And there'll be a note on the student's file regarding unauthorised use. That's procedure. I'm sure you understand."

"Fine."

"The note on the student's file." Sakaguchi pauses at the door. "You might want to consider whether you could have prevented that."

He nods to both of them, the exact same depth of nod for each. Then he goes out. Through the half-open door Kusakabe watches him stop at reception, sign something, and say thank you to the receptionist in a pleasant voice. Then the glass front door opens and closes, and he's gone.

*He's not wrong,* Kusakabe thinks. *That's the annoying part. Nobody's wrong. The man's wrist is sprained and the boy's coughing blood and the form only has the one box.*

He sits there for a while. He doesn't feel brave. He feels like he's put down something heavy and his arms haven't noticed yet.

Beside him, Inumaki takes out his phone.

---

The boy types something with his thumb. Not long. He stops, looks at it, deletes something, types again.

Then he turns the screen round and holds it out.

*thanks*

Lower case. No full stop. Just the one word in the middle of the white screen, the cursor blinking after it as if it might have more to say and has decided not to.

Kusakabe looks at it.

There are a few things he could say. *It's my job.* *Don't mention it.* *You did the work.* All of them true. All of them more than the moment needs.

He nods.

Inumaki looks at him a second longer, as if checking that the nod was the whole reply. It was. He seems to find that fine. He puts the phone away.

---

The doctor wants to look at the boy again in an hour, to see whether the swelling's gone down. Ijichi has gone to move the car, and then, by the look of it, to apologise to someone about where he moved it.

There's a bench outside the clinic, by the car park, under a small tree that doesn't know what kind of tree it is. The evening is warm. The sky over the houses has gone the soft grey-pink of the inside of a shell. Someone a few gardens away is practising the recorder, badly, the same five notes.

Kusakabe sits at one end of the bench. Inumaki sits at the other.

There's a vending machine by the clinic door. Kusakabe bought a can of coffee from it on the way out without thinking about it. The machine had three kinds and he picked the worst one, also without thinking about it. It's too sweet and not hot enough and tastes faintly of the tin, and he drinks it slowly anyway, because holding it gives his hands somewhere to be.

Inumaki takes the flat tin out of his bag. He unwraps a lozenge, pulls his collar down an inch, puts it in, and pulls the collar back up. The wrapper crackles. He folds it into a tiny square and puts it in his pocket instead of on the ground.

Neither of them says anything.

The recorder down the street gets to the fifth note, fails, and starts again.

Kusakabe thinks about the complaint. It'll be on his file by Monday, next to *Froze?* and a late report or two. It'll be read by somebody he'll never meet, who'll form a picture of him from it, and the picture will be wrong in a way that's mostly right. He thinks about Kanda, and Kanda's lawyer, and how long it takes for that kind of thing to go away. He thinks about the note on the boy's file, the one he couldn't keep off it.

Then he stops thinking about it, because there's nothing to do about any of it tonight, and the coffee is going cold.

Beside him, the boy swings his feet a little. The lozenge clicks against his teeth.

It's quiet. Not the empty kind, the kind that sits in his flat in the evenings with the TV on. A different kind. Two people who've said what needed saying, which wasn't much, and are now not saying anything else. Nobody filling it. Nobody expected to.

*I could get used to this,* he thinks. *I won't. But I could.*

A moth finds the light over the clinic door and starts patiently hitting it.

After a while, without turning his head, Inumaki says, very quietly, in a voice like gravel in a bucket:

"Salmon."

Kusakabe drinks the last of his coffee.

"Yeah," he says.
