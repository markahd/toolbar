# Chapter 4. Paperwork

The envelope is brown, A4, and has his name typed on a white label.

Not handwritten. Typed. Kusakabe has learned to be afraid of the typed ones.

It sits in his pigeonhole in the staffroom on a Monday in November, on top of two mission files he hasn't opened yet. He looks at it for a while without picking it up, the way you look at a dog you don't know.

*Could be nothing,* he thinks. *Could be a pension form.*

It is not a pension form.

NOTICE OF ATTENDANCE. Review of Grade 1 Field Deployment, Tokyo Region. Thursday, 2 p.m. Headquarters, third floor, Conference Room B. Attendance is mandatory. Below that, in smaller type, a list of names. His is the last one. Under the list, a line that says the panel will be "consulting with personnel on readiness, workload and conduct."

He reads the word *conduct* twice. Then he puts the notice back in the envelope, the envelope under the mission files, and the whole pile under his arm.

Out of sight. That's a start.

---

It's been a long fortnight.

Since Gojo stood in the middle of the staffroom and told everyone within shouting distance exactly what he thought of Kusakabe, the files have been arriving the way leaves arrive in a gutter. Three missions in nine days. A shrine in Chiba. A school gym in Kawasaki. A car park that turned out to be two car parks, which nobody had mentioned. Nobody told him the work would increase. The files just got thicker, with his name at the top.

*That's what praise is,* he thinks. *A longer queue.*

He sits at his desk in the corner of the staffroom, the one by the radiator that doesn't work, and opens the first file. Kanagawa. Probably Grade 2. Then the second. Saitama again.

He doesn't take the envelope out again.

He knows what's in it. He knows what Conference Room B looks like, too. A long table. Water jugs nobody drinks from. A row of men who have never drawn a blade in their lives, asking him to describe, in his own words, his approach to risk.

*My approach to risk,* he thinks, *is not to go to Conference Room B.*

---

On Thursday, he goes to Conference Room B.

Or he goes to the building. He gets as far as the third floor. That has to count for something.

The headquarters building is the kind of place that tries hard to look like nothing. Grey stone, a reception desk, a potted plant that may be plastic.

Outside Conference Room B there is a row of chairs. Four other Grade 1s are already sitting on them. He knows two by sight and one by reputation. Nobody talks. One of them is reading a newspaper so slowly he can't really be reading it.

Kusakabe sits on the end chair.

A young clerk comes out with a clipboard and a smile that has been told to be there. "The panel is running a little behind," she says. "About forty minutes. Thank you for your patience."

Through the half-open door, Kusakabe can see a slice of the room. The long table. The water jugs. A row of name cards, white, folded into little tents. He can see the backs of three heads. One of them is bald and very still. One of them is talking, not loudly, but in the voice of a man who has never once been interrupted.

At the far end of the table, a single chair faces the others. Empty. Waiting.

*That's where they put you,* he thinks. *So everyone can see your hands.*

His palms are cold. They're always cold before something. The men in there are only going to ask him questions, and he's going to have to answer them in sentences, with verbs and reasons, and at the end someone will write something down.

He'd rather be in the car park in Kawasaki. Both car parks.

He gets up.

Nobody looks at him. He walks down the corridor towards the toilets, because that's where people go when they get up from a row of chairs. Past the toilets, the corridor ends in a fire door. A cleaner's trolley has been parked in front of it, and someone has wedged it open a hand's width with a mop bucket. Presumably to air out the smell of whatever's in the bucket.

Cold air comes through the gap. A metal staircase. A slice of grey sky.

He stands there for perhaps three seconds.

*Nobody's ever been sacked for going to the toilet,* he thinks. *Probably.*

He moves the bucket six inches with his foot, steps through, and moves it back from the other side.

---

The fire stairs come out in a yard behind the building, between a row of air-conditioning units and a skip full of broken office chairs. Nobody's there. A crow on the skip watches him the way a crow watches anything, as if it's already decided what you're worth.

He walks out of the yard, round the block, and back to the station.

He does not feel free. That's the annoying part. He'd thought he might. Instead he feels like a man who has left the gas on, except the gas is a room full of old men, and there's no going back to check.

He doesn't go home. Home is where the sofa is, and the sofa would make it official. Instead he goes back to school, to an empty classroom on the second floor with the chairs up on the desks, takes one down, and sits at the window with his laptop.

He writes reports.

All three. The shrine in Chiba. The school gym. Both car parks. Times, locations, grades, outcomes. *Exorcised. No civilian casualties. Minor damage to the surface of level two.* He fills in every box. He even fills in the box that asks for "observations relevant to future assessment," which he has left blank for six years, and writes: *None.*

At 4:52 he presses send on the last one.

*Look at that,* he thinks. *On time.*

He sits there a while longer, in the dark classroom, with the screen glowing on his knees. Then he puts the chair back up on the desk where he found it, legs in the air, and goes home.

---

Okabe finds him the next morning, which is not difficult. Kusakabe is at his desk by the broken radiator, where he always is.

The cardigan today is grey. The clipboard is the same.

"Your reports came in," Okabe says. "All three."

"Yeah."

"On time."

"Yeah."

Okabe looks at the clipboard as if the clipboard might know what to do with this. Then he looks back up. "You weren't at the review."

Kusakabe says nothing. The radiator, which doesn't work, ticks anyway, the way old metal does when the building warms up around it.

"The clerk said you were in the corridor," Okabe says. "And then you weren't."

"Something came up."

"What came up?"

He could say it. *A fire door, a mop bucket, a crow.* He could say the truth, which is that he looked at an empty chair and his body decided for him. He could explain. Some people would.

"Something," he says.

Okabe breathes in through his nose. There's a long, careful pause, in which Kusakabe can see a whole speech being drafted, revised, and filed.

"The panel noticed," Okabe says at last. "One of them asked specifically about your attendance. Your name was on the list for a reason, Kusakabe-san. There were questions about your workload. Whether it was... appropriate." He holds out a file. It's thicker than the others. It has a red tab. "This was assigned this morning. By the panel. Directly."

Kusakabe takes it.

Gunma. A disused railway tunnel on a closed mountain line. Two road maintenance workers missing for nine days. A Grade 2 sent in last month came out with a broken arm and a statement that ran to half a page and didn't make much sense. Assessed Grade 1, possibly higher. Under *Personnel* there are two names.

His. And under it: *Kamo Tsubasa, Grade 2. Accompanying for field experience.*

"Kamo," Kusakabe says.

"A minor branch," Okabe says. "But still." He says *but still* the way some people say *bless you*. Automatically, and with a little bow in it. "The panel felt it would be valuable. For the young man. To observe a senior sorcerer in the field."

Kusakabe looks at the file. Then at Okabe.

Okabe doesn't smile. Okabe is not a man who smiles at things like this. But there's something about the way he tucks the clipboard back under his arm, very neatly, that is the closest he will ever get.

"Tomorrow," Okabe says. "Eight o'clock."

---

The kid is already at the car when Kusakabe gets there, which is the first bad sign.

Nineteen, maybe. Tall, narrow, with good posture he's clearly been told about since he could stand. Uniform pressed. Hair cut by someone who charges for it. Across his chest, on a leather strap, a row of small clear packs, each one filled with something dark red.

Blood. His own, presumably. Kamo clan. The family technique runs in the family's veins, quite literally, and they carry spares.

Ijichi is standing by the open back door of the black sedan, holding a paper cup of coffee out towards nobody in particular. He has the expression of a man who has been standing there for some time.

"Kusakabe-san," Ijichi says, with feeling. "Good morning."

"Morning."

The kid turns. He looks Kusakabe up and down. It doesn't take long. It's the look of someone checking a menu for a dish they already know isn't on it.

"You're Kusakabe," the kid says.

No *san*. Not even a little one.

"Yeah."

"Kamo Tsubasa." A nod, not a bow. "They told me you don't have a technique."

"That's right."

The kid waits, as if there must be more. As if Kusakabe is going to produce something from his sleeve. When nothing comes, he tilts his head slightly. "So what do you actually do?"

Kusakabe thinks about it. He thinks about the honest answer, which is: *I stand in one place and I hit whatever comes too close. I've done it for years. It's boring, and it works.*

"Sword," he says, and gets in the car.

---

It's a long drive. Three hours, the last one on roads that narrow and climb.

The kid talks. Not to Kusakabe, exactly. Near him. He talks to Ijichi about the mission file, which he has clearly read several times. Every so often he says something like "of course, with blood manipulation, range isn't really an issue," and leaves a small space after it. Like a man leaving a door open in case anyone wants to follow him through.

Nobody does. Ijichi says "I see" at intervals. Ijichi could say "I see" to an earthquake.

Kusakabe looks out of the window.

The trees on the hills are past their best. The red has gone to brown and the brown has gone to nothing, and between the bare trunks there's frost still lying in the shadows at eleven in the morning. A farmer is burning something in a field. The smoke goes straight up. There's no wind at all.

The kid catches him looking at the blood packs.

"Problem?"

"No."

"You're staring."

"Thinking."

"About what?"

*About whether you know how much of that you can spend before you fall over.*

"Lunch," Kusakabe says.

The kid makes a small sound through his nose and turns back to Ijichi. Kusakabe hears him say, quite clearly, not bothering to lower his voice, "My uncle says the ones without techniques are basically auxiliaries with swords."

Ijichi's eyes flick up to the mirror. Find Kusakabe's. Flick away.

"I see," says Ijichi.

---

The tunnel is at the end of a road that stopped being a road some time ago.

Ijichi parks where the gravel gives out. Beyond it, a cutting in the hillside, steep and dark with old moss, and at the end of the cutting, the tunnel mouth. Brick arch, blackened. A rusted gate hanging off one hinge. The rails still run into it, red-brown and half buried in dead leaves, and disappear.

The air coming out of it is colder than the air outside. That's not unusual, for a tunnel. What's unusual is that it smells of iron, and the iron smell gets stronger when he breathes in, as if the tunnel is breathing out at the same time.

Ijichi raises the curtain. The sky goes the colour of a bruise. The birds stop.

Kusakabe takes the sword out of its bag. He does it slowly, the way he always does, because his hands like the routine. Bag. Strap. Hilt. The weight settling where it lives on his hip.

The kid is already at the gate.

"Wait," Kusakabe says.

The kid turns, impatient. "For what?"

"Me."

"Why?"

Kusakabe walks up to the gate and stops beside him. "Statement said it's not at the entrance. It's further in. It drops. From above." He nods at the arch, the dark beyond it. "We go in together. You stay inside my reach. If it comes, it comes to me first."

The kid stares at him.

Then he laughs. Not a big laugh. A short one, the kind you give a waiter who has told you a joke you didn't ask for.

"Inside your *reach*," he says. "You want me to stand next to you so you can protect me with your *sword*." He pulls one of the packs from the strap and turns it over in his fingers. "I can hit it from thirty metres. I don't need to be inside anyone's reach."

"Then hit it from thirty metres," Kusakabe says. "From here."

"I can't see it from here."

"No."

The kid's jaw tightens. He looks into the tunnel, then at Kusakabe, then into the tunnel again. Kusakabe can see the exact moment he decides. It's in his shoulders. They come up and back, like a boy on a diving board who has been told the water is cold and has decided to prove that it isn't.

"I'll find it," the kid says. "You can catch up."

He steps through the gate.

*Oh, good,* Kusakabe thinks.

---

He follows.

He doesn't run. Running in the dark is how you trip over a rail and break your nose in front of a nineteen-year-old. He walks, quickly, and as he walks he draws the circle.

It comes up around him the way it always does, without fuss. The reach of one arm and one sword. Inside it, the tunnel stops being dark and starts being shape. Sleepers under his feet, rotten and soft. Puddles between them, skinned with ice. The curve of the brick overhead, wet, close. Ten metres ahead, the kid's footsteps, too fast, and the faint slosh of the packs on his chest.

And above them both, something else.

It isn't moving. That's what's wrong with it. Everything in a tunnel moves a little: drips, air, small animals. This doesn't. It's spread along the roof like a stain spreads, thin and wide, and it's waiting with the patience of something that has caught two men already and expects to catch more.

"Tsubasa," Kusakabe says. "Stop."

The kid doesn't stop. The kid lifts his hand, and the pack in it bursts, and the blood comes out in a line. Kusakabe hears it rather than sees it: a hiss, a whip-crack against brick. Then another. Then three at once, the kid firing into the dark at the ceiling, at the walls, at the shape of his own fear.

One of them hits something.

The thing on the roof notices.

---

It comes down all at once, like a sheet pulled off a bed.

Kusakabe feels it the way you feel a lift drop. His stomach goes first. The kid is outside the circle, nine metres ahead, half turned, a fourth pack already open in his hand and his face gone white. Too much blood out, too fast. His knees are going. Kusakabe can hear it in his breathing, the little stagger in it.

*Nine metres,* he thinks. *Too far.*

He doesn't decide anything. There isn't time for deciding.

His feet move. Three steps, long and flat, and the circle comes with him, because the circle is him, it goes where he goes. On the third step, the kid is inside it.

So is the curse.

The line is crossed. His hands answer.

There's a sound like a cloth tearing. There's cold on his face and a weight that goes past his ear and keeps going, and a smell like old coins, and then there's nothing coming down anymore. Just pieces. Settling. Fading into the dark between the sleepers like breath on a window.

One cut.

He's standing with the blade out and his heel sunk an inch into rotten wood and his whole arm humming, wrist to shoulder.

His palms are freezing. His palms are always freezing. The sword doesn't care.

Behind him, the kid sits down on the rails, hard, the way a puppet sits when someone lets go of the strings.

---

For a while, nobody says anything.

Water drips somewhere. The kid's breathing is loud and ragged in the curved space. Kusakabe wipes the blade on his sleeve, because it's that or his trousers, and sheathes it. His arm is still humming. He lets it.

Further in, in the light from his phone, he finds the two maintenance workers. Or what there is of them. Two orange jackets. He doesn't look for long. He takes a photo, because the file will want one, and comes back.

The kid hasn't moved. He's holding the empty pack in one hand and staring at it. His lips have gone a strange colour. Grey at the edges.

Kusakabe crouches. He takes a bottle out of his jacket pocket, the sports drink he buys from the machine on the way to every job and almost never drinks, unscrews the cap, and holds it out.

The kid looks at it. Then at him.

"Drink."

"I'm fine."

"You're grey. Drink."

The kid takes it. His hand shakes so much the first mouthful goes down his chin. The second one goes in. He drinks half the bottle without stopping. Then he puts it down on the sleeper next to him and wipes his mouth with the back of his wrist. Kusakabe watches the colour start to come back into his face.

And with the colour, something else.

"I had it," the kid says.

Kusakabe doesn't say anything.

"I hit it. You saw. I hit it first. It was already..." He waves a hand at the dark. "It was coming apart. You just finished it."

The honest answer is simple. *It was coming for your head, and you were out of blood, and you were sitting down before I touched it.*

Kusakabe looks at him for a moment. Nineteen. Pressed uniform, filthy now at the knees. A strap of empty packs. An uncle somewhere who thinks men like Kusakabe are auxiliaries with swords. A whole family waiting at home to hear how it went.

"Okay," Kusakabe says.

He stands up. He offers a hand. The kid doesn't take it. He gets up on his own, slowly, using the wall, and walks out of the tunnel ahead of him, very straight.

---

In the car, the kid makes a phone call.

He makes it in the back seat, with his head turned towards the window, as if that makes it private. It doesn't. The car is small and Ijichi drives very quietly.

"Yes. Yes, it's done. Grade 1, they said, maybe higher." A pause. "No, I'm fine. A bit tired. I used a lot." A longer pause. A laugh, a real one this time, warm and relieved. "Yes. I did. The other one was there. He came in at the end."

Kusakabe looks out of the window.

The frost has gone from the shadows now. The farmer's fire has burned down to a grey smear in the field. A small truck passes them going the other way, stacked with firewood, an old woman at the wheel who doesn't look at them at all.

"I'll tell you everything tonight," the kid says. "Yes. Thank you, Uncle."

He hangs up. He puts the phone in his lap and closes his eyes, and inside ten minutes he's asleep, mouth slightly open, head against the glass.

Ijichi's eyes find Kusakabe in the mirror again. This time they stay.

"Kusakabe-san," he says, very low. "The report. For a joint mission, the junior officer drafts it. You countersign." He swallows. "I just... I thought you should know. That's the procedure."

"I know."

"He'll write..."

"I know."

Ijichi's hands shift on the wheel. Ten to two, then back. "If you wanted to submit a separate statement, there's a form. It's not common, but there's a form. I could find it for you."

Of course there's a form. There's always a form.

Kusakabe thinks about the form. He thinks about filling it in. He thinks about sitting across a table from someone who will read both versions and look up and say *So which is it?* and then wait, the way Okabe waits, for him to explain. About the kid's uncle, who will hear about it, and the kid's uncle's friends. About the kid, nineteen, who will have to sit on a chair at the end of a long table and say *I sat down. He saved me. I lied on the phone.*

He thinks about how many sentences all of that would take.

"It's fine," he says.

Ijichi drives for another kilometre in silence.

"Yes, Kusakabe-san," he says finally, in a voice that makes it clear he doesn't think so, and is sorry, and will never mention it again.

---

The draft comes on Tuesday.

It's in his pigeonhole in a plain brown envelope, his name handwritten on it this time, which is somehow worse. Two printed pages and a yellow sticky note in Okabe's careful hand. *Countersignature required. Return by Friday 5 p.m.*

He takes it to his desk by the radiator and reads it.

*Upon arrival, Grade 2 Kamo assessed the site and determined the target to be located within the tunnel's upper structure. Grade 2 Kamo advanced to engage. Grade 1 Kusakabe remained at the entrance.*

He reads on.

*Grade 2 Kamo located and engaged the target using inherited technique (blood manipulation), inflicting significant damage. Grade 1 Kusakabe entered the tunnel following the initial engagement and assisted in the final exorcism of the weakened target. Grade 2 Kamo sustained no injuries but experienced fatigue due to technique expenditure.*

*Remains of the two missing workers were located. See attached.*

That's it. Nothing false, exactly. That's the clever part. He did stay at the entrance. For three seconds. He did enter after the first engagement. He did assist.

*Assisted,* he thinks. *Fair enough. I assisted it into four pieces.*

He sits back.

There's a second sheet underneath. A copy of the draft, the same text, but this one has been somewhere before it came to him. Someone has stamped it with a received date, Monday, and initialled it. And in the margin, beside the line *Grade 1 Kusakabe remained at the entrance*, someone has written in pencil, very small, very neat, the way men write when they know the paper will be kept:

*Froze?*

Just that. One word and a question mark.

He looks at it for a long time.

He doesn't know whose handwriting it is. He could guess. A bald head, very still, at the end of a long table. A man who has never once been interrupted. A man who noticed, last Thursday, that one of the chairs in the corridor was empty when the clerk came out to call the last name on the list.

*Froze.*

It's almost funny. Two weeks ago, Gojo stood in a room and shouted that he was the best Grade 1 without a technique, and the work doubled. Now a man he's never spoken to has written one word in pencil, and it'll be in his file long after the shouting's forgotten. That's the thing about paper. It doesn't raise its voice. It doesn't need to.

He could go upstairs. He could find Okabe and ask for Ijichi's form. He could sit on the chair at the end of the table and say, in sentences, with verbs: *No. It came down from the roof. He was nine metres in and out of blood. I took three steps and cut it once. I did not freeze.*

He imagines how long the meeting would be.

*Somebody upstairs thinks I froze,* he thinks. *Somebody upstairs already thought I was an auxiliary with a sword. It's not much of a fall.*

It costs something. He knows it does. He can feel it, small and exact, like a splinter he's decided to leave in. One day someone will open that file looking for a reason, and there it'll be. In pencil. Waiting.

He takes a pen out of the drawer.

At the bottom of the second page there are two lines. The first one is signed already, in a quick confident hand that slopes a little forward, as if it's in a hurry to get somewhere. *Kamo Tsubasa.*

Under it, the second line. Empty.

He doesn't read it again. Once was enough.

He signs. Kusakabe Atsuya. Small, plain, perfectly legible, on the line under the kid's. The pen scratches once and stops.

It's Tuesday. It isn't due till Friday.

*Early,* he thinks. *Don't tell anyone.*
