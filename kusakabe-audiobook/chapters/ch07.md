# Chapter 7. Homeroom

Yaga tells him on the last Friday of March, in the principal's office, without looking up from a felt bear.

"Second-years," Yaga says. "From April. Homeroom."

Kusakabe stands just inside the door. He hasn't been asked to sit, and he wouldn't anyway. Sitting turns a sentence into a meeting.

The bear has one ear. Yaga is sewing on the other one with a curved needle and very small stitches, the way a surgeon would, if surgeons were the size of a vending machine and wore sunglasses indoors.

"Gojo has the first-years," Yaga says. "Okkotsu is abroad for the year. That leaves three."

"I know who it leaves."

"Then you know them."

He does, by sight. Everybody does. Last year they were the first-years he walked past on the way to the car yard. The girl with the glasses and the long staff, who trained like the staff owed her money. The panda. The quiet boy with his collar zipped up over his mouth, who never said anything you could use.

"I don't teach," Kusakabe says.

"You've taught for six years."

"Odd jobs. Fill-ins."

"Now it's one job." Yaga bites the thread. "They need someone who turns up."

*I don't turn up. I arrive, eventually. It's different.*

He doesn't say it. He stands there and his hand goes into his jacket pocket on its own and finds nothing, and checks the nothing anyway.

Yaga turns the bear round to look at its face. Satisfied, or not. It's hard to tell with Yaga. He sets it on the desk, on a pile of what looks like budget papers, where it sits looking at Kusakabe with its two black buttons.

"Monday the ninth," Yaga says. "Nine o'clock."

"Fine."

That's all. Yaga picks up another piece of felt. Kusakabe lets himself out.

In the corridor he stands for a moment with his hands in his pockets.

*Six years of avoiding a class of my own. Ended by a bear.*

---

Monday the ninth, he's eleven minutes late.

He doesn't plan it. He leaves at the usual time and walks the usual way, and somewhere behind the shrine the morning just gets longer than it should. It's a talent. Some people can't be late if they try.

The training yard is at the back of the school, down a slope from the main buildings. A long flat rectangle of packed earth, grass worn away to nothing in the middle and left alone at the edges. Stone steps lead down to it from the path. There's a storehouse at one end with its doors open, and a line of cherry trees at the other, dropping the last of their petals onto everything.

His class is down there. More or less.

Maki Zen'in is in the middle of the yard, swinging a staff in long flat arcs, her glasses pushed up hard on her nose, her ponytail whipping round a half-second after her head does. She's been at it a while. There's sweat on her neck and a scuff in the earth around her where her feet have been turning.

Panda is lying on the grass at the edge, on his back, paws folded on his stomach, looking at the sky. He is very large and very round and very much a panda. In the sun he looks like something that would be in a brochure.

Toge Inumaki is sitting on the bottom step with his knees up and his collar zipped to the bridge of his nose. He's watching a petal on his shoe.

"Salmon," he says, to nobody.

Kusakabe comes down the steps.

"You're late," Maki says. She doesn't stop swinging.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Traffic."

The staff stops. She looks at him over her glasses.

There's no traffic between his flat and the school. There's a back road with nothing on it but a man who sweeps leaves, and a path through the trees that ends at the side gate. She's seen him come up that path. Everybody has. He's been coming up it at twenty past nine for six years.

She knows he walked. He knows she knows. For a second he thinks she's going to say it.

She doesn't. She snorts, once, through her nose, and starts swinging again.

That's the first day of the arrangement. He doesn't explain himself. They don't push. Nobody signs anything.

"Morning, sensei," Panda says, from the grass, without lifting his head.

"Morning."

Inumaki raises one hand, palm out, and lowers it again.

Kusakabe stands at the bottom of the steps and looks at the three of them, and they look back at him, or at least two of them do. Maki's still swinging.

He realises he doesn't have a plan.

He's had six weeks to make one. He had a notebook out for it twice. Both times he wrote the date at the top and nothing under it.

*Homeroom teacher,* he thinks. *Eleven minutes in charge of three people and I've already lied to one of them.*

"Right," he says. "Carry on."

Panda turns his head on the grass to look at him. "Carry on doing what?"

"What you were doing."

"I was lying here."

"Then you're doing well."

Panda considers this, and seems to find it fair, and goes back to looking at the sky.

Kusakabe sits on the third step from the bottom, two steps above Inumaki, in the strip of shade from the storehouse roof, and watches Maki work.

---

It goes like that for a few days.

He's not sure what the others expected. Something, probably. Last year they had Yaga in the mornings and Gojo when Gojo felt like it, and between them that's a lot of man. A lot of noise and size and opinion. Now they have him, sitting on a step.

He watches.

That's most of it, the first week. He watches Maki with the staff, and then with a pair of short batons, and then with something long and curved from the storehouse that he's fairly sure isn't on the school's inventory. She goes through weapons the way other people go through pens. Everything she picks up, she learns. Everything she learns, she drills until it stops being interesting, and then she drills it some more, to punish it.

She has almost no cursed energy. He knows that much without anyone telling him. You can feel it, or rather you can feel the lack of it, a kind of flatness around her, like a room with the heating off. She can't even see curses without the glasses. Everything she does, she does with her body and with the tools she carries. Nothing handed to her. Nothing she was born with that anyone wanted.

He doesn't think about that for long. It's too close to something.

On the Wednesday, he sees it.

She comes in off a feint with the staff, a good feint, low and fast, and just before the real strike her back heel comes up off the ground. Not much. A finger's width. It's there for maybe a tenth of a second, and then her weight goes forward and the staff comes round.

He watches her do it four more times, to be sure.

"Your back heel lifts before you strike," he says. "They'll see it coming."

She stops. She looks at her feet, then at him.

She grunts.

The next time she comes in, the heel stays down. And the time after. The strike comes from nowhere now, out of a body that was standing still a moment ago.

She doesn't say thank you. He doesn't wait for it. He sits back on the step.

*That's a day,* he thinks. *One sentence. Paid in full.*

---

There's paperwork, too. There's always paperwork.

On the Thursday, Okabe finds him in the staff room and puts a blue binder on his desk. Okabe is in a brown cardigan today, which is new, or new to Kusakabe. It doesn't seem to have made him happier.

"Homeroom register," Okabe says. "Attendance, daily. Mission eligibility, monthly. Progress notes, end of each term. A paragraph per student is sufficient."

"A paragraph."

"Sufficient," Okabe says, "is the minimum."

Kusakabe opens the binder. Four pages, one per student, each with a photograph clipped to the corner. Maki, glaring at the camera as if it had said something. Panda, with what he would swear is a smile. Inumaki, collar up, eyes only.

The fourth page is Okkotsu Yuta. Thin face, dark hair, a look of someone apologising for being photographed. Across the top, in red stamp: *Overseas assignment. Not resident.*

He's heard the name, of course. Everybody has, since December. The boy who stood in the middle of all that and came out the other side. The hero of the night, people say, in the same breath as Gojo. Now he's somewhere far away doing something nobody will tell Kusakabe about.

*Fine by me. One less person to be late for.*

"The attendance for this week was due yesterday," Okabe says.

"It's Thursday."

"Yesterday was Wednesday."

Kusakabe looks at him. Okabe looks back. Neither of them is going to win this, and both of them know it, and somehow that doesn't stop either of them.

"Tomorrow," Kusakabe says.

Okabe makes a small note on his clipboard. Then he goes, and Kusakabe sits with the binder open in front of him, looking at three faces that are all, in their different ways, looking at him.

He fills in the attendance. Present, present, present. It takes forty seconds. He doesn't know why he didn't do it yesterday. He never knows.

---

Inumaki says "Salmon" a lot.

That's the second thing Kusakabe learns. The first is that the boy almost never says anything else. Rice-ball fillings. That's his whole vocabulary, out loud. Salmon. Bonito flakes. Kelp. Tuna mayo. It's not a joke, or not only one. His technique is in his voice. The marks on his face, when the collar slips, say so: a little curl at each corner of the mouth like a seal on a letter. If he tells a person to stop, they stop. If he tells them something worse, that happens too. So he says salmon, and kelp, and the words can't hurt anyone.

Panda translates, sometimes, unasked.

"Kelp is hello," Panda says, on the Friday. "Mostly. Salmon is yes. Or fine. Or that's good. Or he's agreeing with something nobody said out loud."

"Bonito flakes?"

"No. Or don't. Or that's a bad idea." Panda thinks. "Or he wants a rice ball."

Inumaki, on the step, gives Panda a long look over his collar.

"Bonito flakes," he says.

"See," Panda says. "That one's no."

Kusakabe nods.

He finds he doesn't need much translating, in the end. Inumaki's words are like weather. You don't need the forecast. You can look out of the window. Salmon on a bright morning means the morning is bright. Salmon to a falling petal means the petal's doing well. The boy says it to nobody, often, the way some people hum.

On the Friday afternoon, Kusakabe comes back from the vending machine with a can of coffee and sits on his step. Inumaki is two steps down. He doesn't turn round.

"Kelp," he says.

"Afternoon," Kusakabe says.

That's the whole conversation. It's one of the better ones he's had this year.

---

The second Monday it rains, so they have to use the classroom.

It's on the first floor of the old wooden building, at the end, with windows down one side that look out on wet trees. Four desks in a row. Somebody has pushed the fourth one against the wall, chair tucked in, nothing on it. Okkotsu's, presumably. Nobody sits at it and nobody mentions it.

Kusakabe stands at the front. There's a teacher's desk, and a blackboard with the ghost of last year's writing on it, and a timetable pinned to the wall that says *Mon. 1st period: Theory (Homeroom)*.

*Theory,* he thinks. *I have one theory. It's that I'd rather be outside.*

He has a textbook, at least. It's the one the school issues, blue cover, the grading system for curses laid out in tables. He opens it at the chapter on assessment in the field and reads out the first paragraph. He reads it slowly, so it takes longer.

Panda raises a paw.

"We did this last year."

"Do it again."

"We did it again in February."

Kusakabe closes the book.

The rain gets heavier. It runs down the windows in sheets, and the room goes dim, and for a while nobody says anything at all. It's not uncomfortable. It's the most restful lesson he's ever taught. He suspects that isn't the goal.

"How'd you get Grade 1?" Maki says.

She's sitting back with her arms folded, chair on two legs. She's not looking at him. She's looking at the window, at the rain, as if the question just happened to fall out of her.

"Slowly," he says.

"No technique."

"No."

"Nothing from a clan."

"No."

"So how?"

He thinks about how to answer that. There's a long answer. It has years in it, and the same drill done ten thousand times in an empty dojo, and a lot of nights he doesn't want to describe to a sixteen-year-old in a wet classroom.

"Practice," he says.

She looks at him then. Properly. Through the glasses, with her chin down, the way you'd look at a door to see if it's locked.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one there is."

She lets the chair come down onto four legs with a bang. She doesn't say anything else. But he sees her file it, the way she files a new weapon, and he knows, with a small sinking feeling, that it's going to come back.

Inumaki, at the end of the row, looks from one of them to the other.

"Tuna mayo," he says, thoughtfully.

"What's that one?" Kusakabe asks Panda.

Panda shrugs. "Depends."

---

Panda finds him on the grass in the second week.

It's a Tuesday. Maki has gone to the storehouse to argue with a chain. Inumaki is asleep on the top step with his head on his bag, which is either a skill or a technique. Kusakabe has come down off his step for once, because the shade has moved and he couldn't be bothered to move with it. He's sitting on the slope at the edge of the yard with his legs out in front of him.

Panda drops down beside him. The ground shakes slightly. Panda lies back with a long contented sound, like a sofa being sat on.

They look at the sky. There's a single cloud in it, going nowhere.

"Sensei."

"Mm."

"You always sit with the gate in front of you."

Kusakabe doesn't answer.

"The steps. The bench by the office. The end of the table in the staff room." Panda says it lightly, like a man listing birds. "Gate in front. Wall behind."

*Of course the panda notices. The panda has nothing to do all day but notice.*

"Habit," Kusakabe says.

"Okay."

That's the thing about Panda, he's learning. Panda says *okay* and means it, and also doesn't, both at once, and leaves you to decide which. He's put together out of cloth and cursed energy and a lot of patience, and he's made by the same pair of hands that made a thing Kusakabe doesn't think about at school. Not ever. Kusakabe looks at the cloud instead.

"Sensei. You ever think about quitting?"

"Every day."

"Why don't you?"

He means to say something flat. Something that closes the door. Instead, for some reason, he actually thinks about it. Out on the grass, in the sun, with the panda breathing beside him like the sea.

Money, mostly. His sister. The carer's invoice, higher now. The debt, which isn't the kind of thing you can pay from a different job. The fact that he's good at exactly one thing, and the thing is this.

"Haven't found anything else that pays like this," he says, "and lets me complain this much."

Panda laughs. It's a big, warm sound, the kind that makes people at the far end of a train carriage look round and smile.

Kusakabe doesn't laugh.

It wasn't entirely a joke. He thinks Panda knows that. Panda turns his head on the grass and looks at him for a moment, the round black eyes giving nothing away, and then turns back to the cloud.

"Same, kind of," Panda says.

"You don't get paid."

"No. But I complain a lot."

Up on the top step, without waking, Inumaki says, "Salmon."

---

Maki starts asking on the Wednesday of the second week.

"Spar with me."

He's on his step. She's standing at the bottom of it with the staff upright beside her like a flagpole.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Busy."

She looks at the can of coffee in his hand, and the step, and the general shape of his afternoon. She doesn't say anything. She doesn't need to. She goes back to the middle of the yard and hits the air very hard for twenty minutes.

On the Thursday she asks again. "Properly, this time. Not watching. Fighting."

"Later."

"When's later?"

"Not now."

On the Friday morning she doesn't ask. She just watches him come down the steps, eleven minutes late again. Her eyes follow him all the way to his step, and he can feel them, like a draught on the back of the neck.

He knows what she wants. It isn't complicated. She's spent her whole life being told what she can't do by people who were born with more than her. Now someone's put a man in front of her who has no technique and a Grade 1 anyway. She wants to see it. She wants to stand inside it and find out how it's done, so she can take it apart and use the pieces.

He understands that. He'd want it too.

He still isn't going to do it.

There's no half version of what he does. That's the problem. He could swing a practice sword at her, slow and polite, and she'd know in a second it was a lie, and she'd hate it more than this. And the other thing, the real thing, the circle, doesn't know the difference between a student and a curse. It doesn't do practice. It answers. He's not putting her inside it to find out how hard.

*And she'd see,* he thinks. *That there's nothing else. Just the circle and a lot of years. No secret. Nothing to take apart.*

He doesn't know which of those two reasons is the real one. He doesn't look too hard.

On the Friday afternoon she asks the last time.

It's late, the light going long across the yard. Panda is sitting up for once, doing something careful with a bandage on his paw. Inumaki is on the bottom step, eating a rice ball, which feels like it might be a statement. Maki has been working with a bokken all afternoon, and she walks over now and stops in front of the steps, and holds it out to him handle first.

"Seriously," she says. "Not one sentence from a step. Get down here."

"No."

"Why?"

"Don't want to."

The yard goes quiet. Panda's paws stop on the bandage.

Maki lowers the bokken. She looks at him the way she looks at a weapon she's decided isn't worth learning.

"You know what you are?" she says. Not loud. She doesn't need loud. "You're lazy. That's all. Everybody says you're good, and maybe you are, I don't know, because nobody's ever seen you try. Grade 1. Sitting on a step with a can of coffee. That's all you'll ever be."

"Maki," Panda says.

"Bonito flakes," says Inumaki, quietly, through rice.

She doesn't look at either of them. She's looking at Kusakabe, waiting for something. For him to get angry. For him to stand up. For a reason.

He looks back at her.

"Probably," he says.

Something closes in her face. She turns round and walks back into the yard and starts working the bokken again, harder than before, every strike landing on nothing with a sound like a door slammed in another building.

Panda looks at him. Kusakabe drinks his coffee. It's cold. He drinks it anyway.

*That's fine,* he thinks. *She's allowed. She's right about the step.*

It's fine. He keeps telling himself that it's fine, and his face keeps agreeing, and that's the important part. The face is the part that's on show.

Underneath, though, something has gone in. Small and sharp. Not deep. Just there, in a place he can't quite reach to pull it out.

He's been called worse. By clan men, by officials, by curses, if you count the noises they make. None of it stuck, because none of it was from anyone who'd watched him. She's watched him for two weeks. She's taken one of his sentences and put it in her feet. And she's looked at all of it and decided *lazy*.

*Not a bad word for it,* he thinks. *From the outside.*

He stays on the step until the end of the day, because getting up and leaving now would be its own sentence, and he doesn't want to say it.

---

That night he does the register.

He does it at the kitchen table, which he never does. The binder open, a pen, the lamp on. Present, present, present, for the Friday. He signs the bottom.

Then he turns to Maki's page and looks at the space for progress notes. End of term, Okabe said. A paragraph is sufficient. It's April. There's no need to write anything for months.

He writes: *Back heel. Fixed.*

He looks at it. It isn't a paragraph. It isn't sufficient. It's two words, and one of them is a part of a foot.

*Lazy,* he thinks, and finds the word is still there, sitting where she put it. He turns it over. He doesn't argue with it. It's hard to argue with something you've been practising for years.

He doesn't put the TV on. He sits at the table for a while with his hands flat on either side of the binder, looking at the photograph of a girl glaring at a camera.

He doesn't feel guilty, exactly. He doesn't feel much of anything he could name. Just the small sharp thing, not going anywhere.

At eleven he closes the binder and goes to bed and lies there, and the water stain in the corner is too dark to see, which is something.

---

Monday he's late.

Nine minutes, this time. It's an improvement nobody will notice. He comes down the path from the trees and through the side gate and down the stone steps, and he doesn't know, until he's on them, what he's going to find at the bottom.

Maki is in the middle of the yard with the staff. Panda is on the grass. Inumaki is on the bottom step, collar up, watching a beetle cross the stone.

"You're late," Maki says, without stopping.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

A beat. The staff keeps going round.

"Traffic," he says.

She snorts. Once, through her nose.

That's all. She doesn't look at him. She doesn't look away from him either. She just keeps working, and the arrangement, which he'd half thought was over, turns out to be still standing, a little bent, a little scuffed, like everything else in this yard.

He doesn't know what that means. He decides not to ask.

He goes to his step. The third from the bottom, in the shade. The gate in front of him. The wall behind.

"Kelp," says Inumaki, without turning round.

"Morning."

---

Late in the afternoon, the light comes in low under the cherry trees and lays itself across the yard in long gold strips.

Maki and Panda are sparring. Not hard, not soft. Panda is enormous and patient and much faster than anything that shape has a right to be, and Maki goes at him with the staff like a woman cutting a path through a hedge. Inumaki is down off the step for once, circling them, darting in when there's an opening, slapping Panda on the back and dancing away. Panda roars, mostly for show. Maki laughs, a short, surprised sound, as if it got out without permission.

Kusakabe watches from the step.

Maki comes in off a feint. Low, fast. The back heel stays down.

The staff catches Panda square in the stomach, and he sits down hard on the grass with a sound like a beanbag dropped from a height. Inumaki says "Salmon!" very loudly, delighted, to all of them at once.

A few petals come down off the trees and land in the dust and stay there.

Kusakabe sits with his elbows on his knees and a can of coffee going warm between his hands.

For about the length of a breath, he doesn't want to be anywhere else. Not his sofa. Not a hot spring. Not some other job in some other city where nobody's ever heard of him.

Just here. This step. These three.

Then Maki looks up at him, sees him watching, and glares, and the moment goes.

He drinks his coffee.

*Still lazy,* he thinks. *Still here, though.*
