# Chapter 23. Training Yuji

The sheet is in his pigeonhole on a Monday, a week before Christmas Eve, folded once.

Not the dispatch board. Not a red tab. One page, handwritten, in a fast square hand he doesn't know, as if whoever wrote it had six of these to do before lunch and this was the fourth.

*Afternoons. Two till dark. The yard. Itadori Y. Reaction, footwork, reading. Until further notice. Kusakabe A.*

He reads it standing up, with people going past behind him.

*Itadori Y.*

He has seen that name on paper for six months. Mostly at the bottom of things. A line in a casualty list. A note on a dispatch sheet. Dead, a month ago, according to Ogawa. Then not dead, and gone into a barrier. And once, six weeks ago, at the top of a page that said what ought to be done about it, and how soon. He'd agreed with that page. Out loud.

He folds the sheet back along its crease and puts it in his coat.

He waits for the part of him that finds the back door.

It doesn't come. That's the odd thing. A year ago he'd have had three ways out before he reached the end of the corridor. A job in Chiba. A knee. A Sunday. He knows every fire door in this building and what's wedged against it. He's just stopped walking towards them. He couldn't say when. Somewhere between a slab of concrete and a paper calendar.

*Feet,* he thinks. *Fine. I can do feet.*

---

Two o'clock. He's seven minutes late.

The boy is already there.

He's alone in the middle of the yard, doing press-ups on his knuckles on the packed earth, counting under his breath. The count is somewhere past a hundred and not slowing. The leaves have been raked. Somebody's done that since last month. Kusakabe doesn't know who.

He stops at the top of the steps.

This is the thing he managed, all year, not to have. Pink hair across a courtyard. A back, kneeling in grey rubble. A face that wasn't the boy's, with too many eyes in it. He's been very good at it. A whole year of corridors timed to the minute. A whole year of the long way round.

The count stops at a round number. The boy gets up in one movement, without using his hands, brushes his knuckles on his trousers and comes over at a jog. He stops a polite distance below the bottom step and bows from the waist. Properly. All the way down, and a beat at the bottom, the way an old man teaches a boy to bow.

"Itadori Yuji," he says. "First year. Thank you for doing this."

He straightens up.

And there it is. The face.

It's ordinary. That's the first thing. Fifteen, and looks it. Broad through the shoulders, the way a boy who's played every sport going is broad. The pink hair is growing out a little darker at the roots. The uniform collar is whole. Kusakabe last looked at that collar from thirty metres away, burnt at the edge, because looking anywhere else would have been a movement.

The eyes are brown. Just brown.

Under each one there's a small line, healed pale, like a cut that closed neatly and left a seam.

Kusakabe knows what opened there.

He looks at it for exactly as long as he can stand, which turns out to be longer than he'd have guessed. Then he looks at the two brown eyes instead. They're waiting for him to say something.

*So that's what you look like,* he thinks. *Took me a whole year not to find out.*

His circle is out before he's decided anything. It always is, near a thing he's afraid of. He reaches through it, quietly, for the weight he felt on Meiji-dori. The thing with no edge that everything leaned towards.

There's a boy.

A warm, solid, heavy-boned boy, standing just past the edge of his reach with his hands at his sides. Breathing a bit fast from the press-ups. That's all there is in the circle.

He doesn't know what that means. Nobody's told him, and he hasn't asked. He lets the circle go.

"Kusakabe," he says.

"I know. Panda-senpai said." The boy nods, very serious. "He said you'd be late."

"Did he."

"He said not to ask why. He said you'd say traffic."

Kusakabe looks down at him. The boy looks back. There's nothing in his face but interest.

"Was there?" the boy says. "Traffic?"

"No."

He doesn't know why he says that. It just comes out true, the way a foot comes down where the ground actually is.

The boy grins. It takes up most of his face.

"Okay," he says. "Thank you."

*For what,* Kusakabe thinks.

He doesn't ask. He comes down the steps.

---

The storehouse doors are open. The practice swords are in the rack where they always are, the grips wrapped in white tape, the tape grey now and coming loose at the ends.

Maki wraps them. Wrapped them. Every few weeks, sitting on the floor of the storehouse with a roll of tape and a face like she was punishing them for something. Nobody's rewrapped them since October. Maki is away. Her name was on a sheet on the dispatch board under a heading that said not to discuss it, and nobody does, and he doesn't ask.

He takes one. He doesn't take one for the boy.

In the middle of the yard he drags his heel through the dirt, a short curved line, about the distance of one arm and one sword from where he's standing. Then he stands behind it.

"Come in," he says. "Touch my shoulder. Don't get hit."

"With what? Like, a hand?"

"Anything."

The boy looks at the line. He looks at the practice sword, hanging loose by Kusakabe's knee. Something goes over his face that Kusakabe has seen on a lot of first-years. It's the look of someone who has been taught a great many things very quickly by a great many people, and is trying to remember which one goes here.

Then he comes.

He's fast. Kusakabe knew he'd be fast. Everybody has a story. A shot put thrown halfway out of a stadium. Fifty metres in three seconds. A curse put through a wall with a fist. Knowing it is one thing. Standing in front of it is another. The boy covers the ground between them the way a dropped thing covers the ground between a table and the floor.

Kusakabe taps him on the forearm with the practice sword.

Not hard. Just enough to be there.

The boy stops, a step inside the line, looking at his arm.

"One more," the boy says, before Kusakabe can say anything.

He comes again. Tap, on the ribs. And again. Tap, on the outside of the knee. Then faster, with a feint off the left foot that would have put most Grade 2s on the floor. Tap, on the wrist, as the hand comes up.

"Thank you," the boy says, every time. Getting his breath. Going back to the start. "Thank you. Okay. Thank you."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything. He watches.

It isn't speed the boy's short of. He's got more of that than anyone Kusakabe has ever stood in front of who wasn't trying to kill him. It's the half-second before. Just before each step, the boy's eyes drop. Not far. A flick, down and up again. Checking. Like someone following a recipe they don't trust.

He's thinking about his feet. Every time. Somebody told him where to put them, and he's being good, and putting them there.

By the twentieth go the sun is behind the storehouse roof and the boy has a red mark on most of his visible skin. He's still grinning. His shirt is stuck to his back.

"That's today," Kusakabe says.

"It's not dark yet."

"It's dark enough."

"Can I do it a few more times? On my own?"

"There's no one to hit you."

"I'll imagine it." The boy bows again. All the way down. "Thank you. Really."

Kusakabe goes back up the steps.

There's someone at the far end of the yard, under the cherry trees. Big. Black and white. Sitting with his back to a trunk in the last of the light, the way you'd sit if you'd been there a while and hadn't wanted anyone to notice you arrive.

Panda doesn't wave. Kusakabe doesn't either.

He goes home the long way, out of habit, and then wonders who exactly he's avoiding now. The boy is behind him in the yard. Panda is under a tree. There's nobody left on the short way to miss.

---

Tuesday, the boy is there before him again.

Kusakabe's eight minutes late. He doesn't say why. The boy doesn't ask. He just says "Afternoon! Thank you," and goes to stand at the start, already up on the balls of his feet, the way a boy waits at a starting line on sports day.

Panda is by the storehouse today. In the shade, against the wall. Not near the steps.

They do it for an hour. Tap. Thank you. Tap. Thank you. The flick of the eyes, every time. Down and up.

Kusakabe stops him.

He's never been good at this part. Saying the thing. He's always thought that if you say more than one sentence you're mostly just hearing yourself talk, and the student is mostly just waiting for you to stop. So he stands there on his side of the line for a while, with the boy waiting, and finds the one that's true.

"Stop thinking about where your feet go," he says. "Feel the edge. Let the body answer."

The boy blinks.

"The edge of what?"

"Where I can reach." Kusakabe touches the line in the dirt with the tip of the practice sword. "You know where it is. Your body knows. It knew the first time. You keep checking."

"So don't check."

"Don't check."

The boy nods slowly. Not the eager nod. A different one. Kusakabe has seen it once before, on a girl from Kyoto at three in the morning in a ruin.

The boy goes back to the start. He stands there a moment with his hands loose. He doesn't look at his feet. He looks at Kusakabe's chest.

Then he comes in.

And stops.

Exactly on the line. Not over it. His front foot is a finger's width from the curve in the dirt, his weight already back on the other one. And the practice sword, which was going to be there, isn't anywhere. There's nothing to tap.

They stand like that for a second.

Then the boy slips left, inside the line, and the sword taps him on the shoulder. But it's late. Only just, but late.

The boy looks at his own feet now. Afterwards. As if they belong to someone he's only just been introduced to.

"Oh," he says.

"Go on," Kusakabe says.

It isn't every time after that. It's one in four, and then one in three. But it's there now. The flick has gone. When the boy's feet are wrong now they're wrong without being asked, which is a better kind of wrong, the kind that gets fixed.

"Thank you," the boy says, when the light goes. "That was. Thank you. Seriously."

Kusakabe nods and goes up the steps.

*One sentence,* he thinks. *Took me two days to find it. Pay's the same either way.*

---

Wednesday, more of the same. The line. The tap. The thank you. One in three, then two in five.

At the water break the boy stands with the bottle halfway to his mouth and doesn't drink from it.

"Were you in Shibuya?" he says.

Kusakabe looks at the yard.

"Yeah."

"Where?"

"North. Meiji-dori."

The boy is quiet. Kusakabe can see him working out the map. Where Meiji-dori is. What happened on it, and when, and in whose body. The grin goes out of his face completely, for the first time all week, and what's under it is older than fifteen by a long way.

"I'm sorry," the boy says.

It's the wrong way round. Kusakabe knows it's the wrong way round. He sits with it for a moment, the wrongness of it, like a coat buttoned one hole off.

He thinks of a burnt collar, thirty metres away, and four red eyes going through him as if he were a window. Not one of them brown.

"Wasn't you," he says. "I saw."

The boy looks at him for a long second. He doesn't say thank you. It's the only time all week he doesn't. He just nods, once, and drinks his water, and goes back to the start.

---

That evening he rings Ono on the walk home. He's been doing that more. He doesn't think about why.

"Fine," Ono says. No hello. There never is. "Ate. Physio tomorrow. She asked if you'd got a cold."

"I haven't."

"I told her you sound like it." A pause in which he can hear her writing something down. "Sunday?"

"Sunday."

She hangs up first.

---

Thursday, Okabe finds him in the corridor with a form.

Okabe's in a grey cardigan again. Kusakabe takes it as a sign of something. He isn't sure what. That the world's still there, possibly. That it's gone back to having forms.

"Instruction hours," Okabe says. "For the record. Anyone assigned to a student this month logs them. There's a box for each day."

Kusakabe takes the form. There are boxes for each day. Each box has a space for *From* and a space for *To*.

He writes a *2* in the first one. In the second he writes *Dark.*

Okabe reads it upside down.

"Dark isn't a time," he says.

"It is in December. Half four."

Okabe looks at him for a moment longer than he needs to. Then he takes the form back and puts it in the clear plastic sleeve on top of his pile, where the forms that have been done go.

"Half four," Okabe says. He writes it in, small, beside the word *Dark*, in pencil, in case it changes.

---

Thursday, in the yard, he moves on.

"Reading," he says. "I'm going to come at you. Slowly. Tell me where before I get there."

"Out loud?"

"Out loud."

He comes at the boy at walking pace with the practice sword low. The boy watches the sword. Of course he watches the sword. It's the thing that hurts.

"Left," the boy says. It goes right. Tap.

"Thank you. Okay. One more."

"Not the sword," Kusakabe says. "The sword's the last thing to know where it's going. Watch the middle. Here." He touches his own breastbone with two fingers. "And the shoulders. Everything starts there and goes outwards. By the time it's at the end of the blade, it's old news."

The boy looks at the middle of him. It's an odd feeling, being looked at there. Like somebody reading a letter over your shoulder.

"Left," the boy says. It goes left. "Low," he says, and it's low. "Right. No. Oh, that's mean."

"Yeah."

"Thank you."

At half three they stop for water. The boy has a convenience-store bag by the steps. He sits on the bottom step, the one that used to be Inumaki's, and eats three rice balls one after another, and then a sandwich. Then he looks into the bag with an air of deep disappointment, as if it has let him down personally.

*Earnest,* Kusakabe thinks. *Eats a lot. Thanks people.*

He knows where he heard that. A break room on the fourteenth floor, in a building nobody worked in any more, at about one in the morning. A man in a pale suit setting a can of coffee back on its ring, saying he'd been looking after one of Gojo's first-years. An earnest one. Kusakabe had known which boy, and changed the subject. To a coin in a machine.

There's nowhere to change it to now. The subject is sitting on the bottom step with rice on his chin.

He doesn't say anything about it. He drinks his water.

Panda is lying on the grass at the edge of the yard, on his back, near the cherry trees. He calls out, "Yuji. Eat slower. You'll be sick." He doesn't look at the steps.

"I'm never sick!"

"You said that last time."

"Last time was different!"

Kusakabe watches the two of them shout at each other across the yard, easy, the way people shout who've stood in the same bad places. He doesn't join in. He wouldn't know how to. And it isn't his to join.

Panda's voice stops when it gets to the steps. It's been doing that for weeks. It goes round him the way a conversation in a staff room goes round a desk.

He knows why. He was there when he earned it.

---

Friday it's cold, the dry kind, the sky very high and pale.

"Eyes shut," he says.

"Shut?"

"Shut."

The boy shuts them. He stands in the middle of the yard with his hands at his sides, and his eyes shut, and his face tipped up a little, as if he's expecting rain.

Kusakabe has a paper bag of dried soybeans from the supermarket. It cost a hundred and eight yen. It's the cheapest thing he's ever bought for work.

He can't teach the boy the circle. There are rules about the circle. Old ones. Who gets taught it, and by whom, and what they promise first. Made by men in a dojo he's never been invited to, a long time before he was born. He's spent his whole working life inside those rules without once being asked whether he minded them. He can't hand it over.

But he can show what it's for. He can show that there's an edge. That you can know where it is without looking.

He flicks a bean.

It hits the boy in the middle of the forehead.

"Ow. Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Move."

"Move where?"

"Away from the bean."

"I can't see the bean."

"That's the point."

He flicks another. Shoulder. Another. Ear. The boy flinches after each one, a half-second late, and opens his mouth. Kusakabe can see the *thank you* coming up the way a sneeze comes up. Then the boy remembers, and shuts his mouth again, and nods instead.

It takes most of the afternoon.

Then, somewhere after three, a bean goes past his ear. It doesn't hit him. He's moved. Not far. A hand's width. His eyes are still shut and his face has gone very still, the way faces go when the person inside them is listening to something a long way off.

Another bean. Another hand's width. It goes past.

The boy's mouth opens.

"Don't," Kusakabe says.

The boy shuts it again. He's smiling with his eyes closed. That's the most annoying thing Kusakabe has seen all week, and he's seen Okabe write *half four* in pencil.

*He's got it. Not the circle. He'll never have that from me. But the edge. He knows there's an edge.*

The bag runs out at quarter to four. Beans everywhere. On the packed earth, in the grass, one balanced on the boy's collar. The crows will be pleased.

---

They stop as the light goes. The boy picks up beans for a while, one at a time, because he's the kind of person who picks things up. Then he straightens and looks over at the steps.

"You don't have to stay till dark, you know," he says.

Kusakabe looks at him.

"It's fine. If you've got stuff. I know some people aren't..." The boy stops. He rubs the back of his neck. He doesn't look upset. He looks like someone reading out a fact off a sign. "Some people don't really want to be near me. After. I get it. It's fair, honestly. I'd probably be the same."

He says it the way you'd tell someone the shop on the corner closes early on Wednesdays.

Kusakabe is sitting on the third step. He doesn't remember sitting down. He hasn't sat there in weeks. He sits very still.

He wonders, not for the first time this week, whether the boy knows. Whether somebody's told him what was said in which room, and who said what about him in it. Whether that's what this is. Or whether he doesn't know, and this is just what he's like. A boy who's been told for six months that he ought to be dead, by enough people, often enough, that he's started agreeing with them politely.

He can't decide which is worse.

"Two till dark," Kusakabe says. "It says."

The boy looks at him for a second. Then the grin comes back. Not all of it. Most of it.

"Okay," he says. "Thank you."

*Stop thanking me,* Kusakabe thinks. *I wanted you dead six weeks ago. I said so. In a room. Out loud. I'd have signed it myself.*

He doesn't say it.

It sits in his mouth for a while, all of it, whole and true. He could say it. It would be the truest thing anyone's said in this yard all week. And then it would be the boy's to carry, on top of everything else, for the sake of letting Kusakabe put it down.

He keeps it.

"Tomorrow," he says. "Two."

"You'll be late."

"Probably."

"Okay." The boy bows. "Thank you."

Kusakabe gets up, and his knees tell him exactly how many afternoons this week he's spent standing on packed earth with his weight back, and he goes.

At the top of the steps he looks back once. Panda is by the storehouse, sitting against the wall. Panda is looking at him. Not at the boy. At him.

Then Panda looks away, and Kusakabe goes home.

---

At home he lies on the sofa for an hour with his arm across his eyes.

An hour. That's all it gets now. Then he gets up and eats something standing at the counter and goes to bed.

The calendar's on the kitchen wall where he can see it from the counter. He doesn't look at it. He knows what's on it.

His right arm aches from the wrist to the shoulder. Hundreds of small taps. Each one the same weight, in the same place, at the end of the same arm. Like writing your name over and over until the pen wears a groove in your finger.

He realises, lying in the dark, that he's tired in a way he isn't usually. Not the flat grey tired, the sofa tired, the kind that comes from nothing. This is the other kind. The kind from doing something.

He'd forgotten there were two.

---

Saturday he's five minutes late. The boy says, "Better!" and Kusakabe says, "Don't."

They do all of it. The line. The reading. The beans, a new bag, a hundred and eight yen. The boy stops on the edge three times in four now. He calls *left* and *low* and *right* and is mostly right. A bean goes past his ear with his eyes shut and he doesn't say anything, and doesn't smile either this time. He just goes on listening.

None of it's a technique. None of it's a new thing he can do. It's a boy who was already very fast, now wasting a bit less of it. Half a second here. A flick of the eyes there. It isn't much.

*It's what I've got,* Kusakabe thinks. *It's all it ever was.*

At four the light starts going. At twenty past, he says, "That's it."

"Can I stay?"

"There's no one to hit you."

"I know. I'll do the line." The boy's already walking back to the start. "On my own. Thank you. Really. For all week."

Kusakabe doesn't answer that. He goes up the steps and sits down on the third one from the bottom. Gate in front. Wall behind. The step is cold right through his coat.

The boy stands at the start with his hands loose. Then he comes in. Up to the line. Stops. Back. Up to the line. Stops. Back. No sword in front of him now. Nobody. Just a curve in the dirt that's mostly been scuffed away, and his body finding the edge of it anyway, on its own, without being asked.

The circle tells him first. It's been out all afternoon without his asking, thin as breath. Something big comes into it from the side, from the storehouse, unhurried, the grass hushing under it. He doesn't turn his head.

The stone takes the weight without a sound. Panda doesn't go past him this time, down to the bottom one. He sits on the third step. Next to him. Not near. Next to.

The step is wide enough. It always was.

Neither of them says anything.

Kusakabe doesn't say *sorry*. He doesn't say *I was wrong*, or *you were right*, or *I've been teaching him all week, did you see*. Panda saw. That's why he's here. There's nothing to add to it that wouldn't make it smaller.

Panda doesn't say anything either. He just sits, paws flat on his knees, like someone in a waiting room who has decided not to read the magazines, and watches the boy.

The light goes from grey to a darker grey. The cherry trees lose their edges. Somewhere beyond the wall a bicycle bell goes twice, for nobody.

In the middle of the yard the boy keeps going. His breath comes out white. Up to the line. Back. Up to the line. Back.

He doesn't look down once.
