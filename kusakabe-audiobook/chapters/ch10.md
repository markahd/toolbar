# Chapter 10. Two Tired Men

Nanami Kento is already there.

He's standing in the lobby of an empty office building in Shinagawa, at four minutes past nine on a Thursday night in October, under a ceiling light that hums. Pale suit. A tie with a small, busy pattern on it. Glasses that are closer to goggles, tinted, the frames patterned too, worn the way other men wear a wristwatch. Under one arm, something long and flat wrapped in cloth.

He's looking at his actual wristwatch when Kusakabe comes through the revolving door.

"Four minutes," Nanami says.

"Yeah."

"I'm not complaining. I'm noting it."

*Fair. It's a long list.*

---

The sheet came that morning, across the dispatch coordinator's desk, between a rice cracker and a mug.

*Kusakabe A. (Grade 1). Nanami K. (Grade 1). Shinagawa. Commercial premises, vacant. Manifests after midnight. Possible Grade 1.*

"Two of us," he said.

"Twenty-two floors," she said. "The file doesn't say which one. Car at half eight."

"I'll get the train."

She shrugged, and wrote something next to his name, and that was the end of it.

He knows Nanami the way he knows most of the people at the school. From corridors. From the staff room, at the far end of the table, reading a newspaper folded into quarters. They nod. They've never worked a job together. Kusakabe has heard things, the way you hear things. That Nanami left jujutsu after he graduated and went to work at a company, an ordinary one, with a desk and a pension. That he came back. That nobody knows why, and nobody asks, because Nanami has a face that makes asking feel like a bad investment.

That he goes home at six. Every day. Whatever's happening.

*So that's two of us who'd rather be somewhere else,* Kusakabe thought on the train. *At least nobody has to pretend.*

---

The building belonged to a freight company until August. Then the company moved to Saitama, where the rent is cheaper and the air is worse, and left twenty-two floors of grey carpet behind it, waiting to be sold.

The night guard is called Iwata. Sixties, a uniform jacket a size too big, reading glasses on a cord. He's been here eleven years. He stayed on with the building, the way some people stay on with a house after the family's gone.

He gives them the briefing in the lobby, standing, because there are no chairs left.

"The lifts first," he says. "About a month ago. They go up on their own after midnight. Fourteen, every time. Then the phones on fourteen. All of them at once. There's no line in. The company cut it in August."

"Fourteen," Nanami says.

"Accounts." Iwata touches the cord of his glasses. "They were famous for it, upstairs. Lights on all night. You'd do your rounds at three and there'd be twenty of them up there, eating out of the vending machine."

"And your colleague," Nanami says.

Iwata nods slowly.

"Fukuda. Young lad. I found him on fourteen at half three in the morning, a fortnight ago. Sitting at a desk in the dark, typing." He stops. "The computer wasn't plugged in. There wasn't a computer. Just the keyboard. He'd been at it a while. His fingers."

He doesn't finish that.

"He's alive," Iwata says. "He doesn't remember any of it. His mother rang me to say thank you, and I didn't know what to say to her."

Kusakabe doesn't say anything either. Neither does Nanami. It's a good silence, as these things go. It doesn't try to fix anything.

"I don't go above ten after midnight now," Iwata says. "I'll be honest with you."

"That's sensible," Nanami says. "Stay down here. Keep the doors locked. If you hear anything, don't come up."

"What will you be doing?"

Nanami looks at his watch again.

"Waiting," he says.

---

They take the stairs. Fourteen floors. Nanami doesn't trust the lifts, and Kusakabe doesn't trust anything, so it's unanimous.

Fourteen is a long open floor of desks with nothing on them. Grey partitions at shoulder height. Grey carpet with paler squares where filing cabinets used to stand. A whole wall of windows on the far side, and through them the city doing what it does at night, all those lit rectangles where other people are still at work.

On every desk, a phone. Beige. Cord coiled. Unplugged.

The air on fourteen is wrong in the way a room is wrong after an argument. Nothing there. Everything there. Kusakabe lets the circle out a little, just to the length of his arm, and the wrongness sits right at the edge of it, everywhere at once, not coming in.

"Well," Nanami says. "It's here."

"It's everywhere."

"Yes. That's what makes it tiresome."

At the end of the floor, by the lifts, there's a break room. A sink, a round table, four chairs that didn't go to Saitama, and a vending machine still plugged into the wall, lit up like a shop window, the only bright thing on the floor.

Nanami looks at it for a while.

"Someone forgot to turn it off," he says.

"Or it's the only one still working."

Nanami glances at him. It isn't quite a smile. It's the place where a smile would go if he were a different man.

They sit down at the round table to wait.

---

It's half past nine. The file says after midnight. Nobody knows how long after.

Kusakabe has done a lot of waiting in his life. He's good at it. You find a wall to put your back against and a door to face, and you let the body go quiet, and you don't think about anything you'd have to get up to deal with.

Nanami waits differently. He sits very straight. He takes the cloth-wrapped thing from under his arm and lays it on the table in front of him, parallel to the edge. He takes off his jacket, folds it once along the seam, and hangs it on the back of the chair. He looks at his watch.

"It's past my hours," he says.

"Since six."

"Since six." Nanami turns his wrist so the watch face sits on the inside. "I want to be clear that I find this objectionable."

"Noted."

"Not this job. The principle." He considers the dark floor beyond the doorway. "Overtime is the moment a company tells you what it actually thinks of your life. That it's a thing it can have more of, if it asks politely. Or doesn't ask."

"The company's a curse, tonight."

"Then it's the most honest employer I've had."

Kusakabe snorts. It surprises him. Nanami doesn't react, which is somehow the right reaction.

---

Ten o'clock. Nanami gets up and goes to the vending machine.

He stands in front of it with his hands in his pockets, reading it the way some men read a menu in a restaurant they don't trust. Then he feeds it coins. Two cans drop.

He puts one in front of Kusakabe without asking.

It's coffee, black, in a brown can with a picture of a mountain on it. Kusakabe opens it. It's hot, which is the best that can be said about it. It tastes of burnt sugar and a little of the inside of a pipe.

Nanami drinks his and looks at the can.

"That's dreadful," he says.

"Yeah."

"I worked in a building like this for four years. Different company. Same machine, I think. I think they make one machine and move it around." He sets the can down, exactly on the ring it came from. "I used to drink three of these a night and tell myself it was a choice."

Kusakabe waits. He doesn't ask. It seems to be the right thing, because Nanami goes on.

"Labour is shit," he says.

He says it calmly. The way you'd give a measurement.

"It takes your time and gives you money, and then you spend the money on things that help you recover from losing the time. Food you don't have the energy to cook. Rest that isn't rest. A bigger flat to be tired in." He turns the can a quarter turn. "It's a closed loop. Very elegant, if you're not inside it."

*He's right,* Kusakabe thinks. *I'd say it if I could be bothered to say things.*

"So you came back to this," he says. "Where it's worse."

"It isn't worse. It's the same shit." Nanami thinks about it. "But at the end of the day, sometimes, something is different because I was there. At the company, the only thing that was different at the end of the day was the date."

He doesn't say any more than that. Kusakabe has a feeling there's a story under it, small and specific, the kind of thing a person doesn't tell twice. He doesn't go looking for it.

"Someone thanked me once," Nanami says, as if he's read that. "For something very small. I hadn't budgeted for how much it would matter."

Then he picks up the can again, and the subject is closed, the way a drawer closes when you lean on it.

---

Eleven. Out on the floor, nothing. The lit city in the windows. A helicopter, somewhere, going over without stopping.

Nanami takes a paper bag out of his briefcase. It's folded over twice at the top and creased flat with a thumbnail. He opens it on the table. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, a sandwich cut in half: thick white bread, something breaded and dark in the middle, a smear of mustard.

He looks at the two halves. Then he moves one of them a few centimetres across the table, towards Kusakabe.

"I can't eat both," he says. "And it won't keep."

"I'm fine."

"You haven't eaten. You came straight from somewhere."

That's true. Kusakabe can't think how Nanami knows it. Possibly it shows. Most things do, on him, if anyone bothers to look.

He takes the half.

It's good. It's very good. The bread is soft and dense at the same time, and the crust gives under his teeth with a small, definite crackle. He eats it slowly, which isn't like him, and when he's finished he finds he's looking at the waxed paper as if there might be more of it.

"Where's that from."

Nanami tells him. A bakery, a small one, near a station, open at seven. The woman who runs it starts at four. He's been going there for two years. He describes the bakery the way he hasn't described anything else all night: carefully, with pleasure, with the hours and the name of the street.

"The melon bread is overrated," he says. "Everyone gets the melon bread. Get this."

"Okay."

*I'll go one Sunday,* Kusakabe thinks. *On the way back. I'll get round to it.*

He folds the waxed paper into a square and puts it in his pocket, because there's nowhere to throw it, and then forgets it's there.

---

At a quarter to twelve, Nanami says, "I've been looking after a student."

Kusakabe looks up.

"One of Gojo-san's first-years," Nanami says. "He lends them out. Like an umbrella you don't expect back." He turns the can. "This one's earnest. Painfully. He eats a lot. He thanks people for things. He asks questions I stopped asking some time ago, and I find I don't have good answers for them."

He says it without much expression. But something sits differently in his shoulders when he says it. Like a man describing a dog he didn't want and has started walking twice a day.

Kusakabe knows which first-year. Everyone at the school knows which first-year. There are words people use about that boy in the staff room, quietly, when Gojo isn't in it, and none of them are *earnest*.

He doesn't want those words at this table.

"Your machine's still got change in it," he says. "In the return. Someone left a hundred yen."

Nanami looks at him for a moment. Not long. Long enough.

Then he says, "Then it's yours. You found it." And lets it go, the whole thing, without a mark on it.

Kusakabe fishes the coin out of the return slot. It's cold. He puts it in his pocket, the other one, the one without the waxed paper. His hand doesn't check anything. It's just a coin.

---

Just before midnight, he walks the floor. It's something to do, and Nanami is rolling back his shirt cuffs, exactly two turns each.

He goes slowly down the rows between the partitions with the sword bag on his shoulder and the circle held close. The company took the computers and the chairs and the filing cabinets. It didn't take everything.

A wall calendar, still on August, with a photograph of a lighthouse. A mug in the corner of a desk with a name written on it in marker, half washed off. A sticky note on a partition at eye level: *Back in 5 min.* The ink has faded. Whoever wrote it never came back for it.

On one desk, near the windows, the beige plastic of the phone has a dip worn into it where a hand rested on the receiver. Day after day. Year after year. Waiting for it to ring, or hoping it wouldn't.

The wrongness is thicker here. It isn't angry. That's the strange part. It's tired. It's the most tired thing he's ever felt through the circle, and he's felt it from the inside, on bad mornings.

*Fourteen floors up. Lights on all night. Eating out of a machine.*

*It didn't come from nowhere. It came from them.*

He stands at the window for a moment. Below, a taxi waits at a red light on an empty junction, patient, with nobody to see it be patient.

He goes back to the break room. Nanami looks up.

"Anything?"

"It's tired."

Nanami nods slowly, as if that were a technical finding, and writes nothing down.

---

Midnight comes and goes. Nothing.

Half past twelve. Nanami checks his watch and doesn't comment on it, which is a comment.

One o'clock. The lifts don't move.

It's the hour when a room goes thick. When the hum of the vending machine starts to sound like it's coming from inside your own head. Kusakabe sits with his back to the wall and the doorway in front of him and the circle held in, folded, ready.

"What would you do," Nanami says, "if you could stop?"

He says it to the window. Not to Kusakabe. It could be a question for anybody.

Kusakabe doesn't answer.

Nanami seems to expect that. After a while he answers it himself.

"Malaysia," he says. "Kuantan. On the east coast. There's a beach there that goes on for longer than seems necessary." He looks at the dark glass. The reflection of the break room floats over the city, the two of them at a round table under a strip light. "A house near the sea. Not a big one. I'd sell everything that needs a key. I'd take the books I've bought over the years and never read. There are a great many of them. I keep buying them. It's a kind of optimism, I suppose."

"How many."

"I stopped counting at a hundred and forty," Nanami says.

"That's a lot of optimism."

"It's a lot of evenings I thought I'd have." Nanami takes off his glasses and cleans them on a handkerchief, slowly, one lens and then the other. Without them his eyes are tired and quite ordinary. "I'd read them in order. Badly. In a chair. With the window open. And when I got to the end, I'd start buying new ones, and never read those either."

He puts the glasses back on.

"That's all," he says. "It's not much of a plan. I don't need it to be."

Kusakabe looks at the window too. He sees it for a second, more clearly than he'd like: a chair, a window, a long flat beach, the sea going in and out without needing anyone to watch it.

"You?" Nanami says.

"Nothing."

"Nothing."

"Nothing. Somewhere quiet. Nobody asking."

"That's not nothing," Nanami says. "That's a very precise description of a good life."

Kusakabe doesn't answer that, either.

He drinks the last of the coffee instead. It's gone cold. It's worse cold, which he wouldn't have thought possible. He finishes it anyway, out of something like loyalty, and puts the can down next to Nanami's, not quite on its ring.

Nanami looks at the two cans. He doesn't move them. Kusakabe gets the feeling it costs him something.

---

At eleven minutes past two, every phone on fourteen rings at once.

It isn't a ring, exactly. It's the memory of one, coming from forty dead handsets, all slightly out of time. Under it, the sound of keys. Hundreds of keys. Someone typing very fast, everywhere, on nothing.

Kusakabe is on his feet. He doesn't remember standing. The sword is in his hand and the bag is on the floor. The circle is out, and the cold has gone into his fingers the way it always does, quick and specific, like a tap turned on.

Nanami stands up beside him. He loosens his tie. He pulls it free of his collar, and winds it round his right hand, twice, and pulls it tight with his teeth. Then he unwraps the cloth from the thing on the table. A blade, broad, squared off, more like a cleaver than a sword, with a dull edge.

"From here," Nanami says, "this is overtime."

Out on the floor, between the partitions, something stands up.

It's the shape of a back at the end of a long day. Very large. Grey, stooped, with a hundred thin arms hanging off it like coat sleeves off a rail, and the ends of the arms are fingers, and the fingers are moving. It turns towards the light of the break room. It doesn't have a face. It has the place a face would go if anyone had ever looked up.

It comes for Kusakabe first. He's nearest. Things always are.

The arms cross the edge of the circle and the sword answers. He doesn't decide the cuts. He hears them: four, five, short and dry, and fingers falling on the carpet like dropped pens. The thing jerks back and comes again, wider, trying to go round him. It can't. Wherever it reaches, he's already there, a step left, a step back, the circle moving with him, and every arm that comes in comes off.

His heart is going like something trying to get out of a box.

*Keep it busy. Just keep it busy.*

Behind him, Nanami says nothing at all. Kusakabe can feel him in the circle, standing quite still, looking at the thing the way a man looks at a plank before he cuts it. Measuring. Seven parts here. Three parts there.

Then Nanami steps past him.

One stroke. Not at the thing. At a place on the thing, low on the left side of that great tired back, where there's nothing to see at all. The blunt blade goes in like it was expected.

A sound like a load-bearing wall deciding it's had enough.

The curse folds in half, the wrong way, and comes apart in grey flakes that drift down over the empty desks like ash from a paper fire. The phones stop. The keys stop. One by one. The last phone, by the window, rings once more on its own, a long way after all the others, and then it stops too.

The floor goes still.

Kusakabe lets the circle go. It takes him a moment. It always does.

---

They don't talk for a while after.

Nanami sits back down at the round table. He doesn't unwind the tie from his hand. He rests that hand on the table, palm down, and looks at it. Kusakabe sits opposite. He puts the sword away and then puts both hands flat on his knees, and waits for them to be his again.

"Thank you," Nanami says. "For holding it."

"It came to me."

"You let it."

Kusakabe shrugs. That's what the circle's for. You stand still and let things come to you, and then they stop coming.

"It was a clean job," Nanami says. "I don't say that often. Most people make a lot of noise."

"You didn't."

"No." Nanami looks at the flakes still settling on the desks outside. "Neither did you."

That's all. It's enough. It sits on the table between them with the two empty cans.

Kusakabe rings Iwata on the lobby line, and then rings dispatch. The car for him won't be here till four. Nanami will take the first train. There's an hour and a half of nothing in front of them, and nowhere to put it.

So they sit.

---

It's three in the morning. The worst hour. The city in the window has thinned out to a few hundred lights. Somewhere down there people are getting up for early shifts, and somewhere else people are still at work, and in this one room on the fourteenth floor, two men are doing neither.

Kusakabe doesn't know why he says it. Possibly the hour. Possibly the sandwich. Possibly that Nanami asked him a question three hours ago and then didn't make him answer it, and some debts get paid whether you mean to or not.

"I've got a sister," he says.

Nanami doesn't move.

"I see her on Sundays."

That's the one thing. He's said it now. It's out on the table with the cans and the cloth and it can't be put back. Nobody at the school knows that. Not Ijichi. Not Gojo. Not Okabe, who knows everything that can be written on a form.

Nanami is quiet for a moment. He doesn't ask where she lives, or what's wrong, or why Sundays. He doesn't make the face people make. He looks at the window, and nods, once, as if Kusakabe had told him the time.

"Then don't work Sundays," he says.

"I don't."

"Good. Keep it that way. Write it in somewhere. Make them ask you." He turns his wrist and looks at the watch. "Everything else they'll take."

*He's right,* Kusakabe thinks. *Again. That's twice in one night. I'm not sure I like it.*

He thinks, briefly, that this is what people mean by a colleague. Not a friend. Something a bit less than that and a bit more useful. Someone who sits at the same table at three in the morning and doesn't need you to be interesting.

He thinks they might do this again. Another job, another long night in a dead building, another can of something dreadful. He finds he wouldn't mind. He files that away somewhere he won't have to look at it.

---

At ten to four they go down the stairs. Fourteen floors. Nanami carries his jacket over his arm and the cloth-wrapped blade under it. Neither of them says anything about the lifts.

In the lobby, Iwata is standing by the reception desk with his glasses on, as if he's been standing there the whole time. He probably has.

"It's done," Nanami tells him. "It won't come back."

Iwata looks at the ceiling. He looks at it for a long time.

"I'll tell Fukuda's mother," he says.

Then he bows, deeply, to both of them, in a way that's clearly been waiting a fortnight to happen. Kusakabe bows back, less deeply. He's never sure what to do with his hands at a moment like this. Nanami bows exactly as deep as Iwata, no more and no less, as if he'd measured that too.

---

Outside, the air has gone cold and clean. It's still dark. The street is wet from a rain neither of them noticed falling. Down at the end of it, a delivery van is unloading crates of milk outside a convenience store, the bottles clinking.

Kusakabe's car isn't here yet. He stands on the pavement with his sword bag on his shoulder and his hands in his coat pockets. One pocket has a folded square of waxed paper in it. The other has a cold hundred-yen coin.

Nanami puts his jacket on. He unwinds the tie from his hand, slowly, and smooths it once between his fingers. Then he turns his collar up, and loops the tie round his neck, and knots it without looking, the way a man does who has done it every morning for most of his life.

He checks his watch.

"The first train's in nineteen minutes," he says. "If I walk, I'll be on time."

"Course you will."

"One of us should be." Nanami looks at him through the tinted glasses. Something in his face eases, very slightly, and then puts itself back. "Get some sleep before Sunday, Kusakabe."

"Yeah."

Nanami adjusts his tie. A small movement. A finger and thumb at the knot, setting it straight.

"See you," he says.

He turns and walks off down the wet street, towards the station, pale suit going grey in the dark, not hurrying, exactly on time.

Kusakabe is watching the milk van. He raises a hand without looking.
