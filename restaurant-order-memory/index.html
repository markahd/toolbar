The method

Your memory is fine. Your procedure is missing.

Working memory holds about four things for a few seconds. A four-top with drinks, mains, temps, sides and mods is fifteen to twenty-five things. Nobody holds that raw. The servers who look like they do are compressing it into a structure they built through repetition, and the structure does the remembering.

If you forget item one by item three, item one was never stored. You heard it while getting ready for item two. Hearing is not encoding. Every step below exists to force encoding to happen at the moment the words are said, and to give the POS a fixed order to unload into.

The seven rules

  1. 1Anchor

    Before the first word, seat 1 is fixed by a rule you never change: the seat on your left as you stand at your usual spot, then clockwise. Stand in the same place at every table. Position is the key the ticket runs on. Then tag each seat with one thing about the person (glasses, red shirt, the loud one). Waiters who ran on position alone got wrecked when customers swapped seats between ordering and delivery. The one who tagged faces did not.

  2. 2Slot

    Every seat has the same slots in the same order: drink, snack, main, temp, side, dessert, mods. The customer says it in whatever order they like. You file it in slot order. An empty slot is “none” and you remember the “none” too.

  3. 3Say

    Repeat every item back, out loud, immediately, compressed, in slot order. This is the encoding step. It is also the customer's one cheap chance to correct you. Skipping it because it feels slow is how you end up back at the table asking again.

    They say: “Can I get the wagyu burger with the salad instead of chips, no bacon, and a flat white on oat.” You say: “Flat white, oat. Wagyu, no bacon, salad.”

  4. 4Overwrite

    When they change their mind, say the change out loud as a replacement: “Scratch the fries, salad.” Then re-say the whole seat. Never hold two versions. The old one was rehearsed longer and it will win at the POS if you let it live.

  5. 5Sweep

    After the last seat, before you step away, run seat 1 to seat N in your head or under your breath, slot order. Any seat that comes up blank, you ask now, at the table, with a smile. That is free. The same question from the POS is a walk back and a table that now doubts you.

  6. 6Dump

    Straight to the POS. Do not stop. Enter it clockwise, seat numbers on, in slot order. Every “quick question” between the table and the terminal costs you a seat. If you are stopped, run the sweep again before you answer anyone.

  7. 7Cap

    Above your cap you write. Tonight the cap is whatever level you cleared in the drill three times running, and it is probably three or four seats. A pad is not a failure. A wrong plate is.

If you are the person who forgets item one by item three

Run the failsafe version. Say-back is not optional. Sweep after every two seats, not only at the end. Cap at three seats. Write everything past that. You are not trying to impress anyone tonight. You are trying to never re-ask and never mis-ring. That is the whole job, and this version does it with the memory you have, not the one you wish you had.

Bulk tables and who sits where

Seat numbers solve “who ordered what.” The ticket carries the seat, the runner reads the seat, and you never have to remember faces. When you have to hold two tables before you can ring, take table A, sweep A, take table B, sweep B, then ring A then B. Never ring B first because it is fresher; A decays faster the longer you leave it.

Once slot order is automatic, add the trick expert waiters use: for a slot with only a few options, remember the table as a pattern, not as individuals. Milks around a four-top become “oat, full, full, skim.” Chips-or-salad becomes “chips, salad, chips, chips.” Two or three short patterns plus the seat anchor rebuild the whole table. This only works when the slot is fixed and the options are few, which is why the slots come first.

Tonight

Before the shift, 45 minutes.

  1. Pick your anchor rule. Decide where you stand. Write it on the pad if you have to.
  2. Put your real menu into the drill. Mark what gets a temp.
  3. Drill, tap-to-advance, voice on. Start at level 1. Three clean tickets in a row moves you up. Stop after 30 minutes wherever you are. That level is your honest cap for tonight.
  4. Copy the shorthand table onto your pad for anything over the cap.

On the shift. Run the seven rules at every table, including the two-tops you could wing. The two-tops are where the habit gets built for free. Keep a tally on a receipt in your apron: re-asks and wrong items. That number, not a feeling, tells you whether tonight worked.

After. One drill at your level. Write the tally next to the level. Sleep. Short sleep takes back most of what the drill gave you.

The next two weeks

Capacity is built by drilling your own menu, ten to fifteen minutes a day, with changes and interruptions on, and by running the rules on every real table. Expect to move up a level every few days, not every night. Brain-training apps and dual n-back games make you better at the app; the transfer to a dinner rush is close to zero in the research. The waiter who could take twenty orders unaided took years and built his tricks around one menu. The tricks are yours to copy. The speed is not, yet.

What the research says, in one screen

  • Four things, not seven. Adult working memory holds about four chunks when rehearsal and chunking are blocked. Anything bigger is structure borrowed from long-term memory. Cowan, Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2001.
  • Unrehearsed, it is gone in seconds. With rehearsal blocked by a counting task, recall of a three-letter item fell from roughly 80% at 3 seconds to roughly 10% at 18 seconds. That is the walk to the POS. Peterson & Peterson, 1959.
  • The 20-order waiter. JC encoded by category across the table, not by customer: all the dressings as an acronym (BOOT: blue, oil, oil, thousand), all the temps, all the starches, each tied to seat position, recalled clockwise, in units of four. Change the category structure and his performance collapsed. His trick is a structure, not a talent. Ericsson & Polson, JEP: Learning, Memory and Cognition, 1988.
  • Position works until they move. Nine Buenos Aires waiters who take orders unaided: eight served correctly while customers stayed put; when customers swapped seats before delivery, all but one erred. The exception used faces and clothing, not seats. Bekinschtein, Cardozo & Manes, Behavioural Neurology, 2008.
  • Experts are not remembering harder. Forty cocktail waitresses beat forty students on 7, 11 and 15 drink orders and placed drinks about twice as fast. The accurate ones reported vivid interaction with each customer at the moment of ordering. Encoding at the moment, not effort afterward. Bennett, Human Learning, 1983.
  • Saying it out loud works. Reading aloud beats silent reading for later memory (the production effect, between-subjects g ≈ 0.37; acting it out is larger, g ≈ 1.2). Read-back is also standard in medicine because it catches errors at the source: 3.5% of critical lab calls were corrected by read-back alone. MacLeod et al. 2010; Fawcett 2013; Roberts et al. 2022; Barenfanger et al. 2004.
  • Testing yourself beats re-reading. After one week, people who practised recalling kept 61% against 40% for people who re-studied. The drill is retrieval practice with interference on purpose. Roediger & Karpicke, Psychological Science, 2006.
  • Brain-training apps do not transfer. Across 145 comparisons, working-memory training improved the trained task and nothing in real life. A 6-week memory-palace course took ordinary people from about 26 to 62 words recalled; the dual n-back group in the same study gained nothing. Train the thing itself. Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016; Simons et al. 2016; Dresler et al., Neuron, 2017.
  • Speed is years, structure is tonight. The digit-span subject SF went from 7 to 79 digits, but it took 230 hours and his letter span stayed at 6. One session of instruction in an ordered mnemonic, however, already beats no method for recalling items in position. Ericsson, Chase & Faloon, Science, 1980; Roediger 1980.
  • Sleep and drink are not optional variables. Sleep loss hits sustained attention and working memory hardest of all cognitive domains. A hangover cuts short-term memory by g ≈ 0.64 the next day. Lim & Dinges 2010; Gunn et al., Addiction, 2018.
  • Not evidence. The story that waiters remember unpaid orders and forget them once paid (the Zeigarnik effect) does not replicate: 59 studies pooled, recall ratio 0.99. Do not build on it. Ghibellini & Meier, 2025.
