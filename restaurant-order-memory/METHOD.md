# Seat One Clockwise

An order-memory procedure for servers, and a drill that trains it.
`index.html` is the drill. Open it on a phone. It works offline, saves
progress on the phone only, and reads lines aloud with the phone's own voice.

## The reality

Working memory holds about four things for a few seconds. A four-top with
drinks, mains, temps, sides and mods is fifteen to twenty-five things. Nobody
holds that raw. Servers who look like they do compress it into a structure
built by repetition, and the structure does the remembering.

If you forget item one by item three, item one was never stored. You heard it
while getting ready for item two. Hearing is not encoding.

## The seven rules

1. **Anchor.** Seat 1 is the seat on your left as you stand at your usual
   spot, then clockwise. Same rule at every table forever. Position is the
   key the ticket runs on. Tag each seat with one feature of the person
   (glasses, red shirt) so a seat swap between ordering and delivery does
   not wreck you.
2. **Slot.** Every seat has the same slots in the same order: drink,
   snack, main, temp, side, dessert, mods. They say it in any order. You file it in
   slot order. An empty slot is "none", and you remember the none.
3. **Say.** Repeat every item back, out loud, immediately, compressed, in
   slot order. This is the encoding step and the customer's one cheap chance
   to correct you.
   They say: "Can I get the wagyu burger with the salad instead of chips,
   no bacon, and a flat white on oat."
   You say: "Flat white, oat. Wagyu, no bacon, salad."
4. **Overwrite.** On a change, say it as a replacement: "Scratch the fries,
   salad." Re-say the whole seat. Never hold two versions. The old one was
   rehearsed longer and will win at the POS.
5. **Sweep.** After the last seat, before you step away, run seat 1 to seat
   N in slot order. A blank seat gets asked now, at the table. The same
   question from the POS is a walk back and a table that doubts you.
6. **Dump.** Straight to the POS. Do not stop. Enter clockwise, seat numbers
   on, slot order. Every quick question on the way costs a seat. If stopped,
   sweep again before you answer.
7. **Cap.** Above your cap you write. The cap is the drill level you cleared
   three times in a row. Tonight that is probably three or four seats. A pad
   is not a failure. A wrong plate is.

### Failsafe version, for the person who forgets item one by item three

Say-back is mandatory. Sweep after every two seats, not only at the end.
Cap at three seats. Write everything past that. The goal is never re-ask and
never mis-ring. This version does that with the memory you have.

### Bulk

Seat numbers solve "who ordered what". Two tables before you can ring: take
A, sweep A, take B, sweep B, ring A then B. A decays faster the longer you
leave it. Once slots are automatic, encode a few-option slot as a table-wide
pattern (milks: "oat, full, full, skim"; chips-or-salad: "chips, salad,
chips, chips"). That is what the 20-order waiter did.

## Tonight

Before the shift, 45 minutes:

1. Pick the anchor rule and where you stand.
2. Open the menu panel in the drill. It is pre-loaded with the Vic on George
   menu: Uber Eats verbatim, plus coffee and a few site-only dishes (see
   MENU.md). Fix anything the board says differently. Mark what gets a temp
   with `*`.
3. Drill, tap-to-advance, voice on, from level 1. Three clean tickets in a
   row moves you up. Stop after 30 minutes. Where you are is your cap.
4. Copy the shorthand table onto your pad for anything over the cap.

On the shift: run the rules at every table, including the two-tops. Tally
re-asks and wrong items on a receipt in your apron.

After: one drill at your level. Write the tally next to the level. Sleep.

## The next two weeks

Ten to fifteen minutes a day on your own menu with changes and interruptions
on, plus the rules on every real table. A level every few days, not every
night. Brain-training apps do not transfer. The menu does.

## Evidence

- Working memory is about four chunks when rehearsal and chunking are
  blocked. Cowan, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 2001.
- Unrehearsed items drop from about 80% recall at 3 s to about 10% at 18 s.
  Peterson & Peterson, 1959.
- The waiter JC handled 20 orders by encoding category-wise across the table
  (dressings as an acronym like BOOT, temps, starches), tied to seat
  position, recalled clockwise in units of four. Break the category structure
  and he collapsed. Ericsson & Polson, *JEP: LMC*, 1988.
- Nine Buenos Aires waiters: eight correct when customers stayed put; when
  customers swapped seats, all but one erred, and the exception used faces
  and clothing rather than position. Bekinschtein, Cardozo & Manes,
  *Behavioural Neurology*, 2008.
- Forty cocktail waitresses beat forty students on 7, 11 and 15 drink orders
  and placed drinks about twice as fast; the accurate ones reported vivid
  interaction with each customer while ordering. Bennett, *Human Learning*,
  1983.
- Saying it aloud improves memory (production effect g ≈ 0.37
  between-subjects; enactment g ≈ 1.2). Read-back corrected 3.5% of critical
  lab calls. MacLeod et al. 2010; Fawcett 2013; Roberts et al. 2022;
  Barenfanger et al. 2004.
- Retrieval practice: 61% retained after a week versus 40% for re-study.
  Roediger & Karpicke, *Psychological Science*, 2006.
- Working-memory training: no far transfer across 145 comparisons. A 6-week
  memory-palace course took people from about 26 to 62 words; dual n-back in
  the same study gained nothing. Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016; Simons
  et al. 2016; Dresler et al., *Neuron*, 2017.
- SF went from 7 to 79 digits in 230 hours; his letter span stayed at 6. One
  session of an ordered mnemonic already beats none for recall in position.
  Ericsson, Chase & Faloon, *Science*, 1980; Roediger 1980.
- Sleep loss hits sustained attention and working memory hardest. Hangover
  cuts short-term memory by g ≈ 0.64 next day. Lim & Dinges 2010; Gunn et
  al., *Addiction*, 2018.
- The Zeigarnik "waiters forget paid orders" story does not replicate
  (59 studies, recall ratio 0.99). Ghibellini & Meier, 2025.
